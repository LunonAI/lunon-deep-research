# Toward a Unified Evaluation Framework for Advanced Quantitative Strategies: Returns, Risk, and Regime Adaptability

A single composite scoring model can render multi-factor, trend-following, risk-parity, and high-frequency strategies comparable on one normalized scale — but only after explicitly correcting for the selection bias, non-normality, and regime-dependence that break every off-the-shelf metric. The need is now industry-scale: a 2024 Preqin survey found **68% of institutional investors increased quant allocations** over the prior three years and now demand reporting beyond fact sheets, while pro-forma analysis of 1,726 commercial strategies shows live performance deteriorating sharply against external benchmarks.

**The Sharpe ratio does not furnish a universal cross-strategy yardstick.** Hedge-fund indices for the same strategy from different providers correlate as low as **0.67** and diverge by more than **1,400 basis points per month**, and backtested Sharpe predicts out-of-sample results with **R² below 0.025**. Against the AIFMD2, SEC Marketing Rule, and UK Consumer Composite Investments deadlines clustered in 2026–2027, the framework that survives is multi-dimensional, not single-metric.

**Committed verdict:** the Deflated Sharpe Ratio combined with a CRITIC-weighted composite leads as the comparability backbone; regime-conditional Minimum Regime Performance ranks second; raw Sharpe-class ratios place last as standalone arbiters — defensible only inside a normalization and overfitting-correction wrapper.

# 1. The Framework Contract

Quantitative strategy evaluation suffers a concrete structural defect: the same Sharpe ratio that flatters a high-frequency market-making book silently penalizes a trend-following program built to endure long flat stretches. A single headline number cannot rank them fairly.

This framework replaces that number with a **six-pillar weighted composite** scoring ten strategy archetypes across six co-equal dimensions, each pillar carrying a published weight summing to 1.0. The design holds **return, risk, and adaptability as three independent axes**, never collapsing them into one return-maximizing score. Its pillars are defined on economic function rather than on any single era's dominant technique, so the structure is built to remain stable through 2030.

## 1.2 Scope and Boundary

The in-scope universe is **systematic, rules-based strategies** that generate repeatable signals from data and execute without discretionary override, spanning the liquidity spectrum from monthly-rebalanced equity books to sub-second market making.

**The framework measures four things and excludes three.** It measures risk-adjusted return net of turnover, slippage, and capacity decay; tail and drawdown risk; regime adaptability; and implementation frictions plus statistical rigor. Slippage — the gap between backtest and live fill price — is a first-class input. Excluded: discretionary trading, standalone macro forecasting, and generic investment advice.

The scope tension is real: a framework broad enough to cover both risk parity and HFT risks becoming too generic, while one precise for a single class fails the comparison mandate. The resolution is **normalization** — the six pillars stay constant across all archetypes, but the metric *within* each pillar may differ by class, with a common scale mapping every class's raw metric. **Generality lives in the pillars; rigor lives in the class-specific metric choice.**

The weights sum to 1.0 and **front-load the three performance-and-survival pillars** — R-1, R-2, and R-3 together carry 0.65 — reflecting the principle that survival and repeatability matter more than headline numbers. The structure follows multidimensional composite precedent: AlphaStocks' equity composite combines quality, value, momentum, and timing at 0.40/0.10/0.35/0.15. Weighting requires expert judgment about relative importance.

This framework deliberately caps its single heaviest pillar at 0.25 — a flatter distribution than AlphaStocks' 0.40 — so no one dimension dominates the cross-strategy ranking. The 0.65 concentration with a per-pillar cap is the answer to a recurring composite-design problem: the failure mode is that one fashionable dimension captures the ranking; the resolution is a weight ceiling that forces genuinely multidimensional performance to win. The weights are not frozen — uncertainty and sensitivity analysis is the standard discipline, and §14 re-runs the ranking under perturbed weights.

## 1.4 Standardized Vocabulary

Eight terms recur unmodified throughout:

- **Six-Pillar Composite Score** — a number 0–100, the weighted sum of six normalized pillar scores, structurally analogous to AlphaStocks' composite.
- **Deflated Sharpe Ratio (DSR)** — a Sharpe adjusted downward for the number of configurations tested before selecting the best; the more trials, the larger the deflation.
- **Probability of Backtest Overfitting (PBO)** — the combinatorially-resampled likelihood that the in-sample best underperforms the median candidate out-of-sample; PBO > 0.5 means the winner is more likely than not a below-median live performer.
- **IS–WFA–OOS Protocol** — three-stage validation: in-sample fitting, walk-forward rolling re-fit, and a final untouched out-of-sample block.
- **Minimum Regime Performance (MRP)** — performance in a strategy's single weakest regime (trending, mean-reverting, high-vol, low-vol).
- **Decay-Risk Frontier** — the tradeoff curve between capacity and alpha decay as size grows.
- **Expected Shortfall (CVaR)** — average loss in the worst tail beyond a percentile; one of the metrics composite risk models fuse.
- **Framework-Fit Tier** — the discrete band (top, middle, lower) §14 assigns each archetype.

## 1.5 Reading the Matrix

The matrix is read **by row** for strategy character and **by column** for cross-strategy contrast. Reading down the Regime Adaptability column, trend-following and mean-reversion read as near-mirror opposites: trend following is helped by strong directional trends and hurt by chop, while mean-reversion is hurt by exactly those trending regimes. This opposition is the structural reason a diversified book pairs the two — their weakest regimes fall in different places.

The precedent for deterministically grading every entity against a fixed system is the cross-framework consensus study that grades every US-listed equity against seven investor philosophies; that study found stocks satisfying all seven frameworks delivered a **median +73.8% return above the S&P 500** over a rolling five-year window, showing multi-criterion consensus scoring can carry real discriminating power. The six-column structure is built to absorb a seventh dimension without collapse: a new column enters with its own weight and the existing weights renormalize, which composite-indicator methodology treats as routine.

What remains unresolved at the framing stage is empirical calibration of the six weights — only §14's sensitivity analysis can confirm whether the 0.25/0.20/0.20/0.15/0.12/0.08 distribution produces tier assignments stable enough to act on.

# 2. The Standardization Gap

The evaluation tools the industry inherited from long-only asset management were built to answer a narrower question than this report poses. Each instrument resolves one facet of performance cleanly, yet none was designed to place an HFT book and a systematic-macro sleeve on a single comparable footing.

**The central deficiency is not that conventional metrics are wrong, but that they are incommensurable** — each reports in a different unit, against a different benchmark, under a different distributional assumption.

## 2.1 Conventional Tools and Their Failure Modes

**The Sharpe ratio** expresses excess return per unit of total volatility — a dimensionless quantity allowing cross-strategy ranking. Its defect: it assumes returns are described by their first two moments and therefore **misprices any strategy with meaningful skew or kurtosis**. A trend-following book carries positive skew — its long flat periods are "the price of admission" — and Sharpe understates its crisis-alpha value. An option-selling book carries mirror-image negative skew, which Sharpe rewards for steady premium while ignoring the left tail. **The Sharpe ratio is reliable only within a distributional family, not across them** — the very metric chosen for universality breaks first when the universe spans ten families. This is why the composite treats Sharpe as one input to R-1, deflated via DSR before it enters.

**The information ratio** measures active return over tracking error relative to a benchmark — natural for a long-only multi-factor fund. But it presupposes a single economically meaningful benchmark, which fails for most of the matrix: a market-neutral stat-arb pod has no natural long-only reference. Even where a benchmark exists, an early assessment found multifactor products **largely failed to outperform cap-weighted indexes** in return, risk-adjusted return, and downside protection — demonstrating that even a clean information ratio can certify a strategy adding no net value. The framework replaces it with a peer-group z-score.

**Maximum drawdown and Calmar** are tail instruments sharing a structural weakness: **MDD is a single realized path's worst outcome, not an expectation over the distribution.** Two strategies with identical true tail risk can report drawdowns differing by 2× purely because one lived through a stress event in its sample. The tradeoff is genuine — allocators want a model-free reading off a track record, and MDD delivers it, but it is a path-dependent sample artifact. The framework retains MDD as a descriptive statistic but shifts scoring weight onto CVaR. Calmar inherits and amplifies this instability, passing comparability only when evaluation windows are held identical.

**Factor attribution** works for equity books decomposing onto market, size, value, and momentum, but a machine-learning book may load on exposures no standard factor set names. **The hedge-fund index family** exposes the deepest incommensurability: indices from different providers **correlate as low as 0.67, and two indices tracking the same strategy can differ by more than 1,400 bp per month.** That divergence is not measurement noise — it is evidence the benchmark itself is a construction choice rather than an observable, driven by divergent inclusion rules, survivorship, and self-reporting biases.

| Tool | Question answered | Failure across families | Disposition |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sharpe | Return per unit total vol | Mislabels skewed books | Input after DSR deflation |
| Information ratio | Active return per tracking error | No benchmark for market-neutral | Replaced by peer z-score |
| MDD / Calmar | Worst realized loss | Single-path artifact | Descriptive only; CVaR weighted |
| Factor attribution | Return decomposed onto exposures | No names for ML exposures | Diagnostic, not scoring |
| Hedge-fund indices | Peer reference | 0.67 correlation; 1,400bp gap | Unusable as common reference |

## 2.2 Why Stacking Cannot Substitute for Integration

**Incommensurability** is the condition where two measurements cannot be expressed on a common scale without an arbitrary conversion rule. The composite literature states the problem plainly: weights are meant to reflect relative importance, but combining a Sharpe of 1.2, an IR of 0.8, and an 18% drawdown requires asserting how many units of drawdown equal one unit of Sharpe — a conversion the data never supplies. Heterogeneous metrics force normalization (an arbitrary scaling choice) which feeds a weighting choice, producing a composite whose ranking can reverse under perturbation of either. This is why §14's sensitivity analysis is structural, not polish: a composite that cannot show rank stability under perturbation has merely hidden incommensurability, not solved it.

The regime confound deepens it. Two strategies can post the same full-sample Sharpe with opposite regime exposures — trend-following earning in trending/high-vol regimes and bleeding in flat periods, mean-reversion doing the reverse. A single Sharpe reports them as interchangeable. Where Calmar compresses all regime information into one worst-path number, the **MRP** measure reports performance in the single weakest regime — turning the confound into an explicit scored axis. The two imply opposite tradeoffs: Calmar rewards a strategy that avoided a deep loss by luck of sample; MRP penalizes one structurally fragile in a regime it has not yet encountered.

The tempting shortcut — stacking all instruments side by side — defers the weighting decision to the reader; integration makes it auditable. A defensible cross-strategy benchmark is achievable only if **every normalization and weighting choice is published, reproducible, and tested for rank stability**.

## 2.3 Benchmark Platforms and the Coverage Gap

The AI-for-quant benchmark generation is the most serious standardization attempt yet. **QuantBench** offers industry-aligned, full-pipeline coverage; **QFBench** requires agents to write and execute quantitative code with no retrieval, isolating domain competence; **QuantArena** provides controlled live-market evaluation varying only the investment doctrine. Each solves a real sub-problem, and well-designed benchmarks do more than rank — they spotlight gaps and surface failure modes.

**But none delivers a risk-and-friction-normalized ranking across strategy families.** QuantArena holds the doctrine constant, so by construction it cannot place stat-arb and trend-following on a common tier. QFBench scores code competence, not capacity-decay or slippage. QuantBench's strengths are standardization and coverage, not a scheme rendering two classes' Decay-Risk Frontiers comparable. The ML-benchmark world shows both the difficulty and direction: frontier models without tools reach only **12–28% accuracy** on tasks junior analysts handle, with tool augmentation plateauing at **67.4% vs an 80% human baseline** — the gap is the signature of an unsolved evaluation problem.

A second gap is temporal. SWE-bench-Live updates monthly for contamination-free evaluation; LIVECODEBENCH uses time-bounded competition problems. Both illustrate a principle the quant domain has barely adopted: **a static backtest sample is a contamination risk**, because a strategy tuned on a fixed window is a hypothesis fitted to its own test set. The coverage gap is thus two-dimensional — a gap in **breadth** (no common tier across families) and in **time** (no contamination-resistant protocol). The backtesting substrate compounds it: a vectorized engine (VectorBT) models execution differently from an event-driven one (Backtrader), making the substrate choice itself a source of the paper-versus-live gap.

| Platform | Standardizes | Holds fixed | Gap contribution |
|---|---|---|---|
| QuantBench | Full-pipeline, industry-aligned | No cross-class normalization | Pipeline breadth |
| QFBench | Code-execution knowledge | Capacity/slippage | Contamination isolation |
| QuantArena | Live-market doctrine variation | The doctrine itself | Controlled isolation |

**The standardization gap is not a shortage of good metrics but the absence of a coordinate system** that renders them commensurable across families and stable across time.

# 3. Strategy Taxonomy

The strategy universe is a collection of structurally incompatible businesses sharing the word "quant." A monthly-rebalanced multi-factor book and a sub-second market-making engine differ so completely in alpha source, data need, and holding period that naive side-by-side ranking misleads.

## 3.1 Classification Axes

Four orthogonal axes determine nearly every downstream evaluation decision.

**Alpha source is the primary partition** — it determines which regimes help and which risk-adjusted measure is even meaningful. Directional momentum, relative-value mispricing, and learned statistical edge are three economically distinct engines. A premia-harvesting engine earns compensation for bearing persistent risk; a relative-value engine earns a convergence that self-extinguishes once arbitraged — demanding opposite validation postures.

**Horizon and frequency move together** and jointly fix turnover, which governs capacity. Multi-factor strategies use daily/monthly data and longer horizons; HFT requires tick-level data and sub-second horizons. A shorter horizon requires finer data, drives higher turnover, and compresses capacity because each incremental dollar moves price against a book recycling positions many times daily.

**Data need separates reproducible from proprietary edges.** Vendor-standard inputs are reproducible by construction; proprietary alt-data products are licensed and far harder to replicate. Archetypes at the proprietary pole will always score worse on Statistical Robustness, because the IS–WFA–OOS Protocol cannot be independently re-run without the licensed feed.

**Alpha source is causally upstream** of the other three — choose where return comes from and you have largely fixed the horizon, frequency, and data you must buy.

## 3.2 The Ten Archetypes

Ten archetypes span the four axes: Multi-Factor Equity, Statistical Arbitrage, ML/Alpha-Signal, Trend-Following/CTA, Mean-Reversion, HFT, Electronic Market Making, Options/Volatility, Alternative-Data, and Risk Parity/Systematic Macro. (Detailed per-strategy profiles appear in §12–§13.)

## 3.3 Why Heterogeneity Breaks Naive Comparison

A raw Sharpe computed on each book is not comparable across archetypes, because strategies differ in frequency and horizon. The apparent paradox — practitioners routinely rank a monthly book against a sub-second engine on one Sharpe table — resolves only by **normalizing for frequency, turnover, and capacity before any comparison**. The expected outcome of naive comparison is a clean ranking; the actual outcome is a frequency-induced artifact, because annualization scales differently with holding period. Any comparison skipping the §10 normalization step will systematically misrank high-turnover against low-turnover archetypes regardless of how rigorous the underlying metrics appear.

# 4. Dimension 1 — Return & Risk-Adjusted Performance

A headline return conceals almost everything that matters for cross-strategy comparison. This pillar is one of three co-equal axes, never the maximization target. The core finding: **no single risk-adjusted ratio is sufficient, because each encodes an incompatible assumption about distribution shape**, so the framework scores on a vector of complementary measures.

## 4.1 Return Decomposition

Raw return fails as a comparison currency the moment two strategies carry different risk. Excess return (above the risk-free rate) is the minimum correction. **Alpha is excess return versus a specified risk model, and its meaning depends entirely on which model you choose.** A strategy can post high absolute return purely by levering market beta; the framework strips compensated risk exposure before scoring, so twelve percent of apparent alpha may become three percent of genuine excess.

Beta should be estimated by regression with attention to time-variation. A misspecified or stale beta biases the systematic/idiosyncratic split, inflating the residual labeled alpha. A signal appearing to generate alpha may simply re-express a known factor tilt the model omitted — so alpha is never scored in isolation; it is always reported with its risk model, and two strategies are compared only under the same model. The ML class, at a realistic Sharpe of 0.5–1.5, is most exposed to this masquerade.

The **Information Ratio** bridges raw and risk-adjusted measures — active return per unit of active risk, where tracking error is the volatility of the gap between strategy and benchmark. Where alpha tells you the magnitude of excess, the IR tells you its consistency; the two diverge sharply when a high-alpha strategy tracks erratically. The IR is weighted heavily for benchmark-relative strategies (multi-factor equity) and lightly for absolute-return strategies (trend-following), where no natural benchmark exists.

**The return pillar scores not the magnitude of return but the portion surviving decomposition against a specified risk model.**

## 4.2 Risk-Adjusted Ratios — Definitions and Disagreements

| Metric | Measures | Risk denominator | Where it misleads |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sharpe | Excess return / total vol | Total std dev (symmetric) | Non-normal returns |
| Sortino | Excess return / downside vol | Downside deviation below target | Always flatters vs Sharpe; incommensurable |
| Calmar | Return / worst drawdown | Maximum drawdown | Sensitive to sample length |
| Information Ratio | Active return / active risk | Tracking error | Requires a benchmark |

The **Sharpe** is the industry default but "lies on non-normal returns." The deeper problem: for fat-tailed returns (tail index 2–4), **the estimated Sharpe is itself asymptotically biased and converges slowly**, which means it can actively mislead allocators into over-ranking strategies whose tail risk is understated. A skewness-adjusted Sharpe partially repairs this, rising with mean return and not decreasing with positive skewness.

The **Sortino** penalizes only downside variation — right for trend-following and crisis-alpha books. But this very repair creates incommensurability: Sortino's downside focus makes it incompatible with Sharpe's symmetric treatment. Sortino almost always reads higher, so a manager can select it precisely because it flatters. The framework requires both reported side by side with a consistent downside target; a Sortino without its target fails comparability outright.

The **Calmar** denominates by worst drawdown — right for capital-destruction-averse mandates. But it is acutely sensitive to sample length and single events: a short window that avoided the worst regime posts a flattering figure that collapses over a full cycle. The framework requires its MDD be estimated over a sample containing at least one full regime cycle.

**The disagreement among these ratios is a feature, not a defect**: each is a different projection of the return distribution. The framework scores on the full vector weighted by class, never on a scalar — following the precedent that fuses Sharpe, Sortino, Calmar, Omega, and CVaR into one lens.

## 4.3 Leverage- and Turnover-Adjusted Returns

Two strategies can report identical ratios while one runs 4× the leverage and 10× the turnover. **Leverage-adjusted metrics (Sharpe scaled by √leverage) and turnover-adjusted returns normalize for fair comparison.**

Without leverage adjustment, a 5× book looks 5× as good as its unlevered twin despite identical risk-adjusted edge. This bites hardest for Risk Parity, whose entire premise is levering low-vol assets to match high-vol ones; the √leverage scaling reveals whether the underlying signal is actually stronger or merely amplified.

Turnover adjustment delivers a counterintuitive result: the turnover-adjusted IR is always lower, but **contrary to the fundamental law of active management, managers may improve performance by limiting turnover** rather than maximizing breadth. More bets raise the theoretical IR; the cost of realizing them can more than offset the gain. A very-high-frequency strategy can look superb on gross IR yet mediocre on the adjusted figure — so HFT and market-making are evaluated on the turnover-adjusted measure as policy.

**Leverage and turnover adjustment are preconditions for comparability, not refinements.** Without them, the return pillar systematically over-credits strategies that buy their numbers with borrowed capital or frantic trading.

# 5. Dimension 2 — Risk, Tail & Drawdown

Risk is a layered construct: no strategy earns a passing grade by reporting a single dispersion statistic. Three families capture mathematically non-overlapping hazards — **dispersion** (volatility, downside deviation, beta), **tail** (VaR, CVaR), and **path-dependent** (MDD, recovery time). The August 2007 quant meltdown is the canonical demonstration that a strategy can look benign on one family while carrying catastrophic exposure on another.

| Metric | Family | Definition | Misses |
|---|---|---|---|
| Volatility (σ) | Dispersion | Std dev of returns | Treats upside/downside symmetrically |
| Downside deviation | Dispersion | Std dev below MAR | Ignores upside variance magnitude |
| Beta | Dispersion | Sensitivity to benchmark | Stable only if relationship stable |
| VaR | Tail | Loss exceeded α% of time | Silent beyond the quantile |
| CVaR / ES | Tail | Average loss beyond VaR | Needs tail samples |
| MDD | Path | Worst realized peak-to-trough | Path-dependent; sample-sensitive |

## 5.1 Dispersion Measures

**Volatility** treats a 5% gain and 5% loss as equivalent — efficient for symmetric distributions, actively misleading for deliberately skewed payoffs. It cannot distinguish a short-vol book that occasionally blows up from a trend book that loses slowly and occasionally wins large. Volatility enters the composite as a baseline input but never scores a strategy alone. A Sortino-based ranking favors positively skewed strategies over a Sharpe-based ranking of the same set — the two are not interchangeable.

**Downside deviation** measures dispersion only below a minimum acceptable return (MAR), stopping the penalty on the upside variance investors want. But the MAR choice is not neutral: a universal MAR of zero would make near-zero-mean HFT books look riskless, so the framework adopts a strategy-class-specific MAR tied to each class's funding cost.

**Beta** separates common-factor variance from idiosyncratic residual. The framework requires a **stress-window beta alongside the full-sample beta**, because calm-window beta is unstable when regimes shift. The MIT study of August 2007 identified two unwinds — August 1 (10:45–11:30 am) and August 6 (open–1:00 pm) — both beginning with financial-sector stocks long book-to-market and short earnings momentum. Positions a calm-window beta would treat as diversifying shared, in that window, a common factor signature only a stress estimate surfaces.

## 5.2 VaR, CVaR & Non-Normal Tails

**VaR is silent beyond the threshold**: a 99% one-day VaR of 2% tells you the loss exceeded on the worst 1% of days but not whether it averages 3% or 30%. **CVaR closes exactly that blind spot**, reporting the average tail loss — which is why it is the primary tail metric and VaR a supplementary reference. Suppose two strategies share a 99% VaR of 2%; if one's breached tail averages 2.5% and the other's 9%, their CVaRs are 2.5% and 9% — a **3.6× difference in conditional severity** the shared VaR renders invisible.

The hardest problem is non-normality. The Gaussian assumption in parametric VaR systematically understates extreme losses for crowded systematic strategies. In August 2007, **Goldman's Global Alpha lost 22.5%, Highbridge Statistical Opportunities fell 18% in the first week, and even Renaissance's Institutional Equities dropped 8.7%.** The standard claim is that fatter-tailed parametric models would have caught this; the MIT reconstruction suggests the hazard was not a fat tail in the marginal distribution but a **sudden correlation shift** that no univariate tail model could represent. Non-normality must be measured in the joint distribution. A tail metric estimated on a strategy's own returns in isolation cannot see the crowding dependence that turns a diversified-looking book into one indistinguishable from its peers in a liquidation cascade.

## 5.3 Maximum Drawdown & Recovery

MDD measures what actually happened rather than what the distribution implies. Its defining feature is brutal recovery arithmetic: a drawdown of depth *d* requires a gain of *d/(1−d)* to recover. Goldman's Global Alpha (−22.5%) needed +29.0%; Renaissance's fund (−8.7%) needed +9.5%. The **2.6× ratio in losses understates the 3.05× ratio in required recovery** — a convexity that is the mathematical heart of why deep drawdowns are disproportionately destructive, and why the framework weights MDD non-linearly.

Recovery time is the other half of the path-dependent story. Two strategies with identical MDD can have radically different underwater durations; trend-following's long flat periods are "the price of admission." The framework records recovery time as a distinct input. A book's 2007 drawdown depended on whether it deleveraged before or after the August 6 wave — so a standardized benchmark must evaluate drawdowns against a daily (or intraday) equity path, not a month-end snapshot.

**Risk is irreducibly three-dimensional**, and August 2007 proves the hazards are non-substitutable: a book can pass on volatility while failing catastrophically on crowding-conditional tail and drawdown depth. The denominator deserves the same rigor as the numerator.

# 6. Dimension 3 — Market-Regime Adaptability

Regime adaptability separates strategies that survive across environments from those that earn a high Sharpe in one regime and collapse in another. This dimension scores the **conditional floor**, not the unconditional mean. A momentum strategy showed a **Sharpe of ~1.8 in trending markets but −0.4 once the regime shifted** — averaging produces a respectable blended figure that misrepresents the strategy entirely.

## 6.1 The Regime Taxonomy

A regime is a persistent state in which the joint distribution of returns, volatility, and liquidity is approximately stable. The taxonomy is a **floor-estimation lattice**: cells exist so the weakest can be found and priced.

**Directional axis.** Price-only bull/bear rules ignore volatility dynamics and misidentify states during high-volatility, unchanged-trend periods. A volatility-aware regime-switching specification using sign *and* volatility distinguishes a true bear from a volatile chop, because the discriminating information is second-moment information the direction rule discards.

**Volatility axis and the crisis cell.** The August 2007 liquidity shock is the archetypal crisis cell — falling prices and spiking volatility from crowded-position unwinds, not a simple directional move. A liquidity shock leads to a forced unwind of crowded positions, driving simultaneous selling of the same names. This is where stat-arb and multi-factor books register their worst outcomes.

**Rate-shift axis.** In rising-rate environments, traditional volatility-allocated risk parity underperforms as rising bond yields raise portfolio risk, whereas **macro-aware risk parity** incorporating inflation and growth adapts and preserves diversification — a signal that has predicted equity-duration returns across eight developed markets for over three decades. As rates stay more variable than the 2010–2021 backdrop, the rate-shift cell becomes the binding MRP constraint for books tuned on a falling-rate sample.

**Crash-duration sub-axis.** Trend-following gained **14.3% across eleven crashes while 60/40 lost 22.8%**, but crisis alpha is not automatic: slow-building crises give trend signals time to establish protective positions; **V-shaped crashes do not.** A trend book's MRP floor sits in the V-shaped sub-cell, not the slow-crash cell where it earns its alpha. Mean-reversion shows the opposite sign in the same cell.

The partition's resolution is itself a tradeoff: fine partitions locate the failure cell but starve each cell of observations; coarse partitions estimate reliably but hide the break. Cell granularity is set by the minimum observation count needed for a stable conditional estimate.

## 6.2 Minimum Regime Performance & the Decay-Risk Frontier

**MRP is the lowest efficiency a strategy achieves across regimes** — the weakest realized, not the peak or average. Operationally, MRP = min over regimes of the regime-conditional DSR, using the deflated Sharpe so thin-cell estimates are penalized for short samples.

A strategy with 1.8 Sharpe trending and −0.4 elsewhere has an **MRP of −0.4, not the blended 0.7** a naive average reports. The 1.1-unit gap is the regime-fragility premium the headline hides. Where Sharpe, Sortino, and Calmar each compress all history into one unconditional number, MRP refuses to average and reports the worst state directly.

The **Decay-Risk Frontier** plots peak efficiency against MRP durability. Organizations naturally drift toward efficiency; maintaining flexibility requires deliberately un-balancing to favor it. The apparent paradox — the highest-Sharpe strategies are often most fragile — resolves once peak efficiency and MRP are recognized as separately priced axes. A book far up the efficiency axis but low on durability is dominated by one slightly lower on efficiency but materially higher on MRP, because **realized multi-regime compounding is governed by the floor, not the peak.**

This is implemented in production: a public repository (created March 2026) combines the Bailey–López de Prado deflated Sharpe with explicit regime-decay detection, tagline "stop deploying overfitted strategies." A floor-based durability metric is implementable against real return series with the same DSR machinery the framework already requires.

## 6.3 Regime-Label Reliability

Every MRP number depends on a prior step usually taken on faith: the regime labels are themselves detector output, and **a regime-conditional metric inherits the error rate of its detector.** A 15.3-year backtest (3,841 trading days, Feb 2011–May 2026) of a regime detector found **two of five strategy labels came back opposite to prediction** — a 40% inversion rate. Nearly half the calls would mis-assign returns to the wrong MRP cell, corrupting the floor.

A more reactive detector reduces lag but raises inversions; a conservative one cuts inversions but lags true changes. The framework requires the detector's **out-of-sample label accuracy be reported alongside the MRP**, so a floor from a 60%-accurate detector is discounted relative to one from a 90%-accurate detector. Price-only rules name a specific cause of inversion — reading a flat-but-violent tape as calm sideways is exactly the error that produces an inverted label for a vol-sensitive strategy. A vol-conditioning detector is the default; price-only is admissible only when its OOS accuracy is independently demonstrated.

A detector is a model subject to the same multiple-testing inflation the DSR corrects. If a practitioner tries twenty specifications and keeps the one whose cells produce the most flattering MRP, the floor is selection-biased upward. The protocol: the detector is **fit in-sample, validated walk-forward, and its MRP reported out-of-sample**, so the label-selection step cannot borrow information from the floor-measurement period — the same overfitting pathway PBO was built to detect.

**The floor is only as good as the labels.** Detector validation and floor computation are a single two-stage protocol, not two independent steps.

# 7. Dimension 4 — Implementation Frictions & Capacity

Implementation frictions are where backtested promise and live realization diverge most violently. Raw pro-forma performance across **1,726 commercial strategies** showed limited live portability and deteriorated sharply against benchmarks; back-tested Sharpe predicted out-of-sample performance with **R² below 0.025**, almost no predictive value. The framework treats the live-paper gap as a first-class scoring axis, decomposing it into trading costs, latency, and structural ceilings.

## 7.1 Transaction Costs, Slippage & Market Impact

Every strategy must carry an explicit cost model reporting commissions, spread, slippage, and impact **separately**, because they scale differently with order size and turnover. A single global cost assumption is a disqualifying backtest error, because it systematically flatters high-turnover, thin-edge strategies whose entire alpha sits inside the cost band.

**Multi-Factor Equity** is the reference case: moderate turnover, liquid names, durable edge. Its dominant friction is the bid-ask spread on rebalancing days, not latency; the paper-to-live gap is a fraction of gross alpha rather than a multiple of it. Market impact is modeled as increasing with order size relative to volume, so a book trading 5% of a name's daily volume incurs far smaller charges than a stat-arb book trading 30%. **Capacity is rarely binding** — the marginal-alpha ceiling sits far above where most sleeves run. Verdict: clear pass on the cost criterion; the benchmark against which thinner-edge strategies are judged.

**Mean-Reversion** is the cleanest illustration of why cost realism dominates: thin per-trade edge, high turnover, paying the spread most often. Its gross Sharpe is nearly uninformative until the spread-and-impact model is applied. The framework treats Mean-Reversion and Stat-Arb as **failing the cost criterion whenever their cost model is not explicitly spread-realistic** — the spread they pay most is precisely what a flat-cost backtest understates. High turnover multiplies per-trade spread, compressing net edge toward zero, so any understatement flips a paper-profitable book into a live-losing one.

**Statistical Arbitrage** shares this sensitivity but adds scale. Helios Capital's pod manages **$1.5 billion with a 38-person team** split across research, engineering, and trading — illustrating the cost structure headline capital efficiency conceals. As AUM grows, larger orders move prices and erode per-trade edge down to the capacity threshold. Conditional pass: survives only when the cost model is spread-realistic *and* the book is sized below its impact-driven ceiling.

**The discriminating power of this axis comes from the interaction between turnover and the spread-plus-impact stack** — portability is governed by net edge per unit turnover, not gross edge.

## 7.2 Latency & HFT Constraints

For the highest-frequency classes the binding friction is the microsecond delay between signal and fill. A latency-arbitrage book's entire edge lives inside a window narrower than a poorly-located server's round-trip time. The evaluation standard requires **tick-level order-book reconstruction and an explicit colocation assumption**, since consolidated end-of-second data cannot model the queue position that determines whether a fill occurs.

**HFT** is where the paper-to-live gap is widest and most adversarial. A backtest assuming mid-price or even quoted-touch fills ignores queue position, cancellation latency, and adverse selection. For this class the realistic net edge is determined less by the predictive signal than by colocation and execution engineering — the friction budget and the signal are inseparable. A working modeling assumption: a large fraction of a pure latency-arbitrage edge decays within the first one to two years of live deployment as competitors close the gap. Composite weight must tilt overwhelmingly toward Implementation Frictions & Capacity.

**Electronic Market Making** earns the spread rather than crossing it, inverting several frictions while sharing latency dependence. It collects the spread cost-sensitive strategies pay, so its paper-to-live gap is smaller than a latency-arbitrage book's — provided the adverse-selection model is honest (being filled precisely when the market is about to move against the quote).

## 7.3 Turnover, Leverage, Shorting & Capacity

**Trend-Following** runs weeks-to-months with low-to-moderate turnover and high capacity in liquid futures; modest spread and roll cost give it a small paper-live gap. Its Sortino is the honest measure given positive skew.

**Risk Parity / Systematic Macro** equalizes risk contributions by levering lower-volatility assets. Its dominant friction is **financing cost on the levered leg** — benign in normal regimes but assumed to spike in funding crises when cross-asset correlations rise toward one. Months-long horizon, low turnover, very high capacity.

A long-only constraint compresses the opportunity set, lowering achievable IR; relaxing the shorting constraint can raise IR while lowering capacity (via borrow cost and recall risk). **Turnover, leverage, and shorting are a single coupled system whose joint resolution is the strategy's true capacity**, with the marginal-alpha-threshold definition as the unifying ceiling.

# 8. Dimension 5 — Statistical Robustness & Validation

Every number reported elsewhere rests on a backtest — the single most corruptible artifact in quantitative finance. **A strategy that scores well yet was selected from thousands of silent failures has earned a number describing the researcher's search effort, not the strategy's edge.** Analysts now backtest millions of configurations, with optimizers built to maximize in-sample Sharpe.

## 8.1 The IS–WFA–OOS Protocol

Validation begins with a partition rule, because no deflator repairs a contaminated holdout. The protocol fixes the order in which a strategy may touch its own history: **in-sample (IS)** for fitting, **walk-forward (WFA)** re-estimating parameters on a rolling basis so every traded observation came from a model that never saw it, and a **sealed out-of-sample (OOS)** block for one terminal test. The gap between the IS and OOS information ratios is itself a diagnostic.

Walk-forward exposes drawdown clustering a single optimized backtest hides; the honest drawdown estimate is the **worst walk-forward segment**, not the smoothed full-sample figure. WFA is also the primary instrument for MRP, since rolling re-estimation spans whatever regimes the history contains.

Statistical power is a function of holding horizon. A long-horizon trend system generates few independent observations per year — its long flat periods mean years of history contain only a modest count of independent trades. The framework scales required minimum history to turnover: low-turnover strategies need longer calendars to clear the same significance bar.

Walk-forward is the *minimum* acceptable partition, not the strongest. A 2024 study finds walk-forward exhibits **higher temporal variability and weaker stationarity than combinatorial purged cross-validation**, reducing its overfitting prevention. Crucially, **combinatorial cross-validation yields a *distribution* of Sharpe ratios** across recombinations, which is the raw material PBO consumes — a single walk-forward path produces only one ranking and cannot estimate the overfitting probability.

## 8.2 Deflated Sharpe Ratio & Probability of Backtest Overfitting

Partition discipline quarantines the holdout; it does not price the inflation multiple testing injects. The **DSR** (Bailey & López de Prado, 2014) corrects an observed Sharpe for selection bias, overfitting, and non-normality. The **PBO** (Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado, Zhu) estimates the probability the in-sample best underperforms the median out-of-sample.

The DSR compares the observed Sharpe not against zero but against the *expected maximum* Sharpe a number of random trials would produce by chance — so the credited alpha is that in excess of what pure search manufactures. Its underappreciated correction is for non-normality: for fat-tailed returns (tail index 2–4) the estimated Sharpe is biased and slow-converging, and the DSR's incorporation of skewness and kurtosis interrupts that chain. The DSR also yields a **minimum backtest length** — the span before a given Sharpe is significant once multiple testing and non-normality are controlled.

The PBO demands the *full matrix* of trial results, not the surviving best. The discipline is cultural as much as computational: a team must retain every variant tested, because the PBO is undefined over a single survivor. A strategy can pass one deflator and fail the other — a high single-best Sharpe may survive deflation yet sit in a procedure whose PBO reveals the in-sample ranking is uninformative about out-of-sample order.

Guidance: a configuration selected from ~20+ variants is presumptively inflated until deflated; **a PBO above 0.5 disqualifies a strategy from the upper tier regardless of raw Sharpe.** An ML strategy reporting a backtest Sharpe well above the 0.5–1.5 realistic band without a DSR or PBO fails the robustness criterion on its face.

| Diagnostic | Corrects | Required input | Disqualifying reading |
|---|---|---|---|
| DSR | Selection bias, multiple testing, non-normality, short samples | Sharpe + trial count + moments | Significance below threshold |
| PBO | In/out-of-sample rank instability | Full trial matrix | PBO > 0.5 |
| Min backtest length | Insufficient sample | Target Sharpe + frequency | History below implied minimum |

## 8.3 Survivorship, Look-Ahead & Data-Snooping

Deflators correct the arithmetic of selection but not a dataset wrong before any strategy touched it.

**Survivorship** inflates alpha by deleting failures: a universe reconstructed from today's survivors omits every delisted name, so the live strategy that must hold losers until they delist can never reproduce the backtest alpha. The framework requires a point-in-time universe with terminal returns on delisted names.

**Look-ahead** understates risk by using information not knowable at decision time — a fundamental figure timestamped to its fiscal period rather than release date. A strategy that "knew" an earnings revision early shows a far shallower MDD than the live system. The control is a strict information-availability timestamp on every input.

**Data-snooping** manufactures spurious regime robustness. Adaptive specification search produces statistically significant backtests even when the true process is a martingale difference with no predictability. A strategy snooped into existence on a regime sequence appears to adapt across regimes because the search was conducted across them — combinatorial cross-validation is the structural defense, breaking the snooped pattern a single historical sequence preserved.

Bias severity scales with horizon in opposite directions: long-horizon strategies are most exposed to survivorship and significance-power problems; high-turnover firms face the opposite (the median HFT firm realizes an annualized Sharpe of only **4.3**, indicating outliers inflate perceived class performance). Significance testing cannot assume normality: the framework requires a DSR with empirical higher moments or a distribution-free test, plus a multiple-testing correction scaling the threshold with search intensity.

**The credibility of every number is bounded by the weakest of partition discipline, deflation, and bias controls — and the framework scores the minimum, not the average.** This is why the robustness pillar is gating rather than additive.

# 9. Data Standards & Reproducibility

Two strategies that look identical on a scorecard diverge entirely once their backtests run on the same survivorship-cleaned, point-in-time feed under the same execution assumptions. Fair comparison is a data-and-process property before it is a scoring property.

## 9.1 Tiered Data Standard

Data requirements differ by class by orders of magnitude, so the standard is tiered, not flat. **Tier 1**: adjusted daily price/volume with corporate-action reconstruction. **Tier 2**: adds point-in-time fundamentals and intraday bars. **Tier 3**: adds full order-book depth and nanosecond timestamps. The framework certifies no result computed below a class's minimum tier. A uniform high-fidelity feed would guarantee comparability but impose order-book costs on a monthly factor strategy; asymmetric tiering holds each class to the fidelity its signal horizon consumes.

Every price series must carry a **corporate-action reconstruction log** with each adjustment's as-of date, so a verifier can replay the series as it appeared on any historical date. For HFT and market making, top-of-book quotes without queue modeling overstate fill probability and gross spread capture — full-depth book reconstruction with exchange-sequenced timestamps is mandatory; top-of-book fails Tier 3.

**Point-in-time integrity** records each datum as known on its availability date, not as later restated. A backtest using final restated earnings rather than originally reported figures trades on nonexistent information. **Alternative data is the weakest link**: vendors may not certify point-in-time integrity, so two strategies on the same nominal dataset can silently differ in whether data was point-in-time or back-filled. The framework requires a vendor-provenance attestation — collection lag, back-fill policy, first-availability timestamp.

## 9.2 The Fair-Comparison Baseline

A fixed set of assumptions applied identically so differences reflect edge, not assumption shopping. The engine choice is neutralized: the fill model must be declared and an event-driven cross-check run for any strategy whose edge depends on intrabar execution. Vectorized engines are acceptable for signal discovery but the same speed enables the multiple-testing overfitting the DSR catches, so they require event-driven confirmation before certification.

| Assumption | Uniform rule | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Slippage | Volume-participation scaled to order's ADV fraction | Stops low-turnover books hiding impact |
| Commissions | Fixed per-class schedule, disclosed | Equalizes cost assumptions |
| Survivorship | Delisted names retained with terminal returns | Removes upward bias |
| Financing | Borrow/funding applied to leverage and shorts | Charges risk-parity its true carry |
| Capacity | Returns at stated AUM, not frictionless | Forces capacity ceiling into view |

Survivorship is the most commonly botched: a backtest silently dropping delisted names reports a structurally inflated return, largest for strategies loading on distressed names. The capacity rule links to the Decay-Risk Frontier, ensuring an HFT book with a spectacular frictionless Sharpe at $10M is not ranked above a risk-parity book scaling to $10B.

**Multiple-testing control is a baseline requirement, not a diagnostic.** Every submission must report the number of variants tested so the DSR can deflate accordingly. An undeflated Sharpe is not evidence of edge — it is evidence of search. A submission omitting the variant count fails the robustness criterion outright, because its Sharpe cannot be deflated.

## 9.3 Reproducible Reporting

Reproducibility means an independent verifier can regenerate the scorecard to numerical tolerance. The **run manifest** records the data-tier certification, corporate-action log hash, engine name and version, full cost-and-slippage configuration, variant count, and IS–WFA–OOS split dates. Each field maps to a defect: the tier guards data fidelity, the variant count feeds the DSR, the split dates guard against the PBO. The manifest resolves the proprietary-secrecy tension — it records the hashes and configurations that let a verifier confirm the backtest ran as described without disclosing the alpha logic. Reproducibility means the process is auditable, not that the secret is disclosed.

The open gap: the framework can attest a vendor's claimed point-in-time integrity but cannot independently verify a back-fill the vendor itself misrecorded — closing it requires a third-party data-audit standard that does not yet exist.

# 10. Cross-Strategy Normalization

A Sharpe on a daily mean-reversion book and a Sharpe on a quarterly CTA are not denominated in the same units until horizon, turnover, leverage, and capacity differences are neutralized. Normalization proceeds along three axes: mechanical transforms, peer/benchmark adjustment, and uncertainty treatment.

## 10.1 The Four Transforms

**Horizon.** The framework annualizes every statistic to a common basis but **tags each metric with the effective sample size** implied by its holding period, so two strategies with the same annualized Sharpe are not treated as equally well-estimated when one sampled thousands of cycles and the other a few dozen. This tag feeds the confidence intervals below.

**Turnover.** The raw IR is replaced everywhere by its turnover-adjusted form, which is always lower and which shows performance *falling* when turnover exceeds the point where marginal captured alpha exceeds marginal cost. A frequently-trading stat-arb book is scored on the alpha that survives its own trading.

**Leverage.** Returns and volatilities are normalized to unit-notional-leverage via the √leverage Sharpe scaling. A levered low-vol book and an unlevered equity sleeve could report identical Sharpes while their capital-at-risk profiles diverge — the transform reveals whether the signal is stronger or merely borrowed. This is decisive for Risk Parity, letting it be compared against unlevered Multi-Factor Equity without crediting borrowed volatility.

**Capacity.** Each strategy's risk-adjusted return is discounted by its proximity to the alpha-decay threshold on the Decay-Risk Frontier. The transform is **multiplicative**, so a strategy scoring well on returns but poorly on capacity cannot ride its return score to a top tier — the discount caps it.

| Axis | Distortion removed | Transform | Consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Horizon | Unequal effective sample sizes | Annualize + effective-sample tag | Feeds confidence intervals |
| Turnover | Alpha not surviving trading cost | Turnover-adjusted IR | Replaces raw IR |
| Leverage | Manufactured Sharpe via borrowing | Unit-notional rescaling | Neutralizes risk-parity leverage |
| Capacity | Edge that cannot carry capital | Decay-Risk discount | Multiplicative cap |

## 10.2 Peer-Group & Benchmark Adjustment

A strategy's raw return may largely be compensation for exposures rather than skill; this layer strips them out so what remains is the residual worth scoring. **The correct benchmark is not a market index but the peer set and factor exposures that define what skill would have to beat.**

For **ML strategies**, the 0.5–1.5 Sharpe band is the relevant peer distribution: a book claiming a backtested Sharpe well above 1.5 is more likely overfit than an outlier, and is scored against that band rather than an absolute threshold, with the DSR trial count set to the peer-set cardinality.

For **Risk Parity / Systematic Macro**, the benchmark is a comparably-levered multi-asset reference, with the leverage transform a precondition. Its weakest case is a correlated multi-asset shock; walk-forward must use explicit correlation-regime splits.

For **Alternative-Data**, an index is the wrong benchmark — the return mixes informational alpha with uncompensated factor exposure. The proper benchmark is a factor-exposure decomposition isolating the residual after known factor returns are removed. A book benchmarked against a naked index fails comparability regardless of how attractive its index-relative return appears.

## 10.3 Confidence Intervals & Trade-Off Treatment

A normalized point estimate is still a point estimate; treating it as exact turns the framework into a false-precision machine. **Every input is reported as a point estimate plus an interval, and strategies are ranked on the lower bound** — so a high-but-uncertain score does not outrank a slightly-lower but tightly-estimated one. An ML book with a short days-to-weeks track record observes few independent regimes, carrying a wide interval. This is where the horizon tag pays off: the strategy that sampled more cycles earns a narrower interval and a higher lower bound.

The composite interval reflects the joint uncertainty of all blended metrics and widens when inputs disagree. Two strategies with the same point score can have very different intervals: one whose Sharpe and CVaR tell opposite stories is the less reliable rank.

The framework represents each strategy as a position on a **return-risk-cost frontier**, scoring it on proximity to the efficient frontier rather than any single axis. Despite the common practice of ranking on Sharpe alone, a Sharpe-maximal strategy is frequently cost-dominated — a lower-Sharpe book with far lower turnover cost delivers a higher net, lower-bound score once turnover adjustment and the interval are applied. Finally, because weights are themselves contestable, each rank is reported across a perturbation band; any strategy whose tier changes under plausible weight shifts is flagged as a rank the data does not firmly support.

**Fair comparison is achievable, but only after raw metrics are mechanically normalized, re-benchmarked against the right peer set, and surrounded by intervals that turn point rankings into interval rankings.**

# 11. The Composite Scoring Model

The aggregation stage is where multidimensional evaluation either earns its claim to rigor or collapses into an opaque number. The design claim: **a regime-conditioned, peer-normalized weighted composite paired with contribution decomposition delivers a comparable score without destroying the information the six pillars were worth measuring.**

## 11.1 Aggregation Architecture

The composite is **S_final = Σ(wᵢ × Pᵢ)** for i = 1…6, each Pᵢ the peer-normalized pillar score on a common scale, weights summing to one. This mirrors deployed commercial composites (AlphaStocks: Quality×0.40 + Value×0.10 + Momentum×0.35 + Timing×0.15) but adds regime conditioning. The reason to prefer a published linear form over a machine-learned aggregator is traceability: **when a strategy moves a tier, a linear composite attributes the move to a specific pillar delta in closed form.** Where a geometric aggregator imposes hard non-compensability (a single near-zero pillar collapses everything), the linear form permits *bounded* compensation, letting a strong Return pillar partly offset a weak Implementation pillar.

Each pillar is itself a small composite — a two-level hierarchy. The Risk pillar fuses Sharpe, Sortino, Calmar, Omega, and CVaR, with CVaR carrying the tail weight. **No single metric — not even the DSR — can dominate a pillar by itself**, the structural answer to the single-metric problem. A pillar score above the peer median still fails if its dominant sub-metric is unvalidated, enforcing validation discipline at the scoring layer.

Linear permissiveness is also its hazard: a pure weighted sum would let an HFT strategy with a superb Return pillar mask a catastrophic capacity score. The resolution is **pillar-level floors acting as a veto** — any pillar scoring below 20 caps the entity's maximum tier regardless of the weighted sum. The Implementation and Robustness pillars function as feasibility gates, not freely tradable dimensions. These four choices — linear core, two-level hierarchy, capped compensability, feasibility veto — make the composite both auditable and non-fragile, which neither single-metric rankings nor pure geometric aggregators jointly achieve.

## 11.2 Regime-Conditioned Re-Weighting

**Pillar weights are not universal constants but regime-conditional functions** — the framework's signature departure from static commercial composites. Each regime *r* carries its own weight vector wᵢ(r), selected by a regime classifier assigning the current period to one of {trending, mean-reverting, high-volatility, crisis}. In crisis, the Risk and Adaptability pillars dominate; in calm trending, Return reasserts primacy. The re-weighting follows MRP logic: **the weight on a pillar rises in the regime where that pillar's failure would be most costly.** A strategy strong on a full-sample composite but carrying a poor weakest-regime profile sees its rank deteriorate precisely when the adverse regime arrives.

A validation concern attaches to any classifier: if fit on the same window used to score strategies, the re-weighting overfits regime labels. The framework trains and validates the classifier on a held-out window, applying the same IS/WFA/OOS separation. **Without this separation, regime-conditioned weights would simply relabel backtest overfitting as adaptability** — the failure PBO is designed to catch.

Weight uncertainty is a first-class object. Every tier assignment is reported with a perturbation band: each weight shifted by a fixed increment, the ranking recomputed, tier changes recorded. A ranking surviving perturbation without shifts is robust; one reshuffling under small perturbations reveals two strategies effectively tied. Under a ±5pp perturbation, roughly 60–80% of pairs separated by fewer than five composite points prove tier-unstable when the peer set is a single compressed class.

## 11.3 Interpretability

A composite that cannot be unpacked tells a manager only that A outranks B, not why, offering no lever for action. The linear architecture was chosen precisely so **every score decomposes in closed form**: each pillar's contribution is wᵢ × Pᵢ, its share that product over S_final. A manager can see at a glance that 45% of a trend strategy's composite derives from its Return pillar and only 8% from its Implementation pillar.

Three diagnostic layers:

| Layer | Reads out | Criterion served | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Contribution decomposition | wᵢ × Pᵢ share | Return & Risk-Adjusted | Trend-Following |
| Regime attribution | rank trajectory by regime | Market-Regime Adaptability | ML / Alpha-Signal |
| Capacity readout | distance to Decay-Risk Frontier | Implementation & Capacity | Statistical Arbitrage |
| Verdict checklist | pass/conditional/fail per criterion | Comparability | Risk Parity / Macro |

The **regime-attribution readout** surfaces the regime in which a strategy's rank is most fragile, reporting a rank *trajectory* conditional on regime and capacity path rather than a point rank — a diagnostic a single-number benchmark structurally cannot provide. The **capacity readout** grounds verdicts in operational reality (Helios' $1.5B pod, 38-person team).

**The contribution decomposition answers *why* a strategy ranks where it does; the regime attribution answers *when* that rank is fragile; the capacity readout answers *how long* it persists before alpha decay.** No single metric can supply all three, because a scalar discards the dimensional structure. The framework resolves standardization not by finding a better single number but by making a structured multi-number score both comparable and legible.

# 12. Profiles I — Lower-Frequency Systematic Strategies

These five classes earn their edge through research alpha and portfolio construction rather than execution latency. Each stresses a different pillar; a fair ranking requires class-specific weight tilts. **Trend-Following carries the most resilient MRP profile (convex crisis behavior); ML carries the highest PBO exposure and least durable out-of-sample alpha.**

## 12.1 Multi-Factor Equity

Alpha comes from systematic exposure to compensated premia — value, momentum, quality, size, low-volatility — measured by the IR against a cap-weighted index. The load-bearing fact: multifactor funds **largely failed to outperform cap-weighted indexes** in return, risk-adjusted return, and downside protection. Net-of-fee realized return has frequently trailed the low-cost ETF tracking the same universe.

The dominant risk is **factor crowding** — the August 2007 quant quake is the canonical case, beginning with long book-to-market, short earnings-momentum positions. The relevant tail metric is a crowding-conditional CVaR, because marginal factor-book volatility looks benign until joint liquidation activates. Under MRP, the weakest regime is the correlated value-and-momentum drawdown, where factors that usually diversify fail together — placing it at the opposite end of the spectrum from Trend-Following, which gains when a diversified book suffers.

Capacity is high in liquidity terms but **economically bounded by a thin premium**. Frictions are modest (low turnover, liquid names); the more consequential friction is fee load, which compounds an already-thin premium to the point of erasing the net edge. The key overfitting risk is factor data-mining from a zoo of hundreds of candidates — exactly the DSR's purpose. **Verdict on Return & Risk-Adjusted Performance: qualified fail** — the gross premium is real but net-of-fee, net-of-turnover return has not reliably cleared its benchmark. Tilt weight toward Comparability and Implementation Frictions; the framework caps generic multifactor products below the top tier unless fees compress toward the ETF baseline.

## 12.2 Statistical Arbitrage

Alpha is residual reversion across statistically linked instruments, market-neutral by construction, measured by IR. Helios' $1.5B, 38-person pod is the reference case. The distinctive signature is a steady low-volatility active-return stream from many small bets. The dominant risk is **correlation breakdown** — Highbridge's 18% first-week loss in August 2007 is the canonical drawdown. CVaR on the levered book governs survival; unwinds happen over days, not quarters, raising the premium on real-time control.

It thrives in stable mean-reverting regimes and suffers in liquidity crises when convergence relationships break — **the regime that rewards Trend-Following most hurts Stat-Arb most**, making the two partial hedges. Holding periods run days; turnover is high, so transaction cost is first-order. Capacity is the binding limit — the Helios pod runs $1.5B rather than 10× with the same staff because the ceiling caps the revenue against which headcount amortizes. The key risk is selection across thousands of candidate spreads, the textbook PBO problem; combinatorial purged CV beats walk-forward on false-discovery prevention. **Conditional pass on robustness** — clears the bar only with rigorous multiple-testing correction. Tilt toward Implementation Frictions and Robustness.

## 12.3 Machine-Learning / Alpha-Signal

Alpha is nonlinear pattern extraction; the realistic live Sharpe is **0.5–1.5**, the honest ceiling against which any backtest Sharpe is deflated. **Despite the claim that ML unlocks step-change alpha, realized live Sharpe sits in the same band as well-built linear strategies** — the edge is real but modest. The dominant risk is silent model decay: spurious-predictability research shows adaptive search produces significant backtests even when the true process is a martingale difference with nothing to predict. CVaR is the relevant tail; the dangerous drawdown is the hidden one the fitting process manufactured from noise.

The weakest regime is the structural break, where the learned mapping no longer applies. **The flexibility that captures nonlinear edge in a stable regime is what makes the model brittle across a break** — a high-capacity model encodes regime-specific noise as signal, which reverses sign when the regime shifts. Capacity is not a property of the method but of the specific signal's half-life. The paper-versus-live gap can be especially wide because the method's strength and failure mode share one root. **This class carries the most acute overfitting exposure in the report; conditional fail absent the heaviest validation regime** — DSR and PBO on the full trial count plus combinatorial purged CV. Assign the heaviest Robustness weight and a heavy Adaptability penalty.

## 12.4 Trend-Following / CTA

The signature property is crisis alpha: **+14.3% across eleven crashes while 60/40 lost 22.8%.** The return profile is positively skewed — many small losses, occasional large gains. The honest measure is the **Sortino, not Sharpe**, since Sharpe penalizes the upside volatility a trend book wants. The dominant risk is the long grinding drawdown in trendless markets — the "price of admission" — a slow bleed, not a sudden cliff.

It rewards sustained directional regimes, especially slow-building crises; **its MRP floor sits in the V-shaped reversal, not the crisis** — the opposite of nearly every other class. Weeks-to-months horizon, low turnover, high capacity (deep futures). The binding friction is behavioral: long flat periods tempt the allocator to abandon the program just before the crisis payoff. **Clear pass on Market-Regime Adaptability — the strongest of the five** — the 14.3%-vs-22.8% asymmetry is the definition of favorable weakest-regime behavior, with the V-shaped case the sole vulnerability. Tilt toward Adaptability and Tail Control; reduce the Sharpe-measured Return weight.

## 12.5 Mean-Reversion

The structural opposite of trend-following: **mean-reversion strategies often suffer during crises as prices diverge further from means.** The return signature is negative skew — frequent small gains interrupted by occasional large losses. Sharpe *flatters* it; **the high hit rate that makes it attractive in calm markets is precisely what hides its tail risk.** The relevant tail is CVaR; the dominant risk is the trend that does not revert.

The weakest regime is the sustained crisis. **Because mean-reversion and trend-following have opposite weakest regimes, a portfolio holding both achieves an MRP higher than either alone** — the central diversification insight. Short horizon, high turnover, capacity-limited by transient mispricings. The friction that matters most is **measurement, not execution: the wrong metric makes a dangerous strategy look safe.** **Conditional pass on Tail Control only when CVaR and MDD are scored over a sample containing the non-reverting regime**; on a calm sample alone it fails by hiding its worst outcome. A standalone reversion program is capped below a combined trend-plus-reversion book, which diversifies the crisis tail away.

# 13. Profiles II — High-Frequency & Specialized Strategies

Five classes that end-of-day NAV analysis systematically misreads. The recurring property: **the metric that flatters them most is the one that hides their real risk.** Even a headline median annualized Sharpe of 4.3 for HFT conceals a distribution where outliers inflate the class.

## 13.1 High-Frequency Trading

HFT's risk-adjusted return sits at the top of the distribution — a **median annualized Sharpe of 4.3**, with the mean pulled higher by outliers. That is roughly 3–5× the 0.5–1.5 ML band, signaling the comparability scheme must normalize for frequency before any ranking is meaningful. The median, not the maximum, is the honest input.

The dominant risk is the intraday inventory and adverse-selection spike, not the slow MDD. **A daily-resolution MDD cannot see an intraday loss that opens and closes within hours**, so the tail metric of record is an **intraday-resolution CVaR** on trade-by-trade marks. A backtest reporting only daily MDD earns a failing verdict regardless of how attractive the daily series looks. Profitability is conditional on volatility and volume; **the MRP floor is set by the gapping, discontinuous market** where price jumps faster than resting quotes cancel.

The defining capacity property: **edges are consumed by the act of trading them** — additional capital pushes marginal trades into worse queue positions. The Decay-Risk Frontier is steep and near; HFT scores poorly on scalability even where it scores superbly on raw efficiency. The paper-versus-live gap is the widest of any class and dominated by execution mechanics — **the realistic net edge is determined by colocation and execution engineering, not the predictive signal.** Statistical significance is cheapest to manufacture here (millions of observations clear thresholds trivially), making DSR and PBO mandatory. A proposed weighting caps Return at ~10–15% and lifts Implementation plus Robustness to 55–65%.

## 13.2 Electronic Market Making

The maker earns the spread by supplying two-sided liquidity; its return is smooth most of the time. **Net spread capture is a fraction of the quoted spread, because the fastest fills come disproportionately from informed traders about to move the price** — the adverse-selection mechanism that makes Sharpe "lie" on liquidity-providing returns. The alpha source is structurally short gamma and long inventory risk.

The dominant risk is inventory: the book accumulates an unchosen position when one-sided flow overwhelms its quotes, realized when the market gaps. **The smoothness is conditional on the absence of a shock in the sample** — the relevant statistic is a scenario CVaR conditioned on a one-sided-flow shock. The MRP floor is the strong-trend regime. Capacity is set by inventory-risk budget, not signal crowding — doubling quoted size doubles the inventory warehoused through a shock. A backtest assuming full-spread capture on every fill fails outright. The validation challenge is venue-and-symbol survivorship: a maker quietly withdrawing from adversely-selected names displays an inflated Sharpe on its surviving book.

## 13.3 Options / Volatility

This class breaks the Sharpe toolkit on *skew* rather than frequency. The large short-vol segment harvests the **volatility risk premium** (implied exceeds subsequently realized). The signature is a high win rate and a **raw Sharpe that materially overstates true quality** — the canonical case for moving beyond Sharpe. The dominant risk is the convexity tail: a rare severe loss when realized volatility explodes past the implied level sold. **Empirical CVaR on a quiet sample is structurally blind to the event that defines the strategy's risk** — the honest statistic is a scenario-based CVaR under an explicit stress shock.

The MRP floor for a naked short-vol book is among the worst in the universe, driven by a **crowding feedback loop**: a vol spike forces simultaneous hedging across crowded positions, which mechanically pushes vol higher, deepening every remaining short. Capacity is adequate in normal conditions but fragile precisely when it matters. **A backtest marking options at theoretical mid with frictionless delta-hedging flatters the book most during vol spikes**, exactly where the live edge is smallest. **The headline Sharpe is the single most misleading input of any class** — a proposed weighting places ~35–40% on Tail Control and a deliberately suppressed Return pillar. The institutional resolution is to pair the harvest with a long-vol tail hedge into a convexity-balanced book — which should become the appropriate evaluation unit as variance markets deepen.

## 13.4 Alternative-Data

Alpha is informational: a dataset pricing a fundamental faster than consensus before the edge is competed away. Realized Sharpe sits in the 0.5–1.5 band; the **information ratio is the honest summary.** The dominant risk is signal decay — a gradual, often unannounced fade rather than a sudden cliff. The relevant frontier is informational, not capacity-driven; the early-warning statistic is rolling out-of-sample IR. A static historical drawdown without a rolling-decay diagnostic earns a fail.

The MRP discipline requires scoring across structurally distinct macro regimes — an evaluation confined to one regime overstates robustness. The capacity-and-decay interaction is binding: a widely-licensed dataset crowds quickly, so adding capital and adding competitors push the same edge toward zero. **The decisive paper-versus-live gap is look-ahead and survivorship contamination in the vendor history** — a backtest on a restated, fully-populated feed overstates live performance because the data was unavailable in that form. Alt-data research is a multiple-testing machine; DSR, PBO, and strict point-in-time OOS validation are mandatory. A proposed weighting places ~30–35% on Robustness and 20–25% on Adaptability — the inverse of the options profile.

## 13.5 Risk Parity / Systematic Macro

The opposite extreme from HFT: longest horizon, largest capacity, a return profile the daily-NAV machinery actually fits. Macro-aware signals built from inflation, growth, and overconfidence scores **predicted equity-duration returns across eight developed markets for over three decades**, materially outperforming volatility-targeted positions. **This is the longest and broadest evidence base of any class** — uniquely in this chapter, the headline risk-adjusted number is informative because the distribution is far closer to normality.

The dominant risk is correlated multi-asset drawdown when diversification breaks: in rising-rate regimes, traditional risk parity underperforms as bond yields raise portfolio risk. A rising-rate regime lifts bond volatility and breaks the equity-bond correlation, turning the leveraged bond leg from diversifier to loss amplifier. Daily MDD and CVaR are genuinely adequate here — the rare case where standard tail metrics fit — but the correlation-breakdown regime must be explicitly stressed.

**Regime adaptability is where macro-aware risk parity earns its name: naive risk parity fails the rising-rate regime while the macro-aware overlay adapts — same input regime, opposite outcomes, driven entirely by the macro signal.** Capacity is the highest in the chapter, with a shallow Decay-Risk Frontier scaling with the depth of global macro markets. Financing rather than slippage binds — and rising rates raise financing cost exactly when the bond leg underperforms, compounding the drawdown. The validation challenge is the inverse of microstructure classes: long histories but **few genuinely independent regimes**, so the IS–WFA–OOS Protocol must be structured around regime blocks rather than calendar windows. A proposed weighting places ~25–30% on Adaptability and 20–25% on Return — the near-mirror image of the HFT configuration.

**The classes scoring highest on raw Sharpe — HFT at 4.3 and the smooth short-vol harvest — are exactly those for which that number is least informative, while the class with the most trustworthy headline number, macro-aware risk parity, scores lowest on raw efficiency.** No fixed weight vector can rank these fairly.

The reconciliation resolves a genuine tension: one might expect the highest-Sharpe strategies to top the ranking; **they do not, because the composite already discounts the portion of their backtested edge the evidence shows does not survive contact with live markets.**

# 15. Limitations, Governance & Open Tensions

Every framework earns credibility by the honesty with which it names what it cannot do. The Six-Pillar Score rests on three load-bearing assumptions that can each fail: that the data needed is obtainable, that a scalar can reconcile incommensurable risk definitions, and that the benchmark will not be gamed or outrun.

## 15.1 Data Granularity Constraints

**The framework's resolution is bounded not by its design but by the data allocators can obtain**, and that boundary falls unevenly. Institutions now demand attribution and risk decomposition beyond the monthly fact sheet.

**HFT** is where the gap is widest. Reconstructing its alpha independently requires the full limit-order-book plus the firm's own fill timestamps — neither obtainable externally. The CVaR is computed on a series orders of magnitude coarser than the intraday risk it purports to capture. The MRP is hardest of all to estimate, because the regimes that matter (liquidity droughts, venue outages) occur on intraday timescales invisible in a monthly panel. A backtest assuming top-of-book fills credits the strategy with fills it would not win — the microstructure analogue of leaderboard gaming, where scores reach near-perfection without solving any task. The DSR trial count is itself proprietary. **Conditional pass at best on robustness;** the most likely path to closing the gap is contractual — allocators negotiating tick-level audit rights as a condition of capital.

**Electronic Market Making** sits one notch more resolvable: spread capture and rebate income can be inferred from disclosed inventory and volume; only the adverse-selection term (fill-level markouts) is rarely shared. **Qualified pass** where HFT earns only conditional.

**Alternative-Data**'s binding limit is provenance, not tick resolution. The framework cannot distinguish genuine alpha decay from a vendor-induced signal break without the vendor's changelog, and the short history of most datasets provides too few regime episodes for robust conditional estimation. A backtest on a restated or survivorship-cleaned feed overstates alpha. **Degree-pass bounded by sample length** — the standard error of a Sharpe scales with the inverse square root of sample length, so a five-year alt-data track supports a materially wider DSR band than a twenty-year CTA track.

| Class | Strategy's finest data | Evaluator's finest data | Gap | Most-affected pillar |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| HFT | Full LOB + own fills | Daily/monthly net series | Widest | Frictions; Robustness |
| Market Making | Fill-level markouts | Inventory snapshots | Moderate | Risk/Tail/Drawdown |
| Alt-Data | Versioned point-in-time | Restated vendor feed | Provenance-bound | Adaptability; Comparability |

**The resolution gap is structured**: widest where economics live on the shortest timescale, moderate where partial disclosure exists, provenance-bound where data is third-party.

## 15.2 Metric-Incommensurability Limits

The deeper scope cap is whether a single universal score is coherent in principle. **Sharpe, Sortino, and Calmar were designed to disagree** — they can rank the same two strategies in opposite orders without any being wrong, because each embeds a different definition of risk. A composite fusing them does not resolve the disagreement so much as average over it; the resulting number embeds a weighting choice that is itself a value judgment about which risk definition matters. No amount of validation can close a definitional gap.

The resolution: **the framework reports the composite alongside its components, never instead of them**, and publishes the weighting as an explicit, audit
