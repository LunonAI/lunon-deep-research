# Enhancing Cascaded PID Attitude Control for UAVs Across Diverse Flight Conditions

A single fixed-gain cascaded PID law cannot deliver envelope-wide attitude performance on a multicopter. The practical fix is not a better controller *class* but a better **gain-selection strategy** layered onto the PID the flight stack already runs. Across the methods surveyed here, the dominant failure mode is identical: gains tuned at a single operating point — typically hover, per PX4 convention — degrade predictably as inertia, battery voltage, and airspeed drift away, producing sluggish tracking under added payload and oscillation under voltage sag.

Despite frequent claims that learned controllers (DRL, MPC, SINDy) have rendered PID obsolete, the 2025 "Leveling the Playing Field" critique shows cross-method comparisons are confounded by task and feed-forward asymmetries, leaving well-augmented PID the defensible production default through 2030 for resource-constrained open-source stacks.

**The verdict: gain scheduling plus a disturbance observer leads for production multicopters today, with offline multi-condition optimization (Bayesian "control-window" tuning) second and safety-constrained RL auto-tuning third.** This report computes that ranking explicitly in Chapter 8 and converts it into a deployable pipeline in Chapter 9. The evidence base spans sixteen-plus primary sources dated 2008–2026, covering production features in INAV, Betaflight, and PX4 alongside the multi-objective optimization literature. The open-source trajectory — exemplified by INAV's airspeed PID attenuation — is that enhancements ship as **bolt-ons to the PID cascade, not replacements**.

# 1 Research Framework, Scope, and Evaluation Rubric

Attitude control here means regulating a vehicle's orientation (roll, pitch, yaw) so it tracks commanded angles and rates; "cascaded" means two nested feedback loops rather than one. This chapter draws the scope boundary, publishes the scoring rubric, and fixes the vocabulary the rest of the report reuses. The ten enhancement methods analyzed are not peers in maturity — some ship in firmware today, others live only in the optimization literature.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

## 1.1 Scope: Cascaded PID on Open-Source Stacks

The bounded object of study is the **two-loop PID cascade (inner rate, outer attitude)** as documented for PX4, INAV, and Betaflight. Everything else enters only as a way to improve, select, or benchmark that cascade's gains.

**Why this boundary holds.** PX4's tuning guidance directs the operator to tune around the hover thrust point, using a thrust-curve parameter to compensate non-linearities and high-thrust oscillations. A procedure anchored to a single thrust condition is the visible symptom of gains tied to one operating point — exactly the behavior later methods aim to generalize. Betaflight ships with Airmode, Antigravity gain, and a dynamic filter on by default, static notches off; these are gain-and-filter *conditioning* features layered onto a PID rate loop, not replacements. Antigravity, a transient gain boost countering throttle-driven dips, is itself a small step toward gain scheduling.

VTOL and fixed-wing stay in scope because the stacks handle them by reorganizing PID gains, not abandoning PID. INAV's VTOL stores separate mixer profiles with associated PID profiles for fixed-wing and multicopter modes, switching by flight mode. **This profile-switching is the clearest production evidence that the multi-regime problem is already being solved by swapping gain sets — structurally gain scheduling by discrete selection.**

**Explicit exclusion.** MPC, LQR, sliding-mode control, and RL appear only as ways to augment, tune, or benchmark the PID cascade — never as the recommended replacement. A rip-and-replace framing would rank MPC first; the augmentation framing ranks methods that keep the deployable, auditable PID core intact. Within this rule, each non-PID paradigm has a defined supporting role: LQR/LQG and H∞ generate *starting gains* (Chapter 4), where the H∞ output is still three PID coefficients computed with a robustness guarantee. INAV 9.0's fixed-wing airspeed PID attenuation (APA) — varying PID gains with airspeed to prevent oscillation while preserving stabilization — is a concrete instance of the augmentation pattern: a regime-aware gain behavior added to the existing loop.

## 1.2 Evaluation Rubric

Six weighted dimensions, weights summing to one, feed the Chapter 8 tier formula:

| ID | Label | Weight |
|---|---|---|
| R-1 | Multi-Regime Robustness | 0.25 |
| R-2 | Disturbance Rejection | 0.20 |
| R-3 | Implementability on Open-Source Controllers | 0.20 |
| R-4 | Stability & Safety Margin | 0.15 |
| R-5 | Tuning Effort & Model Requirement | 0.10 |
| R-6 | Validation Maturity | 0.10 |

**Multi-Regime Robustness carries the heaviest weight (0.25)** — it is the direct answer to the core complaint that one gain set works only under specific conditions. **Implementability (0.20) separates this report from a pure control-theory survey:** a method that cannot run in real time on an MCU-class controller is heavily penalized regardless of theoretical elegance. Robustness and disturbance rejection together (0.45) capture the performance half of the problem; implementability and safety together (0.35) capture deployability.

**Scoring formula.** Each method scores 0–10 per dimension; the final score is the weighted sum:

> S_final = 0.25·R1 + 0.20·R2 + 0.20·R3 + 0.15·R4 + 0.10·R5 + 0.10·R6

Because weights sum to 1.0, S_final stays on the 0–10 scale. A linear sum cannot capture interactions, so a **qualitative safety gate** supplements it: any method scoring below a safety threshold is flagged in Chapter 8 regardless of its weighted sum. The linear choice buys transparency — a reader can recompute any score by hand. This mirrors Kamboj et al. (2026), who formulate PID tuning as constrained optimization where performance objectives and actuator-saturation limits enter the cost function explicitly; the rubric's six items are the objective terms, the safety gate the hard constraint.

## 1.4 Vocabulary and Metrics

**Named terms** (used unmodified downstream): *Cascaded attitude-loop PID* — outer loop converts angle error to a desired rate, inner loop converts rate error to actuator commands. *Inner rate loop* — fast loop stabilizing angular velocity. *Outer attitude loop* — slower loop commanding angle. *Gain scheduling* — storing/interpolating gain sets against a scheduling signal (airspeed, throttle, dynamic pressure). *Auto-tuning* — in-flight identification by excitation. *MRAC* — online parameter adaptation toward a reference model. *Fuzzy PID* — rule-based gain modulation from error/rate. *Flight envelope* — the operating conditions over which one gain set must hold. *Integral windup* — integrator accumulation during saturation, producing post-saturation overshoot. *Disturbance observer* — estimator reconstructing external disturbances and feeding a compensating signal forward.

**Canonical metrics** (six units for every performance claim): **Settling time**, **overshoot**, **tracking error** (often ITAE/ISE), **disturbance rejection**, **stability margin** (gain/phase under model error), and **control effort** (bounded by actuator saturation). The metrics are measurable quantities; the rubric items are weighted evaluation axes that aggregate them with implementability and maturity judgments.

# 2 The Attitude Control Problem and Limits of Fixed-Gain Cascaded PID

The cascaded attitude-loop PID is the de facto control law inside both PX4 and ArduPilot. This chapter specifies the baseline, explains why one gain set is only locally valid, identifies the flight-state signals that break it, and shows how that breakage appears in flight logs.

## 2.1 Cascaded PID Architecture

**Signal flow.** An attitude setpoint is compared against the EKF2-estimated attitude to form an angle error. PX4's architecture is a standard cascaded design of mixed P and PID controllers fed by EKF2. **The outer loop does not command motors directly: it converts angle error into a desired angular rate, which becomes the inner loop's setpoint.** ArduPilot describes the identical division: per axis, a P controller converts angle error to a desired rate, then a PID converts rate error to a motor command.

The **inner rate loop** holds the full three-term controller. PX4 implements this in `mc_att_control_main.cpp`; ArduPilot in `Attitude.cpp`. The inner loop outputs three desired body torques plus a thrust demand, which the **mixer** maps onto individual motors per airframe geometry — a fixed 4×4 matrix for a quad, over-determined for hex/octo, which is why fail-safe schemes can maintain altitude on two or three working propellers by re-allocating demands.

**The timescale separation — inner loop running faster than outer — is the single structural reason the cascade is preferred over a flat controller.** By 2027, any open-source auto-tuning or scheduling feature will almost certainly target the inner rate loop first, since that is where the three-term gains governing disturbance rejection reside.

**Why two stacks converge.** PX4 and ArduPilot adopted the same topology because a nested structure lets one codebase serve manual, assisted, and autonomous modes by switching which loop receives pilot input. Feedforward terms are layered on for setpoint tracking; PX4 exposes feedforward as its own rate-controller parameter. **Feedforward shifts work off the feedback path but leaves the locally-valid nature of the feedback gains intact.** The cascade trades the global stability guarantees of geometric SO(3) controllers for practical tunability, modularity, and MCU-class real-time feasibility.

## 2.2 Why One Gain Set Performs Well Only at One Operating Point

**Causal chain from linearization.** Controller design begins by linearizing the nonlinear rigid-body dynamics about hover. Per a 2024 Uppsala thesis, **linearization neglects higher-order Taylor terms, which become significant as the quadrotor moves away from hover, introducing unmodeled dynamics that destabilize the controller and cause oscillations when position changes**. The thesis observed this for an LQR controller that stabilized hover but oscillated when commanded to a new position.

**This is not a tuning error better parameter selection could fix; it is a structural property of using one linear model to approximate a nonlinear plant across a wide envelope.** The only way to preserve hover performance and extend it off-nominal is to *change the effective model* — by scheduling gains against an envelope signal or adapting online — not by searching harder for a single better set.

**Axis coupling and underactuation.** Open-source cascades tune each axis as an independent SISO channel. Near hover, where body rates are small, this is excellent. But the rigid-body rotational dynamics couple the axes through gyroscopic and inertia-cross terms, so a fast roll induces pitch and yaw moments the per-axis controllers see only as disturbance. **Gains tuned at hover are valid over a narrow thrust band *and* a narrow rate band, and both constraints bind during dynamic flight.** A quadrotor has four actuators but six DOF, so position control is realized through attitude control via the cascade's nesting — which assumes the inner loop is much faster than the outer. Aggressive maneuvers push the attitude loop toward the rate loop's bandwidth and weaken that assumption. Where LQR accounts for coupling through off-diagonal gain terms, the per-axis PID cascade leaves them at zero and relies on each loop's disturbance rejection.

## 2.3 Flight-State Variability That Detunes Fixed Gains

The detuning drivers split cleanly into **slow parametric** and **fast disturbance** classes — a split that determines which remedy can address each.

**Slow drivers.** *Payload/inertia change:* added mass raises the moment of inertia, reducing angular acceleration per torque, so fixed gains yield sluggish tracking (too low) or instability (too high). The Yale study established explicit stability bounds within which grasping an object would not destabilize the aircraft — direct evidence of a finite payload margin. *Battery-voltage sag:* as voltage drops, the same PWM produces less thrust, so fixed gains behave as too-high, causing overshoot and oscillation — the mechanism Betaflight's dynamic sag compensation targets. *Flight-regime transition:* hover, maneuver, and forward flight are distinct operating points; VTOL makes this explicit with a dedicated RC mode switch and stored per-mode PID profiles.

Because these are slow relative to the rate loop, they fall within reach of **scheduling-class methods**. INAV's APA is the cleanest open-source illustration — varying PID gains with airspeed to prevent oscillation while preserving stabilization. A VTOL switch handles the regime transition discretely (simple but transient-prone); airspeed attenuation handles the forward-flight envelope continuously (smooth but requires a reliable airspeed estimate).

**Fast drivers.** *Forward-flight aerodynamics* (the bridge case): airspeed- and angle-of-attack-dependent forces perturb attitude and alter thrust; hover-tuned gains lack rejection for these effects. *Wind/gusts* arrive as transient moments the rate loop must reject directly — outside a scheduler's reach. A **disturbance observer** that estimates the lumped external moment and cancels it at the rate loop is the plausible complement (developed in Chapter 8). *Actuator saturation* is the dominant fast driver because it breaks linearity outright: when commanded torque exceeds motor capability, the mixer clips and the loop stops behaving linearly, producing integral windup. PX4 recommends inspecting motor saturation directly in Flight Review. *Ground effect* near a surface warrants dedicated attitude estimation using motor current rather than hover defaults.

| Driver | Mechanism | Speed | Remedy class |
|---|---|---|---|
| Payload/inertia | Higher inertia lowers angular accel | Slow | Scheduling/adaptive retune |
| Battery sag | Lower voltage cuts torque | Slow | Sag compensation/scheduling |
| Regime transition | Distinct plants | Slow–step | Profile switch/scheduling |
| Forward aero | Airspeed-dependent moments | Slow–medium | Airspeed scheduling |
| Wind/gust | Time-varying external moments | Fast | Disturbance observer at rate loop |
| Saturation | Clipping breaks linearity | Fast | Anti-windup + allocation |
| Ground effect | Near-surface estimation | Situational | Region-specific estimation |

**The slow/fast split is the organizing principle:** scheduling targets the slow column, disturbance-observer/anti-windup machinery targets the fast column, and no single remedy covers both — which is why the ranking weights Multi-Regime Robustness and Disturbance Rejection as separate criteria.

## 2.4 Failure Symptoms

**Under- vs. over-tuned gains.** An under-tuned inner loop tracks sluggishly: the measured rate lags the setpoint, the vehicle feels soft, rejection is weak — diagnosable as a gap between rate setpoint and measured rate in Flight Review. An over-tuned inner loop oscillates; Betaflight describes exactly this for voltage sag. The distinguishing diagnostic is coherence between the rate trace and the motor output — control-law oscillation shows up on the motors, broadband sensor noise need not.

The contrarian point: oscillation and sluggishness are **not always independent failures**. The same fixed gain set can be simultaneously too high for one operating point and too low for another within a single flight — oscillating at the end of a battery pack (sag makes gains effectively high) while tracking sluggishly at the start (heavy payload raises inertia). This is precisely why one gain set cannot satisfy both symptoms at once.

**Integral windup.** Per the libdrone reference, **actuator saturation limits control authority, so error cannot be reduced; the integral accumulates without bound; and when saturation ends, the large integral drives the system past setpoint, producing overshoot or instability**. The worst case is a physically impossible commanded attitude where the integral grows for as long as the command persists. The integral cannot simply be removed — it eliminates steady-state error. **The resolution is to stop integrating while saturated**, retaining steady-state authority without unbounded accumulation, which requires reliable saturation detection — the baseline anti-windup any augmentation builds upon.

| Symptom | Cause | Driver | First-line remedy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rate lags setpoint | Under-tuned loop | Raised inertia | Increase rate gain |
| Ringing on rate and motors | Over-tuned loop | Voltage sag | Reduce rate gain |
| Overshoot after saturation | Integral windup | Saturation | Anti-windup clamp |
| Attitude error builds with airspeed | Missing rejection | Forward aero | Airspeed scheduling |

**Synthesis:** the open-source cascade is locally correct and globally incomplete by the same construction. **No amount of offline parameter search can produce a single gain set satisfying Multi-Regime Robustness, because the limitation is in the fixed-gain assumption, not the search.** What remains quantitatively unresolved is a general map from envelope excursion to margin loss — the identification task Chapter 4's model-based methods and Chapter 6's auto-tuning approach from opposite directions.

# 3 Defining "Optimal": Objectives, Constraints, Trade-Offs

Optimality is not a single number but a negotiated position among competing specifications. This chapter establishes that "optimal" for an open-source flight controller resolves into **four coupled specification families — time-domain response, frequency-domain robustness, control effort, and the multi-objective cost binding them — and that no single gain set is globally optimal across the envelope.**

## 3.1 Time-Domain Objectives

The four canonical metrics: **rise time** (10%–90% climb, indexing agility), **settling time** (entry into a ±2%/±5% band, indexing transient decay), **overshoot** (peak excursion, indexing damping), and **steady-state tracking error** (residual offset). In a cascade these are defined separately on each loop, with the inner loop tuned to settle faster so the outer sees it as near-instantaneous. As general intuition, Kp drives rise time, Kd tempers overshoot, and Ki eliminates steady-state error at the cost of windup risk — the separation PX4's manual procedure follows.

**Target bands.** A well-tuned multirotor rate loop aims for fast, near-zero-overshoot tracking, expressed operationally through log inspection: good rate tracking with unsaturated motors is the acceptance condition. The apparent tension — tuners tolerate small bounded overshoot despite a "no visible overshoot" target — resolves because rejection and rise time both improve with gain, so tuners push to the edge where tracking is crisp and treat "no visible oscillation" as operative. Mission shifts the band: racing quads in Betaflight push toward maximum stiffness; PX4 multirotors prioritize clean unsaturated tracking.

## 3.2 Frequency-Domain and Robustness Constraints

**Gain margin** is the factor loop gain may increase before instability; **phase margin** the tolerable additional lag at crossover; **crossover frequency** sets bandwidth and the agility ceiling. The robust PID literature frames the design task as computing the stabilizing region in (Kp,Ki,Kd) space, which is generally non-convex.

**Causal chain (structural-mode limit):** raising crossover to buy agility passes more high-frequency gyro noise, forcing heavier filtering, which injects phase lag near crossover, which erodes the very phase margin the higher crossover meant to exploit. Betaflight's twin narrow notches with *less delay* are a direct response. Small racing quads push crossover aggressively because resonances sit well above the control bandwidth; large or flexible airframes must respect structural modes near bandwidth, part of why PX4 steers toward the conservative hover point.

**The central trade-off** is that robustness margin and agility are antagonists. Per MathWorks, increasing gain to improve rejection reduces phase margin and increases overshoot; with bandwidth fixed, faster rejection requires steepening the gain slope near crossover. Each link is a single consequence, so no retuning at fixed bandwidth escapes it. A fixed-margin tune holds margins only within the narrow envelope where the plant resembles its design model and fails Multi-Regime Robustness for any airframe whose safe crossover shifts — the fixed-wing airspeed case INAV's APA addresses.

## 3.3 Effort, Energy, and Saturation Constraints

**Control effort** is actuator command magnitude/rate; **saturation** is when the demand exceeds capability, opening the loop and lapsing tracking/stability guarantees. A tracking-only cost returns gains commanding more authority than the airframe possesses, which is why Kamboj et al. write saturation limits into the cost function alongside performance objectives. Their pipeline — embed objectives and safety, run surrogate-based clustering optimization, reduce simulations, yield constraint-satisfying gains — is the cleanest statement that effort limits belong *inside* the cost. The constraint bites hardest at the envelope edge: a quad flown on two opposing propellers has far less margin before saturation, invalidating any tune assuming full authority.

**Noise sensitivity** is driven by the derivative term, making noise rejection a competing objective against overshoot suppression. Differentiation amplifies high-frequency content, so large Kd on a noisy gyro produces a noise-dominated command — hence Betaflight's heavy filtering investment around the derivative path. Fuzzy PID offers a route around the conflict: a 25-rule fuzzy base could lower Kd when error is small and noise dominates, raising it only during large transients. The residual cost is complexity.

## 3.4 Multi-Objective Formulation

**Integral error criteria.** ISE penalizes large early errors and tolerates small lingering ones; IAE weights error equally; ITAE multiplies error by elapsed time, tolerating the unavoidable step-instant error but punishing slow settling — the criterion most aligned with a fast, well-damped attitude response. Puangdownreong et al. optimized PID gains via multipath adaptive tabu search under ITAE, overshoot, and settling-time objectives with actuator constraints. Scalarization (e.g., Altınöz's weighted active-suspension objective) is cheaper and returns one answer but hides the trade surface; the Pareto route preserves it at higher cost.

**The Pareto front** — gain configurations where no objective improves without worsening another — is the formal reason no single gain set is globally optimal. Reynoso-Meza et al. build their framework around inspecting it: define objectives, generate the Pareto set, inspect the front to trade performance against robustness, select. A 2013 turbine study simultaneously optimized setpoint tracking, disturbance attenuation, and robust stability, then ranked Pareto solutions; Huang, Wang & Zhao used a tissue-P-systems multi-objective algorithm to lower complexity.

**Contrarian framing:** despite the belief that a careful tuner can find "the best gains," Hast, Åström, Bernhardsson & Boyd optimize performance *subject to* robustness constraints via convex-concave programming — the very structure concedes that tightening robustness costs performance. "Best gains" is not a point but a mission-dependent choice along a front. Across the surveyed studies the objective count clusters at three to four; for the attitude problem it is agility, robustness, and noise, with actuator effort the near-universal fourth.

| Cost component | Family | Indexed metric | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| ITAE/IAE/ISE tracking | Time-domain | Rise/settling/overshoot | Puangdownreong |
| Sensitivity bound | Frequency | Gain/phase margin | Hast et al. |
| Saturation constraint | Effort | Control effort | Kamboj |
| Derivative-noise penalty | Effort/noise | Noise sensitivity | Betaflight |
| Pareto-rank selection | Cross-family | All of the above | Reynoso-Meza |

**Synthesis:** "optimal" for attitude-loop PID is a *selected position on a Pareto front*, because agility, robustness, and noise rejection are provably antagonistic. This reframes the prompt from "how do I find the optimal gains" to "against which weighted cost, under which constraints, for which mission." The per-mission weights encode a value judgment the control theory cannot supply alone — the recommendation pipeline (Chapter 9) must provide them explicitly.

# 4 Classical and Model-Based Offline Parameter Selection

Offline selection fixes a gain set *before* the aircraft flies that configuration. The defining property: every technique returns a **single gain triple** intended to hold across some declared operating region. The central question is therefore "how wide a slice of the envelope can one fixed slice cover, and at what cost in agility." **Verdict: classical heuristics give a cheap, model-free starting point that fails Multi-Regime Robustness; LQR/LQG and H∞ buy genuine envelope-wide stability at the price of a plant model and peak agility; none alone resolves the multi-regime problem.**

## 4.1 Ziegler–Nichols and Heuristic Variants

The ultimate-gain procedure: raise the rate-loop proportional term until a steady oscillation, record the critical gain and period, then read back-off factors from a conversion table. It is **model-free** — it infers just enough phase behavior at the stability boundary to place gains without writing the equations of motion. Lee's quadrotor adaptation runs the experiment twice — empty and full payload — retaining two gain tables because added mass moves the critical gain materially. This spirit survives in PX4's manual workflow, which raises each rate gain until oscillation then reduces it, framed explicitly as the fallback "when tuning around the hover thrust point is not sufficient".

**Why it cannot cover more than one point.** The single experiment characterizes the plant at one operating point and silently assumes it represents the envelope. The moment the vehicle enters fast forward flight, descends into downwash, or picks up payload, the plant shifts off-design. A fixed inner-loop gain sees a different effective plant gain once the aircraft loads up, shifting crossover, eroding the phase margin the back-off factor guaranteed, surfacing as overshoot or limit-cycle oscillation diagnosed in Flight Review. **Heuristic tuning earns a clear pass on Implementability — it is the method the stacks document first — but a clear fail on Multi-Regime Robustness, functioning as a seed for the auto-tuner or scheduler.**

## 4.2 System Identification as Prerequisite

Every model-based method must first fit a plant model from input/output data. The cascade is implemented per axis, so identification reduces to single-axis problems: the map from commanded torque to measured body rate, which on a symmetric X-quad is a low-order model dominated by axis inertia and motor/ESC lag. The data comes from the same Flight Review logs operators use for diagnosis — commanded rate as input, measured rate as output, with motor-saturation flags marking samples to exclude. The PX4 rate controller publishes exactly the rate setpoint and error that define the plant.

**The model-fidelity limitation undermines everything built on it.** The EPFL modifier-adaptation work states it generally: model-based optimization suffers from limited model accuracy, and when plant quantities are not known accurately, tuning yields suboptimal or even infeasible settings. For a quadrotor, axis inertia changes with every payload swap, motor lag drifts with temperature and voltage, and ground-effect aerodynamics are rarely identified at all. An inaccurate inertia estimate produces a gain optimal for the wrong plant, shifting real crossover, shrinking realized phase margin, pushing an aggressive loop into a limit cycle. **The resolution is not to abandon the model but to stop trusting it as exact:** robust synthesis keeps the model and budgets for its error; online adaptation (Chapter 8) re-estimates in flight.

## 4.3 LQR/LQG-Optimal Pre-Tuning

LQR casts gain selection as minimizing a quadratic cost trading state regulation against control effort. The construction: augment the plant state with integrators (which produces the integral term), define a quadratic cost, solve for the optimal feedback gain K, and map it to PID terms via an output-feedback transformation. **The weighting matrices, not the gains, become the design knobs** — the operator specifies the relative cost of attitude error versus actuator effort instead of guessing three numbers. When not all states are measured, the formulation extends to LQG via a Kalman estimator. For a quadrotor, gyro-measured body rates make the inner loop near-full-state (LQR), while the outer loop relies on the EKF2 attitude estimate, making it LQG-flavored.

**Validity region.** An LQR pre-tune is optimal for one trim; the question is how far it remains acceptable. Rate-loop dynamics (inertia, lag) change slowly across multicopter flight compared with the airspeed-dependent aerodynamic terms that force fixed-wing scheduling — **so one hover-trim LQR gain covers a usefully wide multicopter region it would not cover on fixed-wing.** This asymmetry is visible in firmware: PX4 multicopters tune around hover with a static thrust curve, while INAV 9.0 fixed-wing varies gains with airspeed. Because the LQG observer adds phase lag consuming margin, the estimator-dependent outer loop fails first as the envelope widens — the inner rate loop tolerates wider excursion.

## 4.4 Robust Synthesis: H∞ and Loop-Shaping

H∞ treats the identified model as nominal and budgets for its error, producing one fixed controller guaranteed stable across a declared uncertainty set. Per MathWorks, high loop gains attenuate uncertainty and reduce sensitivity, but if uncertainty is so large the sign of the plant gain is unknown, large gains risk instability. **That sentence is the entire design tension: robustness is bought by limiting gain exactly where the plant is least known.** Rather than demand an exact model, H∞ asks only for a *bound* on the error and returns a gain stable everywhere inside it.

The theory: for a finite set of LTI plants, one can compute robustly stabilizing regions in (Kp,Ki,Kd) space, and these regions can be **non-convex** — making the fixed gain a genuine synthesis problem, and motivating the metaheuristic search of Chapter 5. A quad's empty, loaded, and ground-effect plants enumerate as a finite set; the robust region is their intersection. **LQR and H∞ are duals:** LQR asks "best performance at this plant," H∞ asks "acceptable performance across all plausible plants." On Multi-Regime Robustness, H∞ earns the strongest pass of any offline method.

**Loop-shaping** turns the abstract objective into one controller by shaping the open-loop response — high gain where uncertainty is low for performance, reduced gain where uncertainty is high for stability, keeping the loop clear of the instability boundary. The output, constrained to PID form, is one fixed triple per axis covering an *explicit* designed-in range. The stacks do not yet expose this natively, so it arrives as a fixed parameter file. Where the uncertainty bound is wide (mode transitions), the shaped loop is forced conservative — which is exactly where the stacks stop trusting one gain and switch to per-mode profiles.

**The trade-off:** a gain stable across the whole plant set cannot be the highest-bandwidth gain at any one plant — the robust gain is *deliberately detuned* relative to the per-plant optimum, a fundamental limit imposed by the uncertainty set's width, not a tuning imperfection. This separates use cases: racing quads want the per-plant LQR optimum and peak bandwidth (Betaflight's aggressive defaults); survey/delivery multicopters want the robust fixed gain and accept softer response. Robust synthesis and aggressive-with-filtering are two routes to the same stability goal, differing in where they spend the agility budget.

**Synthesis:** every offline technique buys multi-regime coverage only by bracketing operating points (the heuristic's two tables) or budgeting for variation in advance (robust synthesis), and **neither adapts once airborne** — the precise gap the online families address.

| Method | Model req. | Coverage | Stability margin | Implementability | Best-fit |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Heuristic (Z–N) | None | Single point | Empirical only | Native | Narrow-envelope first pass |
| LQR/LQG | Full linear | Single trim | Nominal optimum | Offline | Well-modeled, hover-dominated |
| H∞/loop-shaping | Model + bound | Bounded set | Envelope-wide guarantee | Offline tool | Safety-critical, wide variation |

# 5 Optimization-Based Parameter Selection

Optimization treats gain selection as a formal search over a cost landscape evaluated on simulated or physical flights. The defining question across this family is **how many cost evaluations each method consumes and whether the gains generalize beyond their single tuning point.** **Central finding: metaheuristic search (GA, PSO) buys model-light global exploration at the price of thousands of cheap simulations; Bayesian optimization inverts that economics for expensive real-flight trials; and multi-operating-point formulation is the structural defense against overfitting any of the three can adopt.**

## 5.1 Genetic Algorithms

A GA evolves a population of candidate gain vectors through selection, crossover, and mutation, scoring each by simulating the closed loop and measuring integral error. No gradient, no analytic model — only a simulator returning a cost. A 2007 comparative study and a separate random-search study established that both GA and PSO yield better PID performance than Ziegler–Nichols on the same plant. ITAE drives the search toward fast settling; IAE tolerates a low-amplitude tail — the same machinery yields materially different gains by cost choice.

**The economics hinge on evaluation count.** A 50-candidate population over 100 generations is 5,000 evaluations — cheap at milliseconds each on a workstation, prohibitive as real flights. The apparent paradox (you turn to a GA to avoid modeling, but it demands thousands of model evaluations) resolves because evaluations need only a black-box *simulator*, not an analytic model — effort shifts from human derivation to automated computation. The constraint: if the simulator misrepresents the plant, the GA faithfully optimizes the wrong cost.

A plain single-point GA covers exactly one operating point — its most consequential limitation. The named risk is that the GA optimizes only the cost it is given: without a margin penalty, it can converge on aggressive gains minimizing tracking error while sitting near the stability boundary. The GA runs **entirely offline**; the product is a static gain set loaded into the existing cascade, so runtime feasibility is a non-issue. On Validation Maturity it earns a partial pass — simulation-level advantage over Z–N, but simulation-bounded. By 2026, Kamboj et al.'s constrained formulation embedding saturation limits points toward surrogate-assisted GA variants that preserve model-lightness while cutting evaluation count.

## 5.2 Particle Swarm and Swarm Methods

PSO replaces evolutionary operators with a velocity-and-position update pulling each particle toward its personal best and the swarm's global best. It occupies the same matrix cell as the GA — model-light, population-based, simulation-scored — but differs in convergence character.

**PSO vs. GA** is the cleanest named-alternative contrast: both minimize the same cost with different machinery. PSO's velocity update contracts the swarm toward the incumbent best quickly, giving fast initial convergence but risking **premature convergence** on a local optimum. The GA maintains diversity longer, trading early speed for sustained coverage. PSO also has fewer hyperparameters — inertia weight plus two acceleration coefficients, versus the GA's population size, crossover rate, mutation rate, and selection scheme. For a six-to-nine-gain cascade tuning problem, a 20–40-particle swarm typically reaches a usable set in 20–50 iterations (400–2,000 evaluations), modestly below a GA.

**The decisive shared risk — and the one the prompt names — is single-point overfitting.** A more powerful optimizer that drives cost lower on the tuning maneuver can produce gains that generalize *worse*, because squeezing out the last fraction of error means exploiting point-specific dynamics. The fix is not a weaker optimizer but a different cost: optimizing against a battery of conditions removes the incentive to specialize. PSO's fast convergence aggravates this — it can lock onto a single-point basin before exploring a more robust one.

**Synthesis (metaheuristic family):** the GA and PSO are interchangeable on model requirement and deployment cost, differ only in convergence character, and **share one decisive weakness — single-point overfit — that no search-algorithm cleverness resolves**, because it is a property of the *cost formulation*. Both fail Multi-Regime Robustness identically in plain single-point form.

## 5.3 Bayesian Optimization and Sample Efficiency

BO is the sample-efficient member, built for when each evaluation is an expensive real bench or flight trial. It builds a probabilistic **surrogate** of the cost across gain space from the handful of trials so far, and an acquisition function picks the next maximally-informative gain vector. Because each trial extracts the most information, BO reaches a good set in **an order of magnitude or more fewer evaluations** than the metaheuristics — converging in roughly 20–50 real trials where a GA needs hundreds to thousands.

The metaheuristics are cheapest when evaluations are simulation-cheap and plentiful; **BO is the method of choice when evaluations are real, scarce, and costly** — a satellite reaction-wheel HIL platform or a ground-effect bench rig. This connects to the sim-to-real literature: Simulation Distillation achieves substantial gains with only 15–30 minutes of real data; real-is-sim uses a 60 Hz digital twin to mediate between policy and robot. Real data is the scarce resource, and methods respecting its scarcity win on hardware. MetaTune illustrates the trajectory: adjoint-based meta-tuning reduces gradient time by over 50% while improving quadrotor performance.

BO is **model-free in the plant sense** — it builds a surrogate of the *cost*, not the vehicle — the most favorable posture for a team that cannot derive dynamics or afford a high-fidelity simulator. The named drawback is **dimensionality**: surrogates struggle as the gain vector grows past roughly ten jointly-tuned parameters, at which point a decomposition into per-loop BO runs is the better structure. Surrogate computation runs offline between flights; onboard runtime cost is zero. On Validation Maturity in the flight-test setting, BO earns the most favorable verdict of the three families, because its sample efficiency makes real-hardware campaigns practical.

## 5.4 Multi-Operating-Point Optimization for Generalization

The structural answer to single-point overfit: score each candidate against a **battery of conditions** spanning the envelope, rewarding broad competence over specialization. The 2025 BO work states the principle directly — optimizing one gain set across multiple operating points finds parameters minimizing *expected error over the entire envelope* rather than excelling at one point. Kamboj et al. extend it with an explicit pipeline: embed objectives and safety, cluster-search the parameter space, reduce simulations, yield constraint-satisfying gains. The **process-window analogy** makes it concrete: just as a semiconductor process window defines stable manufacturing conditions, optimizing across flight conditions identifies a *control window* where performance stays acceptable.

**The trade-off:** the resulting gain set is best-at-none — beaten at any single point by a point-specific tune. The naive reading is that this compromise is strictly worse; the actual resolution is the opposite, because the point-specific optimum degrades sharply outside its basin while the compromise holds steady. The cause is error-surface asymmetry — specialized optima sit on narrow peaks, compromise optima on broad plateaus, and real flight visits the whole landscape.

**Data-driven gain maps.** The more ambitious move: run optimization separately at each operating point and store the gains as a function of the scheduling signal, converting a battery of optima into a lookup structure indexed by airspeed, throttle, or dynamic pressure — the raw material for Chapter 7's gain scheduling. **The sim-to-real warning is a genuine contrarian result:** the intuition that stiffer gains transfer best is *wrong* — behavior cloning benefits from compliant/overdamped regimes, while sim-to-real transfer is *harmed* by stiff/overdamped regimes. A map optimized purely in simulation may encode exactly the stiff regimes that transfer worst, so it must be built with a transfer-aware cost penalizing stiffness.

**The two-stage pipeline (the chapter's actionable contribution):** first, a multi-condition optimization (GA/PSO/BO) builds a simulation-grounded gain map indexed by the scheduling signal; second, a short real-trial refinement using BO's sample efficiency and a transfer-aware cost corrects the stiff-regime bias. A refinement budget of 15–30 minutes of flight data is an order of magnitude below tuning each point from scratch — economically viable for a small team. Chapter 9's pipeline builds on this structure.

**Synthesis:** the family is defined not by its search algorithms but by **two orthogonal choices — the evaluation economy (cheap simulations favor GA/PSO, expensive real trials favor BO) and the cost structure (single-point overfits, multi-point generalizes).** The single-point overfit risk is a cost-formulation defect, not an algorithm defect, and the multi-operating-point formulation is the shared fix. Optimization does not compete with gain scheduling or adaptive control so much as **feed them:** multi-condition optimization fills the gain map scheduling interpolates.

| Family | Evaluation economy | Typical budget | Model need | Single-point overfit | Best-fit |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| GA | Cheap simulations | Thousands of sim evals | Simulator | High (plain) | Sim-rich, no analytic model |
| PSO | Cheap simulations | 400–2,000 sim evals | Simulator | High (aggravated by speed) | Known point, setup simplicity |
| BO | Expensive real trials | 20–50 real trials | Cost surrogate | Low (multi-cost affordable) | HIL/flight-test campaigns |

# 6 In-Flight Auto-Tuning on Open-Source Flight Stacks

In-flight auto-tuning produces gains *during* a supervised sortie — the one parameter-selection family the firmware executes on the flight controller itself. PX4 calls Autotune "intuitive, easy, and fast" and sufficient for many vehicles around the hover thrust point, yet ArduPilot attaches a blunt warning: AutoTune may produce gains leading to **an unflyable vehicle that may crash**. **The organizing theme: in-flight auto-tuning buys per-airframe convenience at the cost of single-condition identification and a genuine crash exposure — and the right response is to bound both.**

## 6.1 How AutoTune Works

**Hover-point excitation.** PX4 recommends Autotune specifically for tuning around the hover thrust point, using the thrust-curve parameter to compensate non-linearities. The excitation acts on the published cascade — a P stage on angle error feeding a PID stage on rate error. The architecture nests a fast inner rate controller inside a slower outer angle controller, making the rate loop the stage tuned first and the natural target of excitation-based identification, since its response is what a controlled perturbation most directly reveals. The single-point scope is a property of the documented workflow — any whole-envelope extension would need to repeat identification at additional points or defer to scheduling (Chapter 7).

**Outputs.** Per axis, the routine writes the **rate P, I, D gains** (inner loop) and the **attitude P gain** (outer loop). These are inspectable parameters: the controllers live in open source — `mc_att_control_main.cpp` (PX4), `Attitude.cpp` (ArduPilot) — and ArduPilot's modular structure even accepts a replacement controller. The inner-loop rate gains fix the fast dynamics and constrain how aggressively the outer attitude P can be set; an outer loop commanding rates faster than the inner can deliver produces cross-loop oscillation. **On Validation Maturity this is a clear pass:** tune quality is checkable from standard logs showing angle/rate tracking and motor saturation.

| Cascade stage | Controller | Gains | Role in identification |
|---|---|---|---|
| Inner rate loop | PID (rate→motor) | Rate P, I, D per axis | Loop most directly probed by excitation |
| Outer attitude loop | P (angle→rate) | Attitude P per axis | Slower loop, set consistent with inner bandwidth |

## 6.2 Safety and Practicality Constraints

**The crash warning** is the single most important caveat in the workflow: the firmware itself warns the routine can produce gains rendering the aircraft uncontrollable. The resolution is **procedural, not algorithmic.** PX4 requires the airframe to already fly well enough to handle moderate disturbances, to be closely attended with the operator ready to abort, and to pass a pre-flight stability test. The safety margin lives in the operating procedure, presupposing an attentive human. On Stability & Safety Margin, AutoTune earns only a **conditional pass** — the margin is not guaranteed by the algorithm but supplied by supervised operation. This contrasts with stability-preserving RL tuning (Chapter 8), where constraints keep gains inside the stabilizing region *by construction*.

**Operating preconditions.** Excitation in poorly controlled air injects disturbance energy that conflates the airframe's dynamics with the disturbance, biasing the identified gains — which is why the vehicle must already reject disturbance before the tune starts. The abort path must revert to a **known-good configuration**, not the experimental gains being identified. The contrarian point: "closely attended" is a stronger burden than "intuitive, easy, and fast" implies — the ease applies to setup cognitive load, not the supervision burden during the run.

## 6.3 Auto-Tuning vs. Offline Tuning Trade-Off

Rendering the single entity **In-Flight Auto-Tune** against the seven dimensions:

- **Mechanism:** identify-then-write-back, scoped to one operating condition.
- **Coverage:** single operating point by construction — firmware's maintenance of per-mode profiles is practical confirmation that one hover tune does not span a wide envelope.
- **Model requirement:** none supplied by the operator — the sharpest contrast with the model-based families of Chapter 4. The cost moves from desk to field but does not vanish.
- **Strengths:** speed, simplicity, and verifiability from Flight Review.
- **Limitations:** the documented crash risk compounded by single-condition scope. **On Multi-Regime Robustness, AutoTune alone fails for wide-envelope vehicles and passes only where the mission stays near the hover point it identifies.**
- **Cost:** native to both stacks, one supervised flight, no MCU burden beyond the existing cascade.
- **Best-fit:** a new multicopter centered on hover, flown by an attentive operator — or, for wide-envelope platforms, AutoTune as the **hover-anchor step feeding the scheduling layer** of Chapter 7.

**Seeding from a robust offline gain set** resolves the tension. PX4's pre-flyable requirement means a conservative starting set is a precondition, so a robust offline set serves two roles at once: it satisfies the pre-flyable requirement *and* provides the abort fallback. It is also a quality input — a vehicle already rejecting disturbance yields a cleaner excitation response, less biased by wind. The contrast with learning-based tuning sharpens the trade: an RL method predicting PD gains achieved the smallest attitude errors versus hand-tuning, but the seeded offline-then-AutoTune workflow occupies the middle ground — a principled starting point plus data-driven refinement, every gain readable as a standard parameter.

| Property | Offline tuning | In-flight AutoTune | Offline-seeded AutoTune |
|---|---|---|---|
| Model requirement | Identified plant | None | Conservative seed |
| Coverage | Per design point | Hover only | Hover node for schedule |
| Crash exposure | Low | Documented warning | Mitigated by fallback |
| Setup speed | Slow | Fast | Moderate |
| Field supervision | None | Mandatory | Mandatory |

**Synthesis:** AutoTune and offline tuning are complements, not substitutes — the robust workflow seeds the in-flight routine from an offline set satisfying both the pre-flyable precondition and the abort fallback, treating the hover result as **one node of a scheduled controller rather than a whole-envelope solution.** What remains unresolved is quantitative: neither stack publishes a failure rate for AutoTune runs, the input the tier ranking must otherwise estimate from the qualitative warning. (Even limited hyperparameter tuning of an auto-tuner improves it by 94.8% on average, underscoring that excitation settings are themselves consequential design choices.)

# 7 Gain Scheduling Across Flight Conditions

Gain scheduling stores several locally-tuned gain sets and selects or blends them by a measured scheduling signal. It occupies the **middle** between auto-tuning (one set, fast) and adaptive control (continuous online recompute): it pre-computes a discrete map offline and indexes it in real time. INAV 9.0's airspeed PID attenuation and INAV/PX4 VTOL profile switching are the shipping open-source evidence.

## 7.1 Operating-Region Framework

**Scheduling signals.** A signal is useful when it indexes a change in plant dynamics the attitude loop would otherwise see as disturbance. **Airspeed has a shipping implementation** — INAV's APA attenuates gain as airspeed climbs, since control surfaces grow more effective and a low-speed tune over-actuates at high speed. *Payload state* indexes mass/inertia change; the same profile infrastructure could in principle hold loaded/empty sets, though INAV's documented switching is per-mode, not per-payload. *Battery voltage* indexes falling thrust-per-command; PX4's thrust-curve is a static feedforward correction rather than a voltage schedule. *VTOL transition phase* is the most demanding — the plant changes *which effectors command attitude*. **Among these, airspeed and VTOL mode have shipping implementations; payload and voltage remain feedforward corrections or plausible reuses.**

**Partitioning.** The governing tension is resolution versus cost: more regions track dynamics more faithfully but multiply gain sets to tune and store. **The binding constraint is tuning labor, not memory** — each boundary is another operating point a human or autotune must stabilize, bounding region count far below the storage ceiling. INAV's airspeed APA is continuous over a scalar; VTOL uses two profiles. A pure multicopter near hover is a one-region schedule. Boundaries should fall where dynamics change fastest. Against Multi-Regime Robustness, partitioning passes where a single optimized set fails — it is the structural answer, provided boundaries sit where dynamics actually shift.

## 7.2 Interpolation and Switching with Stability

The two transition laws: **gain interpolation** (continuous blend) and **hard switching** (jump at a boundary). The NTNU dwell-time study states the mechanism: interpolating produces smooth transitions and continuity of commanded torque, while a minimum dwell time lets the state converge under the active controller, preventing the instability discontinuous or too-frequent changes excite. Two distinct links — smoothing removes the command discontinuity, dwell-time enforcement removes chattering when the signal dithers across a boundary.

The practical reading: the scheduling signal must evolve slowly relative to the inner loop's settling time, and airspeed, payload, and voltage all satisfy this by wide margins — the VTOL corridor stresses it most. Hard switching retains one advantage: it is what shipping VTOL firmware does, adequate when the aircraft transitions quickly and barely occupies the corridor. Against Stability & Safety Margin, interpolation with dwell-time enforcement passes where naked hard switching is conditional — the dwell-time condition is a checkable certificate.

**Parameter-dependent stability.** A schedule stable at every frozen point is *not* automatically stable while the point moves. The robust PID result that stabilizing regions are **non-convex** justifies cautious interpolation: a straight-line path between two individually-stable anchors is not guaranteed to stay inside the stabilizing set — it can pass through an unstable pocket. This is precisely why dwell-time and slow-variation conditions matter: they bound how fast the interpolated point traverses any pocket. **Parameter-dependent quadratic stability** — letting the Lyapunov function depend on the scheduling parameter — recovers margin a fixed certificate loses. For an implementer, the non-convexity result supplies the warning while the dwell-time condition supplies the cheap, checkable guard without an onboard Lyapunov solve.

## 7.3 VTOL Transition: The Canonical Case

In hover, a VTOL's attitude is commanded by rotor thrust differentials; in forward flight, aerodynamic surfaces take over. **The corridor between is where a single gain set is least defensible, because the effectors commanding attitude are themselves changing authority.** The MATLAB/Simulink VTOL example documents the resulting difficulty: in hover, the yaw and horizontal-position controllers can interfere, causing instability the gain-scheduled tuner mitigates. A two-profile hard switch assigns *no gain to the middle of the corridor*, assuming fast passage; this fails for aircraft that must loiter or retain an abort option, where a continuously-varying schedule becomes necessary.

The VTOL corridor is strictly harder than airspeed scheduling because the variable is not a scalar scaling one plant — it is a *phase variable re-weighting two effector sets*. Building a corridor schedule means a gain set at each of several transition points; the practical way is to repeat single-point autotune at each. **The schedule is a sequence of autotune results stitched by the interpolation law** — autotune and scheduling are complementary, not competing. The natural evolution is an autotune routine that sweeps the corridor in one guided flight, emitting a populated schedule directly.

## 7.4 Implementing Gain Maps on Flight Controllers

The cascade executes at loop rate, so any per-cycle lookup and interpolation must fit the PID cycle budget. **A gain-table lookup with linear interpolation is arithmetically trivial against the PID update it feeds** — a bounded-index lookup plus a few multiply-adds, which is why linear interpolation is the recommended choice over higher-order schemes that risk overshooting between anchors. **Memory is rarely binding:** a two-axis schedule at 3–5 cells per axis implies 9–25 gain sets, a few hundred floats — comfortably within STM32-class budgets (an illustrative estimate, not a measured figure). The real binding constraint is again **tuning labor** — every stored set is an operating point to validate. Against Implementability, a linearly-interpolated gain table passes cleanly. Compared with online adaptive schemes that recompute every cycle, **a pre-computed map moves essentially all cost offline** — the structural reason scheduling reaches shipping firmware more readily than heavier adaptive methods.

**Failure mode: transient detune from schedule gaps.** A gap — a stretch of the scheduling variable with no nearby anchor — leaves interpolation extrapolating from distant cells, producing a mismatched gain set felt as sloppy or oscillatory handling. The two-profile VTOL switch is the extreme: the entire corridor is a gap, and the MATLAB/Simulink yaw/position interference is the kind of instability a gap exposes. The resolution is not uniform gap elimination but **concentrating anchors where dynamics change fastest and tolerating gaps where they change slowly**, safe because the slow-variation condition guarantees a sparse region traversed slowly stays within the active set's envelope. A second guard is **integral-state continuity across a switch** — scaling or carrying the integral accumulator so the integral contribution does not kick.

**Synthesis:** gain scheduling is **a scaffold** — a partition whose cells are filled by the offline and auto-tuning methods of Chapters 4–6, connected by an interpolation-plus-dwell-time law stressed hardest by the VTOL corridor and **bounded in practice by tuning labor, not memory or cycles.** INAV's APA and VTOL profile switching are shipping proof the scaffold fits real firmware. What remains open: whether a *continuous, multi-anchor corridor* schedule (versus the two-profile switch) has been flight-validated on any open-source VTOL — the gap between the MATLAB/Simulink simulation and shipping firmware.

| Decision | Shipping evidence | Simpler option | More capable | Binding constraint |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Scheduling signal | Airspeed APA; VTOL mode | Thrust-curve feedforward | Continuous airspeed schedule | Signal estimation quality |
| Region count | Two VTOL profiles | One region (hover tune) | Multi-anchor corridor grid | Tuning/validation labor |
| Transition law | Hard switch | Hard switch (fast-pass) | Interpolation + dwell time | Non-convex stabilizing region |
| Onboard cost | Lookup + linear interp | Single fixed set | Pre-computed map | Cycle budget |

# 8 Adaptive and Intelligent PID Augmentation

Online parameter-adapting control changes its own gains *while flying*, in response to the error it observes. **The analytical thread: adaptation and learning buy coverage of conditions the schedule designer never enumerated, at the cost of the stability certificate a frozen, well-tuned cascade enjoys by construction.**

## 8.1 MRAC and L1 Adaptive Augmentation

MRAC continuously adjusts controller parameters to minimize the error between the actual system and a chosen reference model. The adaptation is a closed loop: compare actual to reference output, compute a parameter update minimizing tracking error, adjust gains in real time — maintaining stability and tracking despite payload variation.

- **Coverage:** the defining advantage over scheduling — MRAC reacts to whatever discrepancy appears between plant and reference, addressing conditions not enumerated at design time.
- **Model requirement:** a *reference model* (desired response), not a fully identified plant — the adaptation law absorbs the mismatch.
- **Strengths:** maintains tracking performance where fixed-gain controllers degrade, tied directly to payload drift.
- **Limitations:** the Lyapunov-based law guarantees the tracking error converges and the loop stays stable, **but the guarantee is asymptotic** — it certifies convergence, not the parameter trajectory's shape. A falsifier: if a rapid payload change drives the adapted gains through a transient that saturates actuators *before* convergence, the asymptotic guarantee is insufficient.
- **Cost:** a small set of differential equations alongside the cascade, well within MCU reach. The real cost is verification — certifying the adapted parameters remain auditable.
- **Best-fit:** missions with a known, large, discrete parameter change — delivery quads releasing payloads. Where scheduling handles a *measured* envelope variable, MRAC fits when the disturbing variable shows up only as tracking error.

**L1 as the bounded-transient variant.** The Lyapunov certificate's asymptotic nature is its practical weakness — a controller provably stable "eventually" can still command an aggressive transient that matters more on a saturating airframe. L1 **decouples the speed of adaptation from the robustness of the command**: the estimator runs fast to track uncertainty, while a bandwidth-limited filter keeps the control signal smooth and the transient bounded. The trade is explicit — L1 buys bounded transients at the price of a filter bandwidth capping how much of the fast estimate reaches the actuators.

## 8.2 Sliding-Mode Adaptive PID (APIDC)

Within the PID-centric scope, sliding-mode appears only as the adaptive mechanism that updates and robustifies a PID law. The 2026 *Scientific Reports* study credits adaptive-gain nonlinear PID with handling uncertainties and external disturbances a fixed-gain law cannot. The adaptation combines gradient descent on a tracking-error cost with a Lyapunov stability argument — the same certificate structure as MRAC.

- **Coverage:** continuous adaptation covers the envelope without a pre-enumerated schedule; the distinguishing feature is **robustness by construction** — the sliding mechanism holds behavior against disturbances, mapping directly onto gust rejection.
- **Model requirement:** intermediate — a structural model enough to define the surface, tolerating parametric uncertainty.
- **Strengths:** strong disturbance rejection and robustness to model error — capabilities the fixed-gain cascade cannot match once the disturbance exceeds its linearization band.
- **Limitations:** **chattering** — the switching action delivering robustness commands rapid actuator reversals. A falsifier: if the switching term excites structural resonance or overheats motors, the robustness benefit is nullified unless chatter is suppressed.
- **Best-fit:** aggressive or high-disturbance flight. Where MRAC suits a known payload change, the sliding-mode variant suits *persistent, unpredictable disturbance* — the two occupy opposite ends of the smooth-versus-robust spectrum.

**Fuzzy compensator to remove chattering.** The standard remedy replaces the hard switch with a fuzzy compensator grading the transition near the surface. The common objection — that this trades chatter for an opaque rule base — is rebutted: a fuzzy controller can be made *no more complex than a well-tuned PID while preserving effectiveness*, and the minimal fuzzy PID uses only two fuzzy sets per input and four for the output, admitting explicit stability analysis. Two cures for chatter compete: downstream filtering (attacks it after generation, at the cost of phase lag) versus the **upstream fuzzy compensator** (grades the switch so the discontinuity is never generated). The upstream route is structurally preferable because it avoids the phase lag that would erode the very stability margin the sliding-mode design provides.

## 8.3 Fuzzy PID for Real-Time Gain Adjustment

Fuzzy PID computes gains from linguistic rules over the tracking error and its rate. One reported UAV form is a layered architecture: the 2026 *PLOS ONE* study uses a single-layer neural network (10 hidden neurons) to tune PID gains for the y and ψ axes and fuzzy logic for the x, z, φ, θ axes, improving trajectory tracking.

- **Coverage:** computes adjustment from observed error rather than a measured scheduling variable, covering a full six-axis trajectory task — the online responsiveness it shares with MRAC and APIDC.
- **Model requirement:** essentially model-free — rules encode heuristic knowledge of how gains move with error; tuning effort moves into membership functions and the rule base.
- **Strengths:** improved tracking from the fuzzy-plus-neural partition. The cascade form's strength is confining the fuzzy layer to the outer, slower loop and the nonlinear axes, leaving the well-characterized inner loop to classical PID.
- **Limitations:** a falsifier — if the fuzzy outer loop's corrections interact with the inner loop to produce a limit cycle the classical PID alone did not exhibit, the partition's benefit is contradicted.
- **Cost:** a compact rule base is cheap — a few membership lookups and a defuzzification per step; cost grows with rule count, so keep it small.
- **Best-fit:** platforms where certain axes behave nonlinearly and a full model is unavailable. Compared with MRAC's Lyapunov certificate, fuzzy PID trades that formal guarantee for implementation simplicity.

**Counter-evidence on complexity.** The complaint that fuzzy models are "relatively complex and hard to drive to a predetermined expectation" would, if universal, disqualify the method for safety-critical flight. It resolves by **splitting the method:** the minimal two-set form admits explicit models and stability analysis — analyzable and auditable; the elaborate multi-rule form is where the complaint bites, adding tuning difficulty and eroding analyzability faster than closed-loop quality. Set against MRAC and APIDC, which carry *constructive* Lyapunov certificates, even the analyzable fuzzy form relies on **after-the-fact** stability analysis — the structural difference between the families. The credible role on open-source stacks is the compact, frozen-rule-base form on the outer loop.

## 8.4 Neural-Network and Reinforcement-Learning Tuning

A learned policy chooses the gains — the most flexible point on the adaptation spectrum and, without constraints, the most hazardous. Recent UAV work spans the range: a 2025 *Sensors* RL method predicting PD gains achieved the smallest attitude errors and significantly improved tracking versus hand-tuning; a 2026 *Aerospace* study demonstrated hierarchical adaptive PID tuning via **safety-constrained RL** for agile flight.

**Library-restricted safe design.** The governing principle: unconstrained RL-based PID tuning can drive gains *outside the stabilizing region*, whereas stability-preserving methods enforce constraints to avoid instability. The safest design denies the agent free choice and has it **select from a finite set of certified-stabilizing gain configurations** — converting RL from a gain *generator* into a gain *selector*. This is a learned analogue of gain scheduling, with the selection policy discovered by RL rather than hand-designed.

- **Model requirement:** inherited from whatever produces the certified library (Chapter 4/5); the RL layer learns the selection policy from interaction.
- **Strengths:** tracking improvement over hand-tuning plus mitigation of aerodynamic nonlinearities, while inheriting the certified library's stability.
- **Limitations:** a falsifier — if the certified library is too coarse to cover a flight condition the vehicle actually encounters, the selector is forced to a marginally-stable set, and the guarantee degrades at exactly the envelope edge where it is most needed.
- **Cost:** inference on a trained policy selecting from a small library is cheap (a compact forward pass); the heavy cost is offline — training plus generating and certifying the library.
- **Best-fit:** platforms demanding both aggressive tracking and a stability guarantee. Compared with fuzzy PID's hand-written rules, the RL selector discovers its policy from data and exploits structure a rule designer would miss — at the cost of a training pipeline and certification burden.

**The stability-versus-flexibility trade-off.** The flexibility that makes learned tuning powerful is the same property that makes it dangerous. A free-rein agent can find a tuning no human or rule base reaches; the same freedom, absent constraints, lets it wander into a destabilizing set. The resolution is to **fence** the learning: stability-preserving constraints keep the policy inside the safe region, so the worst outcome is still stabilizing — recovering most flexibility while retiring the instability risk. **On Stability & Safety Margin, unconstrained learned tuning fails outright; safety-constrained and library-restricted variants earn a conditional pass** resting on the library's quality. Set against MRAC's constructive certificate, even the safety-constrained RL variant buys its safety from an *external fence* rather than the controller's structure — which is why the certified-library route, not free learning, is the form that reaches flight hardware.

# 9 Comparative Analysis and Tier Ranking

Ten distinct strategies now require a single comparison grid. The obstacle is that comparing classical and learned controllers is confounded by asymmetries in task definition, datasets, and feed-forward information — which the 2025 "Leveling the Playing Field" paper shows can produce misleading conclusions. We address that by scoring **qualitative dimensions against named criteria**, not raw tracking numbers drawn from incommensurable experiments. The field lacks agreed metrics for testing, evaluation, verification, and validation of PID, MPC, or ML controllers, so the matrix and scoring are an author-constructed framework, not a reproduction of a standard that does not yet exist.

## 9.1 Cross-Method Comparison Matrix

| Method | Mechanism | Coverage | Model Req. | Strengths | Limitations | Cost | Best-Fit |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Classical Heuristic | Excitation lookup rules | Single point | Model-free | Fast, no model | Low margins | Trivial | First-pass bring-up |
| LQR/LQG | Quadratic-cost feedback | Single linearization | Full linear | Low overshoot | Local validity | Low | Model-rich platforms |

| Metaheuristic (GA/PSO) | Population cost search | As-modeled | Sim model | Global optimum | Sim-bound | Heavy offline | Index-driven tuning |

| Sliding-Mode APIDC | Switching + adaptive PID | Online | Bounds only | Strong rejection | Chattering | Moderate | High-disturbance |
| Fuzzy PID | Rule base maps error→gain | Multi-regime (soft) | Expert rules | Smooth nonlinear | Rule-design effort | Moderate | Nonlinear plants |
| Neural/RL-Assisted | Learned policy/gains | As-trained | Data (sim) | Flexible fit | Validation gap | Heavy train | Research frontier |

The Uppsala thesis shows that when a quadrotor is linearized about hover, the resulting LQR stabilizes hover but oscillates when the vehicle moves to new positions. The PX4 adaptive autotuner uses retrospective cost adaptive control to optimize the autopilot during a single flight. **Coverage is the axis on which the methods most sharply diverge:** the AL-TUNE study establishes that fixed gains tuned for one regime cannot maintain performance across envelope-wide variations in aerodynamics, inertia, and control effectiveness. **On Multi-Regime Robustness, only gain scheduling and the four online adapters earn a clear pass; the three offline families fail it by construction.** A contrarian note: the Neural/RL row's "flexible fit" strength and "validation gap" weakness are conditional on a fair evaluation protocol the literature does not yet standardize.

**Reconciling "optimal."** The families do not share a definition. For heuristics it is a quarter-amplitude-decay target; for LQR it is literal and provable but *local*; for H∞ it is worst-case-optimal; for metaheuristics it is whatever the index encodes; for the online families it is a moving target relative to the plant as experienced. The cleanest reconciliation is the Pareto view[^33]: **"optimal PID" is not one quantity but a position on a performance–robustness Pareto front, and the families differ mainly in where on that front they natively aim.** The report scores the six rubric criteria directly, so a method's optimality posture enters only through its measurable consequences.

## 9.4 Validation Workflow Behind the Scores

Every score is a claim that must be demonstrable. The progression moves from cheap, safe, low-fidelity tests toward expensive, high-fidelity ones so defects are caught where they cost least:

```mermaid
graph LR
 A[Simulation] --> B[System ID] --> C[SIL/HIL] --> D[Bench Test] --> E[Multi-Condition Flight Test]
 C -->|model error| B
 D -->|instability| C
 E -->|regime failure| A
```

**Simulation** is the first filter, where the metaheuristic family does essentially all its work — the Tehnički vjesnik comparison remains simulation-bound, which is why its scores cannot be trusted without later stages. **System ID** builds the model the model-based methods require; the Uppsala hover-linearization warning is a system-ID defect this stage surfaces. **SIL/HIL** tests the computational-cost column — TinyMPC and GPU-parallelized MPPI achieving an 18.6% RMSE improvement over NMPC on embedded hardware show real-time feasibility is a HIL question. **Bench testing** calibrates the thrust curve. **Multi-condition flight test** is the only conclusive stage: the AL-TUNE study establishes that testing must cover multiple conditions because fixed gains cannot maintain performance across envelope-wide variations. **A method's score is provisional until it survives this stage across the declared envelope** — which is why Validation Maturity weights flight-proven methods above simulation-only ones. The metaheuristic route terminates its evidence at simulation; in-flight auto-tuning generates evidence at the final stage by construction.

**Why gains must be demonstrated across regimes.** The causal chain: linearization about hover neglects higher-order terms, which become significant away from hover, causing unmodeled dynamics that destabilize the controller and produce oscillations when position changes. A hover-only score records none of this. The requirement is sharpest for transitioning airframes — the MATLAB/Simulink VTOL case shows yaw/position interference that appears only in hover. **The validation burden scales with regime count, not method complexity**, and a scheduled or adaptive method's higher Validation score is earned only by paying the fuller test cost. The standard-metrics gap is the field-level version of the same problem: without agreed metrics, even a multi-condition campaign produces numbers that do not transfer between groups.

**Synthesis:** a method's tier is determined less by its peak capability than by **where across the envelope it can demonstrably deliver and how maturely that delivery has been validated.** In-Flight Auto-Tune and Gain Scheduling form a *stable core* justified by rank-robustness; Metaheuristic, APIDC, and the remaining adapters form a *conditional periphery* shifting with mission priorities. The classical and model-based offline families retain value as bring-up and safety-margin anchors, not standalone multi-regime solutions. The top methods are **composable, not mutually exclusive** — the robust fractional-order gain-scheduled adaptive controller for LPV UAVs is concrete evidence a scheduling layer and an adaptive layer can coexist. A program treating them as composable layers inherits both the Robustness leader and the Tuning Effort leader — the logic Chapter 10 builds on.

# 10 Recommendation and Deployment Pipeline

Selecting the right strategy is a decision about how much of the envelope a single gain set must cover and how much uncertainty the mission tolerates. **The committed answer: a robust offline baseline plus gain scheduling is the default for almost every multi-regime UAV, with adaptive augmentation reserved for missions whose uncertainty genuinely exceeds what a finite schedule can bracket.**

## 10.1 Ranked Recommendation

**A three-layer stack, layers added only as the mission demands.** The **base layer** is a robust offline tune — a single gain set holding adequate margin across the widest band a fixed set can span. Reynoso-Meza et al. show how to choose it rigorously via Pareto-front inspection. This layer earns the strongest verdict on Stability & Safety Margin: its gains do not change in flight, so the stability proven on the bench is the stability flown — no adaptation transient to certify.

The **second layer** is gain scheduling, added where one margin-robust set sacrifices too much nominal performance. INAV 9.0's APA is a shipping open-source instance, varying gains with airspeed to prevent oscillation. The **third layer**, adaptive augmentation (MRAC/L1), is added only for high-uncertainty missions where the plant moves in ways a finite schedule cannot bracket. **The escalation discipline: add a layer only when regime variability demonstrably defeats the one below it.** By ~2028, safety-constrained RL may make the adaptive layer safe enough to recommend more broadly.

**Platform-specific picks** — the dominant source of plant variation differs by airframe:

- **Racing drone — base layer alone.** A single aggressively-tuned set for the narrow band actually flown. AlphaPilot enabled racing at 8 m/s, ranking second in 2019; Betaflight's defaults lean on Airmode, Antigravity, and a dynamic filter rather than a schedule, consistent with rewarding a fast fixed inner loop over coverage.
- **Heavy-lift — base plus scheduling on payload/thrust, escalating to MRAC for unpredictable shifts.** The IMAV study considered exactly Direct MRAC and Gain Scheduling for variable-payload quads[^73]: when payload is sensed or commanded, schedule on that signal; when it shifts unpredictably, MRAC earns its cost.
- **VTOL — scheduling is structural, not optional.** PX4 requires a dedicated mode switch; INAV stores per-mode profiles. The hard problem is the *transition region* between nodes — the MATLAB/Simulink analysis shows yaw/position interference destabilizing the hover condition.
- **Autonomous-mission UAV — the full three-layer stack.** This is where learned augmentation pays: AirP
