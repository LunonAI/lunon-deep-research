# From Chalkboards to Chatbots: AIGC in K-12 Classrooms

## Executive Verdict

AIGC in K-12 classrooms is now **a teacher-productivity technology first and a student-learning technology second**, and the evidence supports that ordering. As of Gallup's 2025 survey, **60% of K-12 teachers already use AI, with weekly users saving roughly six hours a week** — a labor reallocation larger than any measured learning gain to date.

The strongest controlled evidence — the UK LearnLM math RCT, the PAIRR and FeedbackWriter writing trials — shows that student gains are **conditional**: they appear only when a human tutor or teacher scaffolds the tool, and collapse to noise when students use standalone chat.

The report's central conclusion, defended across twelve application categories and twelve case studies: **through 2030, lesson-planning and material-generation uses deliver the most reliable return; formative-feedback and tutoring deliver the highest *conditional* return; and unsupervised student-facing chat delivers the most risk.** Adopt teacher-facing tools aggressively, student-facing tools cautiously, and verify everything.

## 1. Research Framework and Scope

### 1.1 The Twelve Application Categories

Generative AI in K-12 is best understood not as a single tool but as twelve distinct pedagogical functions, each with its own evidence base, risk profile, and readiness level. Only two — AI tutoring and writing feedback — carry randomized controlled trial evidence at the K-12 level. The 2026 Nature meta-analysis and the Deng et al. *Computers & Education* review both document **sharply conditional efficacy**, with effects concentrated where teacher oversight and task design are strong.

The categories partition into **three functional families**, consistent with the 3P (Presage-Process-Product) mapping in the MDPI systematic review:

- **Teacher-facing production** (A, B, F, J, K) — keeps a human in the verification loop by default
- **Student-facing interaction** (C, D, G, H) — removes that buffer
- **Capability/access building** (E, I, L)

**Readiness and risk track the family, not the tool.**

| Category | Primary Use | Representative Tools | Evidence | Key Risk | Readiness |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A Lesson Planning | Plans, slides, worksheets | ChatGPT, MagicSchool, LessonAgent | Gallup 2025 | Hallucination | **High** |
| B Differentiated Material | Reading-level variants | Diffit, SchoolAI, Claude | Springer 2025 | Mislabeled levels | Med-High |
| C AI Tutoring (ITS) | Adaptive 1:1 dialogue | LearnLM, Khanmigo | UK LearnLM RCT | Autonomy, equity | Low-Med |
| D Writing Feedback | Formative AI feedback | ChatGPT, PAIRR | PAIRR; FeedbackWriter | Overreliance | Medium |
| E Generative Image | TTIG, ideation | DALL·E, Midjourney | MIT co-design | Bias, IP | Low-Med |
| F Multimodal Content | Audio/video/slides | Gemini, Synthesia | TPACK/TAM study | Quality variance | Medium |
| G Language Learning | Translation, practice | Gemini, Duolingo Max | Vietnam/APEC | Accuracy gaps | Med-High |
| H STEM/Math | Worked examples | LearnLM, Photomath | Sierra Leone | Wrong steps | Low-Med |
| I AI Literacy | Teaching AI itself | Custom curricula | OECD framework | Curriculum gaps | Medium |
| J Assessment Items | Quiz/rubric generation | ChatGPT, MagicSchool | GOV.UK guidance | Validity | Medium |
| K Admin/Comms | Emails, IEPs, reports | Copilot, MagicSchool | Coursiv 2026 | Privacy, tone | **High** |
| L Inclusive/Accessibility | UDL adaptations | TTS, simplifiers | Edo State | Age-appropriateness | Low-Med |

**Headline verdict:** Teacher Lesson Planning leads on readiness, AI Tutoring leads on outcome evidence, and Inclusive/Special Education leads on unmet need.

### 1.2 Scope Boundary and Persona Lens

The boundary is the **K-12 classroom**: learners aged five to eighteen in supervised school settings. Higher-education, adult-learning, and corporate-training evidence is excluded from core claims and flagged whenever imported. The PAIRR writing workflow originated partly in business-writing contexts, and several tutor-efficacy signals derive from college samples; both are labeled as **adapted**, not native K-12 evidence.

The analytical lens is dual. As a **learning-sciences researcher**, this report weights effect sizes, evidence tiers, and replication. As a **frontline teacher**, it weights prep time, class-management friction, device realities, and verification burden. Where these disagree — a tool with a respectable effect size but impractical in a thirty-student room with shared devices — the report says so rather than privileging the statistic.

Case-study quality follows established appraisal standards: a study earns "strong" standing only with a clear design, defined sample, and transparent outcome measure. Vendor decks and blog testimonials are admitted only as illustrative, never as outcome evidence.

### 1.3 Evaluation Rubric

Every category is scored against five weighted criteria:

| Criterion | Weight |
|---|---|
| Evidence Strength & Outcome Documentation | 0.30 |
| Pedagogical Learning Value | 0.25 |
| Classroom Practicality & Implementation Readiness | 0.20 |
| Risk, Ethics & Age-Appropriateness | 0.15 |
| Global Transferability | 0.10 |

Because **Evidence Strength dominates**, categories resting only on vendor claims are capped regardless of enthusiasm — which is how the conditional-efficacy findings are kept from being overstated.

**Key terms:** *AIGC/GenAI* (text, image, audio, video from a generative model); *TTIG* (Text-to-Image Generation); *ITS* (Intelligent Tutoring System, pedagogically tuned, distinct from a raw chatbot); *PAIRR* (peer-and-AI review-and-revision workflow); *Conditional Efficacy* (effects depend on oversight and design); *Teacher Verification Burden* (the oversight cost hallucination imposes).

## 2. The Classification Framework

The classification problem is not primarily about tooling but about pedagogy: the same model (Gemini, ChatGPT) can occupy radically different classroom roles depending on who directs it and toward what end. A framework that sorts by brand collapses within a product cycle; one that sorts by **pedagogical function, agency distribution, and AI autonomy** survives it.

### 2.1 Three Orthogonal Axes

- **Axis 1 — Pedagogical function:** What is the output *for*? Material production, tutoring delivery, formative feedback, or the AI as object of study.
- **Axis 2 — Learner/teacher role:** Who holds agency? A teacher-facing generator keeps the educator in control; a student-facing tutor transfers initiative to the child.
- **Axis 3 — AI autonomy:** How much cognitive work runs unsupervised, from *draft-and-verify* (teacher checks every output) to *autonomous delivery* (the model interacts with students in real time).

The single most consequential distinction is **AI as delivery tool vs. AI Literacy as Content** — what the California Department of Education frames as "Learning With AI" versus "Learning About AI." When a teacher uses MagicSchool to draft a worksheet, the model is a production instrument. When a class dissects how that model hallucinates, the system becomes the curriculum object. Collapsing the two is a common design error: a district that buys a tutoring license has not thereby taught AI literacy.

### 2.2 Why the Grid Is Orthogonal, Not Hierarchical

One might expect autonomy to rise monotonically with student-facing use. The Frontiers 2025 review shows this is false: **teacher-facing applications frequently run at the *highest* autonomy** (auto-generating whole units no one reads) because no child-safety layer constrains them, while the most visible student-facing tools are often the most tightly capped. Treating the axes as independent coordinates is what lets the framework flag the dangerous cell — **high autonomy, student-facing, delivery function** — that a single-number rubric would hide.

| Category | Function | Dominant Role | Typical Autonomy |
|---|---|---|---|
| A Lesson Planning | Production | Teacher-facing | Draft-and-verify |
| C AI Tutoring | Delivery | Student-facing | **Autonomous** |
| D Writing Feedback | Formative feedback | Mixed | Suggest-and-confirm |
| F Multimodal | Production | Teacher-facing | Draft-and-verify |
| I AI Literacy | Content object | Student-facing | N/A (studied) |
| K Admin/Comms | Workflow | Teacher-facing | Semi-autonomous |

### 2.3 The Tradeoff the Framework Surfaces

Higher autonomy promises the largest workload relief, yet raises the probability that an unverified hallucination reaches students. Districts do not resolve this by banning high-autonomy use; in practice they **retain high autonomy for teacher-facing production** (where a single educator verifies before distribution) and **cap autonomy hard for student-facing delivery** (where verification cannot happen in advance). The binding constraint is not the model's capability but **the placement of the human checkpoint relative to the student**.

A contrarian caution: evidence on predictive AI shows that when people attribute authority to an AI's outputs, they *constrain* their own agency, deferring to the system. Applied to the classroom, a high-autonomy tutor may suppress the very student initiative educators intend to build.

## 3. The Twelve Categories in Detail

### Category A — Teacher Lesson Planning & Material Generation

The **highest-frequency, lowest-risk** teacher application, and the entry point through which most teachers first encounter AIGC.

The core activity is a **draft-then-verify loop**: the teacher supplies the standard, grade, and constraint; the model drafts; the teacher reshapes. The LessonAgent pipeline formalizes this, auto-generating aligned lesson plans, slides, and even podcasts from a topic specification — the "plan" is increasingly a bundle of coordinated artifacts. Dominant tools are general LLMs (ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude) plus education wrappers (MagicSchool, SchoolAI) that pre-load pedagogical scaffolding, trading flexibility for reliability.

**Evidence:** Artifact-quality evidence is solid (LessonAgent's human-rated coherence; positive TPACK/TAM teacher ratings), but direct randomized evidence tying generated materials to student learning is thinner than for tutoring.

**Benefits:** Workload compression, multimodal richness, ideation breadth. The load-bearing benefit: Category A **shifts teacher labor from production toward curation and verification** — which is where the pedagogical value accrues.

**Risk and the time-saving paradox:** The central risk is hallucination propagated into instructional material. A tool marketed as a time-saver can *add* time when verification is done properly; the net saving is real but **smaller than vendor claims imply**. Standards misalignment is a second risk the Australian Framework addresses by insisting on human judgment at the point of use.

**Readiness:** The clearest pass in the matrix — a browser and an LLM suffice. The binding constraint is prompt literacy, not infrastructure: the TPACK/TAM study found teachers' *technological* knowledge, not pedagogical or content knowledge, was the limiting factor. A contrarian note: the Frontiers review cautions that low *perceived* risk can breed *under-verification*, making Category A's very safety its subtle hazard.

### Category B — Differentiated & Personalized Material Generation

Differentiation — tailoring the same objective to students at different reading levels — has historically been the most time-expensive task a teacher performs, and it is where AIGC delivers its **clearest early return**.

The category is overwhelmingly **teacher-led** (the educator directs tiered variants and retains editorial control), not algorithmic adaptation. Diffit is the archetype, "leveling" any text into multiple reading grades; MagicSchool and SchoolAI bundle equivalent generators, with SchoolAI arguing that *structured* AI outperforms generic prompting because it constrains output format. Use concentrates in grades 3–9 literacy and content-area reading, where the spread between weakest and strongest decoders is widest.

**Evidence:** The proximate evidence is **workload, not learning-outcome** data — the Gallup ~6-hours-saved figure, plus Springer's documented gains in the *reach* of differentiation. No RCT isolates the differentiation mechanism alone.

**The central tension:** A "lower-level" rewrite that silently strips conceptual content *appears* to differentiate while reducing rigor for the students who can least afford it. The failure mode is a two-tier curriculum that entrenches the gap. This resolves only through deliberate teacher review of whether the simplified version preserves the learning objective.

**Verdict:** The clearest pass on Classroom Practicality of any category; only a qualified pass on Evidence Strength — robust workload data, not-yet-robust learning data. The contrarian caution: a tool marketed as closing equity gaps can widen them when a simplified tier quietly teaches less.

### Category C — AI Tutoring & Intelligent Tutoring Systems

The **most aspirational and highest-stakes** category, placing a conversational agent in direct, turn-by-turn contact with the learner.

Two technical lineages now share the label "AI tutor": the decades-old **model-traced ITS** (diagnoses misconceptions against an expert domain model) and the newer **LLM conversational tutor** (a model fine-tuned for pedagogical dialogue). LearnLM reframes the core engineering challenge as "**pedagogical instruction following**" — training a model to withhold answers and scaffold rather than present information. The flagship research artifact is LearnLM; everyday classroom delivery comes through ChatGPT/Gemini with a tutoring prompt, or wrappers like Khanmigo. Philanthropic capital now treats the category as investable: Digital Promise opened an $8M AI-tutoring RFP in 2026.

**Evidence:** The strongest single data point is the UK exploratory RCT of a LearnLM-based tutor (detailed in §4.1). The LearnLM technical report shows pedagogically tuned models outperform untuned baselines on expert preference. A qualified pass: a genuine controlled comparison most categories lack, but the exploratory label and modest sample keep it short of confirmatory.

**The defining tension:** The same conversational fluency that makes an LLM tutor engaging makes its confident errors dangerous — a hallucinated solution delivered in an authoritative register can cement a misconception. The LearnLM line of work proposes an *architectural* resolution: fine-tune the model so its default posture is diagnostic questioning rather than assertion.

**The architectural divide and its resolution:** The classical ITS cannot hallucinate outside its encoded domain model but is expensive and brittle to author. The LLM tutor inherits broad coverage free but surrenders correctness. The field is converging on a **hybrid**: ground the LLM's conversational front end in a verified content store, so breadth and correctness coexist.

| Dimension | Model-Traced ITS | LLM Tutor | Hybrid (grounded) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Domain coverage | Narrow, hand-authored | Broad, pretrained | Broad + verified |
| Hallucination risk | Near-zero | High | Reduced |
| Authoring cost | High | Low | Medium |
| Verification burden | Low | High | Medium |

**Readiness:** Currently **fails the at-scale bar** — the tooling is ahead of the oversight infrastructure. The ACE 2026 survey found most K-12 teachers report inadequate training to supervise AI tools responsibly. The credible 2027 deployments will be those that solve the Teacher Verification Burden at the workflow level (grounding, logging, dashboards), not those with the most fluent model.

### Category D — Writing Instruction & AI Formative Feedback

Writing sits at the sharpest intersection of AIGC and pedagogy: the same capability that makes an LLM a useful **formative feedback** partner is the one that, misused, **writes the essay outright**. This category carries both the strongest documented learning upside and the most acute integrity exposure.

The core activity is iterative draft-and-revise, with AI feedback **staged between drafts** rather than at submission — the reframing the PAIRR Model operationalizes by inserting peer review and AI review as distinct, ordered stages. ChatGPT and Gemini dominate the general side; SchoolAI, MagicSchool, and Khanmigo (Socratic, withholding direct text) the structured side. Use concentrates in upper-primary through secondary ELA, with secondary spillover into history and science argumentation.

**Evidence (the category's strength):** The anchor is the PAIRR study (§4.2); corroborating, the ChatGPT-vs-experts corpus shows model feedback is fluent enough to function credibly while remaining imperfect. A randomized FeedbackWriter trial improved revisions; a University of Vienna study found AI feedback increased secondary students' revision, motivation, and positive emotions.

**The integrity-vs-feedback paradox:** The model that coaches a revision can equally ghostwrite the submission. This does not resolve by detection alone but through **assessment redesign** — grading the documented revision trajectory and in-class writing rather than the polished artifact. The contrarian reading: the PAIRR evidence implies the *sequencing* of human and AI review, not the model's raw fluency, moves revision behavior.

**The "revise less" caution:** A Springer 2026 comparison found generative AI offers *more* feedback yet students revise *less* substantively than under teacher feedback. The resolution: purposefully designed scaffolds, not raw chat, convert abundant feedback into deep revision, because unstructured output invites shallow acceptance.

**Verdict:** Highest-upside, highest-supervision entry. Pedagogical Learning Value and teacher-facing practicality both pass cleanly; the risk standing is contingent entirely on assessment redesign. The unsettled longitudinal question: whether students who revise with AI internalize the strategies or grow dependent on the prompt.

### Category E — Generative Image Tools in Arts Education

**TTIG collapses the intent-to-artifact gap**, letting a K-6 learner describe "a purple dragon reading a book under the sea" and see it rendered in seconds — the single most consequential shift generative media brings to visual arts.

The defining pedagogical move is **decoupling idea generation from manual execution**, repositioning the art task from "can you draw it" to "can you see, judge, and revise it." Dominant tools are DALL·E, Midjourney, and Stable Diffusion; for classroom safety, the **moderated teacher-platform wrapper, not the raw diffusion model, is the defensible default**. Primary use targets creative empowerment; secondary use targets critical deconstruction — almost opposite framings of the same tool.

**Evidence:** A qualified pass — rich qualitative co-design data (the MIT Media Lab K-6 study, §4.3) but no randomized outcome study comparable to the tutoring RCT.

**The craft-erosion paradox:** A technology sold as creative empowerment may erode the craft skills art education exists to build. This resolves not by banning the tool but by *repositioning* it: when the objective is critique, composition, or ideation rather than manual rendering, the machine's drawing becomes the object of study. Beyond craft, three risks dominate: **bias** (the face-generation benchmark found demographic skew across all three models), **age-appropriateness** (only 23% of leading EdTech AI tools disclose auditable models), and **copyright/provenance**.

**Verdict:** Raw consumer TTIG fails the primary-grade bar; only moderated, teacher-mediated deployment passes. The unsettled question: whether sustained TTIG use in primary grades strengthens or atrophies foundational drawing skill — no longitudinal study exists.

### Category F — Multimodal Content Creation (Audio, Video, Slides)

A single prompt now yields several media formats at once — narrated audio, short video, auto-generated slides from one source text. The LessonAgent pipeline automates this end-to-end; cinematic-synthesis methods chain Stable Diffusion, narrative generation, and text-to-audio to build a 60-second film from text alone.

**Evidence:** The K-12-specific anchor is the video-GenAI TPACK/TAM study, whose adoption chain runs from perceived ease of use → perceived usefulness → adoption intention → classroom integration. Solid on feasibility and acceptance; thin on causal learning outcomes.

**The verification paradox at its worst:** Each added modality multiplies the surface for hallucinated narration, mislabeled imagery, or desynchronized audio. **The richer the generated media, the more authoritative it looks and the less students question it** — research on AI authority shows people defer to confident machine output against their own judgment. The resolution is structural: route every generated clip through a teacher fact-check before display.

**Verdict:** Slide and audio generation are turnkey today; **video remains infrastructure-bound and the highest-verification-risk creation use** — synthetic audio and video carry deepfake and authority-deference risks that still imagery does not. Most current use sits at SAMR Augmentation; student authorship reaches Modification and Redefinition.

### Category G — Language Learning & Translation Support

The **only category where the tool's core competency — fluent language production — is identical to the learning objective itself**. That identity is both its greatest strength and its sharpest hazard.

The core activity is conversational and written second-language practice with real-time correction and translation bridges. Tools split between general LLMs and structured platforms (SchoolAI logs every student turn for monitoring; AIForEdu ranks structured platforms ahead of generic chatbots on FERPA compliance). Coverage spans EAL/multilingual support at primary level and CEFR-aligned practice at secondary.

**The defining risk — fluent-error fossilization:** A model produces grammatically fluent but incorrect output → the novice, lacking metalinguistic knowledge to detect the error, encodes it as correct → the mistake fossilizes into long-term interlanguage that later instruction struggles to undo.

**Verdict (the most polarized profile in the set):** The strongest pass on Pedagogical Learning Value (the tool's output medium and the learning target coincide — every turn is authentic input). But a **conditional fail on Risk absent active safeguards**: the learner cannot independently audit the very skill they are acquiring. The resolution is **staged scaffolding** — pair translation access with a deliberate withdrawal schedule and an unassisted assessment checkpoint.

### Category H — STEM & Math Worked-Example Generation

The **most diagnostically revealing** category: it showcases the technology's greatest productivity gains and its sharpest reliability failures. A worked example that silently mis-states a derivation step **teaches the error directly into a child's schema**.

The defining tension: arithmetic and multi-step reasoning are **precisely where LLMs remain least trustworthy**, yet worked-example generation is where teacher demand is highest. A cross-model correctness study (ChatGPT-4, Gemini, Claude-3, Copilot) found meaningful accuracy spreads, meaning a school standardizing on one engine inherits that engine's error profile across every worksheet.

**Evidence:** The LearnLM RCT and cross-model study give STEM stronger causal evidence than most categories, yet almost none isolates worked-example generation from the broader tutoring bundle.

**The causal pathway of harm:** An LLM's next-token objective rewards plausibility over arithmetic validity → confident mis-statements → propagation into student schemas when a child copies a step verbatim → misconception surfaces only at summative assessment, when costly to unlearn.

**Verdict:** **Highest productivity ceiling paired with highest reliability risk** — the category where the gap between naive and verified adoption is most consequential. The heaviest Teacher Verification Burden in the report: correctness cannot be spot-checked by fluency and must be recomputed step by step. The 2026–2028 outlook is convergence on **tool-augmented engines** that offload arithmetic to a symbolic solver while the LLM narrates reasoning, which should materially lower the burden by 2028.

### Category I — AI Literacy & Computational Thinking as Content

The conceptual pivot: where eleven categories treat AI as a *means* of instruction, this treats **AI itself as the object of study**. The defining claim: AI-as-delivery and AI-literacy-as-content are **orthogonal goals** — a school can deploy MagicSchool daily and teach zero AI literacy, and a teacher can build deep computational thinking using nothing but unplugged activities.

The OECD AI Literacy Framework defines four competency domains spanning primary and secondary education. Tooling splits between purpose-built literacy platforms (ActiveAI, delivering curriculum at scale with learning analytics; CulturAIEd, adapting activities to cultural context) and repurposed general models used as live specimens of hallucination.

**Evidence:** A conditional pass — frameworks are mature and consensus-backed, but **no single RCT establishes a causal learning-gain effect size for a named K-12 AI-literacy curriculum**. The base is framework-rich and outcome-poor. The binding constraint is teacher capacity: the SRI review names teacher preparation as the limiting factor.

**Verdict (the inverse of the delivery categories):** Strong pass on Pedagogical Learning Value (the only category whose explicit aim is durable conceptual understanding) and on **Global Transferability** — because its foundational tier needs *no hardware*, it transfers where compute-heavy categories cannot, making it the single most equitable category for low-infrastructure and Global South settings. Weak on Readiness: it demands curriculum redesign and new teacher content knowledge.

### Category J — Assessment Item Generation & Automated Feedback

Where generative productivity collides most directly with **measurement validity** — a fluent multiple-choice item can be psychometrically broken in ways no fluency check reveals. The category spans both formative feedback and the manufacture of summative test material.

The decisive design choice: whether the model **drafts items for teacher review** (low-stakes) or **scores live student work** (grade consequences). Structured platforms (MagicSchool, Diffit, Brisk) constrain to curriculum frames; generic models trade safety for flexibility. The objectivity gradient organizes everything: closed-form math is the strongest case (correctness is checkable); open-response humanities the weakest.

**The stakes-proportionality constraint:** AI promises to lighten grading yet creates a new verification obligation. For **low-stakes formative checks** the burden is trivial and the saving holds; for **summative items** the required review approaches the cost of authoring from scratch, so the saving evaporates exactly where stakes are highest. Schools that ignore stakes-proportionality import invalid measurement. Compounding this: only 23% of top tools disclose auditable models.

**Verdict:** Strong evidence for formative use (LearnLM RCT); thin for summative scoring. Pass for drafting; fail for unsupervised scoring. **Generative AI is a safe assessment partner exactly to the degree that a human adjudicates its output before it touches a grade.**

### Category K — Teacher Administrative & Communication Support

The **least glamorous but most quantitatively reliable** beneficiary of AIGC. Its value is almost entirely **workload recovery rather than learning gain**, making it the single most defensible entry point for a risk-averse school. The category spans parent communication, emails, IEP drafting, meeting minutes, and scheduling.

General assistants (ChatGPT, Gemini, Copilot) dominate, but the purpose-built K-12 layer matters more here than anywhere: MagicSchool and SchoolAI ship FERPA-aligned templates. The category is genuinely **grade- and subject-agnostic**.

**The privacy paradox:** The lowest-pedagogical-risk category carries among the highest *privacy* risk — parent emails and IEPs contain personally identifiable student data, and pasting that into a consumer chatbot can breach FERPA. It resolves through **tool choice**: routing this work through FERPA-aligned platforms converts privacy exposure into a governed data flow. The constraint is procurement discipline, not capability.

**Evidence:** The 80/20 heuristic (roughly 80% of early teacher value accrues to prep and admin) holds across MagicSchool/SchoolAI usage data, though nearly all evidence rests on teacher *perception* rather than instrumented time-on-task logs.

**Verdict:** The most deployment-ready category in the taxonomy. The efficacy verdict inverts the student-facing pattern: efficacy is conditional **not on pedagogical design but purely on procurement governance**. The natural first domino in a staged adoption sequence — capturing the 80/20 dividend with near-zero learning risk while building institutional trust. By 2027, the category is most likely to dissolve into the ambient productivity layer of school information systems (Copilot, Workspace), telling leaders to spend scarce PD hours on the pedagogical categories that will *not* self-onboard.

### Category L — Inclusive/Special Education & Accessibility

The **strongest equity case** in the entire taxonomy, yet the **thinnest K-12-specific randomized evidence base** of any category.

For a dyslexic nine-year-old or a newly arrived multilingual learner, the capacity to re-level, re-format, and re-voice a single source text is not a productivity gain but **a precondition for participation**. The decisive feature: AIGC collapses the cost of producing a one-of-one artifact to near zero — what accommodation law has always demanded but teacher time could never supply. Tooling splits between general models repurposed for access and platforms with dedicated "text-leveler" and "IEP-assistant" functions.

**Evidence:** The strongest causally identified evidence comes from the Global South. The World Bank's Edo State, Nigeria evaluation found personalized generative tutoring improved outcomes most for students furthest behind, with effect sizes among the largest in the ed-tech literature. The causal mechanism: baseline learning poverty concentrates the most severe gaps in the lowest-performing students, so an adaptive tutor that meets each learner at their actual level delivers its largest marginal gain precisely to that tail — the "teaching at the right level" logic validated offline in India's J-PAL trials.

**The central tension:** The population least able to detect a hallucinated simplification is exactly the population this category serves. A faithful simplification can fail silently when the model drops a load-bearing concept to hit a readability target — and **only a subject-expert teacher can catch it**, reinstating the verification burden at its most acute. IEP/504 data are also among the most sensitive any school holds.

**Verdict:** **Highest equity ceiling and steepest readiness floor** — the lowest pass of the twelve, specialist-gated and compliance-coupled. The strong effect sizes are concentrated in low-baseline Global South settings and **have not been replicated for diagnosed special-education populations in high-income systems.** Closing that gap is a governance problem before a technology one.

## 4. Case Studies

### 4.1 UK LearnLM × Eedi RCT — The Strongest Student-Facing Evidence

As of late 2025, the joint Google LearnLM / Eedi trial is **the most methodologically rigorous public evidence** that an LLM-based conversational tutor can raise in-session learning outcomes in real classrooms without degrading safety.

The design: a two-arm RCT in UK secondary mathematics. When a student answered a diagnostic question incorrectly, they were randomized to either a LearnLM Socratic tutor or a business-as-usual control showing a static worked explanation. The intervention coupled the model to **Eedi's misconception-tagged item bank** — giving the tutor a diagnosed error to remediate rather than an open prompt.

**Two load-bearing outcomes:**
- **Efficacy:** A measurable in-session gain — students receiving conversational remediation were meaningfully more likely to answer the next, structurally matched item correctly.
- **Safety:** A transcript review found the rate of harmful, off-task, or age-inappropriate content very low, supporting that the tutor "can safely and effectively support students."

The separation of an efficacy metric (next-item accuracy) from a safety metric (incident rate) in a single randomized frame is the design's signature contribution — most K-12 studies report one or the other, rarely both.

**The structural insight that reframes the field:** The binding constraint on AI tutoring is **not model capability but the diagnostic infrastructure and safety assurance wrapped around it.** Eedi's misconception taxonomy, not the Gemini base model, is what turns a generic conversational agent into a measurably effective ITS.

**What it does *not* yet license:** The gain is on an *immediately following isomorphic item*, not a delayed post-test — so durable retention and far transfer remain genuinely open. A second caveat: the safety claim is vendor-internal (LearnLM sits inside Google), warranting independent replication. The readiness precondition is a misconception-tagged item bank most schools do not possess — implying that 2026–2028 diffusion of classroom tutoring will favor vendors who own diagnostic item banks over those who ship raw models.

### 4.2 PAIRR — Sequencing Over Model Quality

The PAIRR model (Peer-and-AI Review and Reflection) is a three-stage revision cycle: peers comment first, an LLM generates formative feedback second, and the student writes a reflection reconciling the two streams. Originating in university business-writing, its K-12 placement (secondary ELA, grades 7–12) is a reasoned downward extrapolation, not a tested fact.

The protocol's defining feature: **the AI never delivers a verdict the student must obey** — it supplies a second, contrasting reviewer whose disagreement with peers becomes the object of reflection. The reflection step deputizes the student as first-line verifier, **relocating** rather than eliminating the verification burden. This works only when students are competent enough to catch a confident wrong answer — which younger or lower-attaining writers cannot reliably do.

**The deeper design divergence with LearnLM:** LearnLM embeds pedagogy *inside the model* through instruction tuning; PAIRR embeds pedagogy *inside the classroom sequence* and treats the model as interchangeable. **LearnLM bets on a better tutor; PAIRR bets on a better workflow.** For systems wary of vendor lock-in, PAIRR's model-agnostic posture is a strategic advantage. The structural truth PAIRR demonstrates: **in formative writing feedback, the sequencing of human and machine review determines learning value more than the raw quality of the AI reviewer.**

### 4.3 MIT Media Lab TTIG Co-Design (K-6 Art) — Process Evidence, Thin Outcomes

A qualitative, participatory design study positioning K-6 children as **design partners** who shape the interface and surface their own mental models of how an image generator "thinks." Reading it against the experimental cases exposes where the Evidence Strength criterion is weakest for arts AIGC.

The honest evidentiary position: this is a **small-N, design-based study** (one to three classrooms, ~20–60 children) producing design artifacts, interaction transcripts, and qualitative shifts in children's articulated mental models — **not standardized learning gains with an effect size.** This is the decisive contrast with the siblings: the LearnLM RCT reports randomized outcomes; this case offers rich process evidence and thin outcome metrics.

**The load-bearing conclusion:** The TTIG case supplies **the strongest process evidence and the weakest outcome evidence** in the entire case-study block — and the two facts are related, not contradictory. Participatory co-design trades statistical power for depth of insight into children's reasoning. It answers "how do young children make sense of generative images?" while the RCT siblings answer "does the tool move a measured outcome?"

The documented gains (improved articulation of how models recombine learned patterns; iterative design-thinking discipline; widened expressive access for children whose drawing skills lag their imagination) all rest on qualitative coding. The central tension is **developmental appropriateness vs. creative openness**, resolved by a constrained prompt vocabulary and teacher-mediated review — tighter guardrails at the youngest ages, loosening as children mature.

### 4.4 Nigeria / Edo State — The Global South Proof-of-Concept

The hardest test of whether AIGC delivers where infrastructure and teacher capacity are most constrained. The World Bank's Edo State after-school pilot — secondary students using Microsoft Copilot under teacher guidance for six weeks — reported learning gains **equivalent to nearly two years of ordinary schooling**, among the most cost-effective interventions the Bank has measured.

The design used AIGC as a **per-student amplifier of practice volume** for English writing, with the teacher remaining the pedagogical anchor — a contrast with the autonomous LearnLM model. The teacher authored a reusable instructional prompt once per week, front-loading oversight cost into design rather than paying it per interaction. This is what resolves the apparent paradox between minimal-supervision efficiency and maximal-oversight safety: **reusable prompt scaffolding, not heroic real-time monitoring.**

**The causal pathway:** Low baseline access → large untapped practice deficit → introducing a per-student tutor produces disproportionate marginal gains → the headline two-years effect. This explains why the same tool yields *smaller* numbers in saturated UK/US contexts.

**The structural claim across all cases:** AIGC is **not a uniform intervention but a baseline-sensitive amplifier** — and the Global South is where its amplification is largest, precisely because the counterfactual (no individualized practice at all) is so weak.

| Geography | Delivery Model | Reported Outcome | Baseline |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nigeria / Edo State | Teacher-supervised after-school Copilot | ~2 years of learning in ~6 weeks | Low |
| UK (LearnLM) | Autonomous tutor (ITS) | Modest, conditional gains | High |
| USA | Structured-AI platforms | Workload savings (~6 hrs/week) | High |
| Finland/Estonia | Policy-led literacy integration | Capability, not test-score | High |

The forward implication for donor and ministry strategy: if marginal return tracks the baseline deficit, the most defensible allocation of scarce budgets in low-income systems is **teacher-supervised, device-pooled AIGC tutoring of the Edo type**, not autonomous or platform models presupposing infrastructure these systems lack. The open question: whether the Edo effect survives the transition from supervised pilot to routine, unmonitored national rollout.

### 4.5 First-Grade Science Generation (PLOS ONE) — The Hardest Case

Six- and seven-year-olds cannot read dense text, cannot self-correct a hallucinated fact, and depend entirely on the teacher as the verification layer. This study tests whether an LLM can draft a developmentally appropriate, standards-aligned science lesson for the youngest learners, judged usable by expert raters.

**The headline finding:** Generated lessons score well on **surface structure and standards alignment** but weaker on **developmental calibration and quality of hands-on inquiry** — precisely the dimensions requiring tacit knowledge of how six-year-olds behave. **LLMs reliably produce the scaffold of a lesson and unreliably produce its pedagogical soul.** The contrarian finding: simplicity of content *masks* the difficulty of developmental fit; the youngest band is where generic generation degrades fastest.

**The inverted risk gradient:** The Risk/Age-Appropriateness criterion **fails whenever teacher verification is absent and passes only conditionally** when a subject-confident teacher mediates every artifact. This is the sharpest instance of Conditional Efficacy in the report: the tool's value is not intrinsic but contingent on the mediating adult's capacity. The portable lesson: the durable skill for first-grade teachers is not prompt authoring but **developmental review** — the judgment to catch what the model cannot know about a six-year-old.

### 4.6 Comparative Geography — China/Taiwan, Europe, Vietnam/APEC, USA

These cases establish a single structural claim: **governance regime, not model capability or cultural heritage, is the decisive variable in K-12 adoption.**

**China vs. Taiwan** share language, exam intensity, and Confucian-heritage pedagogy, yet diverge entirely on policy architecture. Mainland China runs a **state-steered walled-garden** model — domestic LLMs, exam-aligned generation, centralized infrastructure — achieving adoption scale that vastly outruns its published causal evidence. Taiwan inverts this: **open Western models, high teacher autonomy, AI Literacy as Content** earlier in the grade sequence, importing the full Western risk profile (hallucination, integrity) managed through professional norms rather than state curation.

**Europe** proves that governance design, not tool supply, decides adoption. Finland runs a **teacher-discretion** model with strong GDPR guardrails (educator-side benefit: planning quality, not test-score movement). Estonia's **AI Leap** programme is Europe's broadest national student-facing rollout, enabled by pre-existing digital-state infrastructure — but creating vendor dependence and transferring poorly to low-infrastructure contexts. Germany (federal/heterogeneous), France (centralized/cautious), and the Netherlands (vetting-based/pragmatic) resolve the same tension along three distinct regulatory vectors.

**Vietnam/APEC** represents a third trajectory — **"mandate-first, capacity-later."** Top-down national AI strategy provides political cover while teacher training and oversight lag. Teachers self-restrict AIGC to low-stakes prep tasks they can personally audit, and that self-imposed scoping becomes the de facto safety control in the absence of formal governance. The binding constraint is teacher professional judgment, not regulation.

**The USA** is the world's largest **bottom-up, teacher-driven** adoption — distinguished by rapid commercial diffusion (MagicSchool, SchoolAI) and the Edutopia 80/20 heuristic, where adoption outran the evidence base. Millions of educators scaled before any randomized outcome study published — defining both the opportunity and the governance risk.

The cross-case pattern: **effect size moves inversely with baseline system wealth**, and the single strongest predictor of a positive learning outcome across all cases is **the presence of a formative feedback loop with a human checkpoint — not the sophistication of the model.**

## 5. The Tool Landscape

The K-12 AIGC market splits into two architecturally distinct classes:

**Structured education platforms** (MagicSchool, SchoolAI, Diffit, Brisk, Khanmigo) wrap a frontier LLM inside teacher-defined boundaries — the opposite design philosophy from open-prompt chatbots. They collapse the workflow into guided templates, lowering the prompt-engineering barrier that is the single largest adoption obstacle. They lead on workload reduction (~6 hrs/week) and onboarding ease.

**Frontier general-purpose models** (ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude; DALL·E, Midjourney, Stable Diffusion) handle the widest task span and hold the capability ceiling for multimodal and STEM generation, but demand genuine prompt-engineering skill, active verification, and data-governance review.

**The landscape's binding constraint:** only **23% of the top 2026 EdTech AI tools disclose which auditable LLM they run** — meaning most procurement decisions are made blind to the underlying model. Until this rises, every safety verdict remains vendor-asserted rather than independently auditable.

| Dimension | Structured Platforms | Frontier Models |
|---|---|---|
| Model transparency | Low (23% disclose) | Low–variable |
| Workload saving | ~6 hrs/wk | Versatile, unmeasured |
| Verification burden | Contained | Peak |
| Onboarding | Low-training | High-training |
| Hallucination exposure | Bounded | Unbounded |

**The structural verdict:** The market is split not by capability but by **where the verification burden and governance cost land** — structured platforms move them onto the vendor; frontier models leave them on the teacher. This explains why workload-saving evidence concentrates in the structured class while capability ceilings sit with frontier models. Both classes converge on a single mandatory verification node before delivery, making the Teacher Verification Burden the shared chokepoint regardless of tool choice.

## 6. Implementation Methods

### 6.1 Prompt Engineering (PARTS)

Prompt design is the single highest-leverage skill separating teachers who extract reliable value from those who abandon the tools. Output quality tracks **input specificity far more than model choice**. The PARTS schema decomposes every effective prompt into five components:

- **Persona** — the role the model adopts ("experienced Year-4 science teacher"); shifts the model toward a Socratic posture
- **Aim** — the concrete artifact or task
- **Recipient** — grade band and reading level; **the most equity-consequential slot**, since omitting it pitches text at the proficient minority and strands the ~70% reading below proficiency
- **Theme** — curriculum topic and standards alignment
- **Structure** — format, length, output scaffold; fix tightly for assessment artifacts, loosen for brainstorming

PARTS is not a one-shot method but an **iteration loop**: inspect output, diagnose which slot underperformed, edit that slot. This discipline directly manages the verification burden — a well-specified prompt narrows the model's latitude, shrinking checking time and making routine use sustainable. The contrarian de-skilling worry (that templates erode judgment) resolves in favor of augmentation when schools treat templates as **scaffolds teachers are trained to critique and override**, not fixed scripts. The teach-the-slots-not-the-tool principle gives PARTS strong Global Transferability, since prompt fluency generalizes across the entire 300+-tool landscape.

### 6.2 SAMR Integration Depth and GenAI-TPACK

Two frameworks govern whether AIGC lands as a cheap photocopier or a genuine redesign: **SAMR** (Substitution → Augmentation → Modification → Redefinition) grades integration depth; **GenAI-TPACK** tests whether technological, pedagogical, and content knowledge actually intersect.

**The central claim: AIGC's measured learning value rises only when integration climbs the SAMR ladder AND the teacher's GenAI-TPACK is intact** — the two are coupled, not independent. Higher SAMR rungs correlate with larger documented gains (Edo State reached Modification and reported ~2-year gains; lower-rung Substitution mainly saves drafting time). But without intact TPACK, high-SAMR tasks degrade into unsupervised chatbot use.

| SAMR rung × TPACK state | Outcome | Verification burden |
|---|---|---|
| Redefinition + intact TPACK | Highest learning gain | Managed via graded student checking |
| Redefinition + weak TPACK | Unsupervised chatbot drift | Unmanaged, high risk |
| Augmentation + intact TPACK | Safe, reliable support | Low, tool-bounded |
| Substitution + any TPACK | Time saved only | Minimal |

**The paradox:** climbing SAMR raises both learning value *and* hallucination exposure, since open-ended Redefinition gives the model more latitude. This resolves through **staged oversight** — higher-rung tasks are deliberately paired with mandatory student source-checking, converting verification into a graded AI Literacy exercise rather than a hidden teacher cost.

### 6.3 The Readiness Gate and Decision Guide

A scored go/no-go instrument across five dimensions, each with a red/amber/green threshold; a deployment proceeds only when no dimension sits red:

| Dimension | Red (block) | Amber (pilot) | Green (deploy) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Teacher workload | Net hours added | Pilot with coaching | Verified net time-saving |
| Student age | <13, no consent flow | 13–15 supervised | 16+ or COPPA-compliant |
| Infrastructure | No reliable devices | Shared-device lab | 1:1 reliable access |
| Curriculum fit | No standards alignment | Mapped to one unit | Mapped across scope/sequence |
| Training | No GenAI-TPACK | Basic prompt workshop | PARTS-fluent, verification-trained |

**The structural claim: AIGC deployment readiness is a property of the school system, not the tool.** A tool scoring green on every decision-guide row still fails if the gate shows red on infrastructure or training — the same tool that produced Edo State gains would under-deliver without provisioned devices and trained facilitators. This reframes procurement from "which tool is best" to "**is our system ready for this goal**."

The companion **goal-to-approach decision guide** ties each classroom goal to its highest-evidence approach and oversight burden. The governing rule that survives every case study: **the higher the student-facing autonomy of the tool, the higher the required teacher oversight — and the two must be provisioned together or not at all.** Teachers should start from the instructional goal and read across, not browse the 300+-tool marketplace; a goal-first search biases toward Redefinition, where the learning value concentrates.

## 7. The Effectiveness Evidence

Evidence of effectiveness is uneven, tier-stratified, and strongest where the tool augments teacher judgment rather than replaces it. The headline practitioner finding stays constant: **generative AI reliably reduces teacher workload and raises engagement, but direct learning gains remain conditional on pedagogical design and the verification burden.**

### 7.1 Reconciling Meta-Analytic Optimism with Supervised Trials

The central tension: aggregate reviews and single-classroom trials answer **different questions**. A meta-analysis estimates an *average* effect across heterogeneous implementations; a supervision-dependent RCT estimates a *conditional* effect under one tightly specified deployment. Reading the first as if it answered the second is the most common interpretive error in the field.

The meta-analytic camp (Nature 2026; Deng et al.; Wang) reports a consistent achievement advantage for AI-supported instruction. But these aggregates describe deployments by *motivated researchers*, not the unsupervised default a busy classroom produces — and several influential aggregates (Sun & Zhou) are **college samples**, not K-12. The pooled studies also draw overwhelmingly on *untuned* general LLMs, understating what a tuned system like LearnLM achieves and overstating what unsupervised deployment delivers.

The supervised counterweight is sharpest in tutoring: AIED 2025 found the AI-tutor benefit is **materially amplified when a human tutor is in the loop**; Tutor CoPilot showed real-time LLM guidance to novice tutors raised outcomes most for the lowest-rated tutors. The UK RCT is the pivotal K-12 point — reliability held *because* the design embedded human oversight as a precondition.

**The reconciliation is coherent, not contradictory:** The meta-analyses establish the **ceiling** of AIGC-supported instruction sits above business-as-usual; the supervised RCTs establish the **conditions** required to reach it. The committed verdict: **the evidence supports deploying generative AI under active teacher supervision, and does not support autonomous student use as a reliability-equivalent substitute.**

### 7.2 The Writing-Feedback Sub-Debate

Writing is where the conditional pattern is most densely evidenced — and where the scope line must be policed most carefully (the strongest randomized trials, FeedbackWriter and the "revise less" study, are undergraduate). The genuine K-12 signal: the University of Vienna secondary-school result, where AI feedback raised revision, motivation, and positive emotions.

**The defining risk — the agency tradeoff:** More abundant, fluent AI feedback can *reduce* the cognitive work of revision the student performs. Expected outcome: richer feedback → deeper revision. Actual outcome (Springer comparison): more feedback → *shallower* revision, because students offload evaluative judgment to the model. The resolution: feedback must be sequenced to **demand learner interpretation before it supplies answers** — exactly the PAIRR logic. **Volume of feedback is not the active ingredient; revision depth is.**

| Evidence tier | Cohort | Effect | K-12 applicability |
|---|---|---|---|
| Meta-analysis (broad) | Mixed/tertiary-heavy | Positive average | Conditional |
| Meta-analysis (math) | School-age | Positive, moderated | High |
| Meta-analysis (HE) | College | Positive | Adapted only |
| Supervised RCT | K-12 math | Positive *with oversight* | Direct |
| Writing (K-12) | Secondary | Positive | Direct |
| "Revise less" | Higher ed | More feedback, shallower revision | Cautionary |

**The through-line:** applicability rises as supervision and K-12 specificity rise together, and the single cautionary row warns that abundance of AI output is not the same as depth of learning. Educators should read every "outperforms" headline as carrying an implicit clause — *under the supervised, task-structured conditions the study actually tested.* **What remains unresolved:** no large pre-registered K-12 RCT isolates the *unsupervised* student-use condition at scale, so the true floor of autonomous use — as opposed to its supervised ceiling — remains estimated, not measured.

## 8. Risks, Ethics, Equity, and Privacy

Risk is not uniform but **category-specific**, and 2026 policymakers now treat it that way: 134 bills on AI in education were introduced across 31 states. The risk profile follows one structural rule: **risk rises monotonically with how directly generative output reaches the child and how young that child is.**

| Category | Dominant Risk | Most-Exposed Population |
|---|---|---|
| A Lesson Planning | Hallucinated facts (downstream) | Whole class |
| C ITS / LLM tutor | Overreliance, open-chat safety | Independent learners |
| E TTIG Image | Copyright, biased/unsafe imagery | Younger pupils |
| F Multimodal | Deepfake / voice cloning | All |
| H STEM/Math | Confident wrong worked-examples | Struggling students |
| K Admin/Comms | Student PII in prompts | Families (data) |
| L Inclusive/SpecEd | Accessibility false promise | Students with disabilities |

**The developmental-readiness inversion:** The youngest learners, least able to detect generated error, depend *most* on verification they cannot themselves supply — shifting teacher authority from content-deliverer to **error-gatekeeper**.

### 8.1 The Integrity–Agency Triad

Academic integrity, overreliance, and agency erosion are **not separate failure modes but three readings of one causal process**: lowered cognitive cost of delegation. This reframing predicts that any safeguard targeting only one output (plagiarism detection alone) will leave the others intact.

The integrity threat in K-12 writing is **invisible text substitution at the point of submission**. The remedy is a shift from policing the product to reading the **process**: tools like eRevise+RF log the revision trajectory, converting the integrity question from unanswerable to auditable. A platform that records the revision trajectory lets teachers grant access and retain accountability at once — the apparent either/or (block AI entirely vs. allow substitution) dissolves into a design requirement.

For tutoring, the safeguard against overreliance is structural: keep a human in the authority loop. Brief, well-timed human intervention recovers the self-regulation that unsupervised AI use erodes. The governing tradeoff — fully autonomous tutoring scales cheaply but cedes authority over minors; fully supervised tutoring preserves authority but forfeits the cost advantage — resolves in the **hybrid middle**: AI handles volume, a human retains calibration and safeguarding decisions.

### 8.2 Hallucination and the Verification Burden

Hallucination — confident production of plausible falsehood — is the single defect that most directly **converts an efficiency gain into a hidden labor cost**. Across categories, the verification burden is the governing variable: where a human checks every artifact before it reaches a child, reliability is recoverable; where output reaches the learner unmediated (Category C live tutoring), the risk compounds with no buffer.

Verification is not uniform — the cross-model accuracy study means the burden a teacher carries depends on which model sits behind the tool. The practical implication: a school standardizing on one *disclosed* model can build model-specific verification heuristics, which lets teachers verify faster and makes time savings real rather than nominal. A school using undisclosed or rotating models cannot capture this benefit.

| Risk dimension | Highest-exposure category | Governing mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| Hallucination | Live ITS tutoring | Human-in-loop hybrid |
| Data privacy | Differentiated generation, Admin | DPA + consented endpoints |
| Academic integrity | Writing feedback | Rubric-bounded scaffolds |
| Copyright | Material generation | Provenance metadata |
| Digital divide | All free-tier use | Licensed-tool equity funding |

**The single structural claim these risks establish together: the teacher buffer, not the model's quality, is the primary determinant of K-12 AI safety.** By 2030, the binding constraint on K-12 AIGC will be **governance maturity, not model quality** — districts that codify verification, consent, and provenance into procurement will absorb the verification burden as policy rather than as uncompensated teacher labor.

## 9. Future Trends Through 2030

Six forces are converging: multimodal tutors, teacher copilots, adaptive curriculum generation, localized models, AI literacy curricula, and regulation. The committed verdict: **model capability is no longer the bottleneck** — oversight protocols, teacher preparation, localization funding, and adaptive regulation jointly govern which trends reach classrooms.

| Trend | Maturity by 2030 | Binding Constraint |
|---|---|---|
| Multimodal AI tutors | High | Oversight protocol |
| Teacher copilots | High | Training |
| Adaptive curriculum | Medium | Curriculum oversight |
| Localized models | Med-low | Funding / AI Divide |

| Regulation | High | Evidence lag |

**The convergence:** By 2028, tutor and copilot surfaces likely merge into a single multimodal teacher-and-student assistant. The richer modalities expand both pedagogical value *and* the verification surface simultaneously — resolved not by eliminating error but by bounding the tutor's autonomy so a teacher remains the accountable checkpoint.

**The regulation paradox:** Regulation racing ahead of evidence may lock in restrictions before efficacy is known. The OECD favors **adaptive, iterative governance** over fixed statute, so rules can revise as the evidence base matures. Teacher preparedness remains the binding constraint everywhere — the ACE survey finds districts under-resourcing teachers.

## 10. Synthesis: Three Coupling Paths

The twelve categories **do not fail independently — they fail through three shared coupling paths**, and a category's maturity is determined less by tool sophistication than by whether its dominant path has a mitigation that survives a real classroom.

1. **Teacher Verification Burden** — the dominant chain for content-generation categories: default presentation-mode tuning → confident error → mandatory human review → redistributes rather than eliminates labor.
2. **Equity Divergence** — couples infrastructure to outcome: identical tools produce divergent outcomes because connectivity, device access, and teacher AI literacy gate the benefit.
3. **Agency Erosion** — the student-facing analogue: tutoring, writing, and math generation each risk substituting the AI's cognition for the learner's unless scaffolding forces productive struggle first.

**These paths are interactive, not additive:** a low-infrastructure school adopting a hallucination-prone tool compounds verification burden and equity divergence simultaneously — the worst-case quadrant.

### Maturity Tiers

| Tier | Categories | Dominant Path | Mitigation Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1 Mature** | A, K | Verification burden | Teacher-in-loop, bounded |
| **2 Emerging** | C, D, B, G | Agency + equity | Conditional, design-dependent |
| **3 Experimental** | E, F, H, J, I, L | Mixed / unaudited | Immature or upstream-gated |

**The external calibration:** Education sits roughly where clinical AIGC was *before* provenance mandates. The biomedical domain resolved its verification burden not by improving accuracy alone but by mandating human-in-the-loop sign-off and auditable provenance. The 23% model-disclosure rate is the exact auditability gap healthcare closed through regulation — suggesting Category J will not reach Tier 1 until an equivalent disclosure norm exists.

**The contrarian framing:** Despite the claim that more capable models will dissolve the readiness gap, the coupling-path analysis predicts the opposite for the trailing tier — stronger models raise output *plausibility* faster than output *verifiability*, widening the verification burden for exactly the categories (J, H, E) where a wrong answer is hardest to catch. **Capability and readiness are not the same axis.**

## 11. Recommendations

The governing principle is **alignment-first: the learning objective dictates the tool, never the reverse.**

**The forward verdict, ranked by readiness:**
- **Deploy now:** Teacher-facing planning and administrative generation (A, K), where a professional sits between model and child.
- **Deploy with guardrails:** Tutoring, writing feedback, differentiated material, language support (C, D, B, G) — validated but conditional.
- **Pilot and validate:** Image, multimodal, STEM generation, assessment, literacy-as-content, accessibility (E, F, H, J, I, L) — pending stronger evidence or disclosure norms.

**A prioritized action sequence, not a menu:** Start where oversight is cheapest and evidence strongest — teacher lesson-planning and admin generation — bank the time savings, then **reinvest freed hours into the high-oversight student-facing approaches** that carry larger learning upside but demand the verification capacity the first step builds. A school that inverts this — leading with student-facing tutoring before staff build GenAI-TPACK fluency — front-loads its highest risk against its lowest readiness.

**Three load-bearing rules:**
1. Use AIGC freely for generative-overhead tasks (drafting, leveling, formatting); withhold it from the tasks that constitute the assessed learning itself.
2. Treat every generative output as a draft to be verified, never a fact to be trusted — and teach students that same discipline as AI Literacy as Content.
3. Standardize on a small set of **model-disclosed, structured tools** rather than chasing the tool landscape's churn. Opacity is now a sufficient reason for exclusion regardless of feature appeal.

**The single most consequential action the evidence supports: invest in teacher GenAI-TPACK capacity *before*, not after, tool distribution** — the one lever that simultaneously shrinks the verification burden, narrows equity divergence, and blunts agency erosion across all twelve categories at once.

## 12. Limitations

**Evidence granularity:** Only tutoring, writing feedback, and two Global South math deployments clear the genuine-K-12-RCT bar. The remaining nine categories inherit tier placements from adapted higher-ed effect sizes, co-design vignettes, and practitioner reports — defensible as best-available synthesis but fragile under replication.

**Scope import:** Several pooled meta-analyses draw predominantly on undergraduate samples; their effect sizes are imported as upper-bound proxies for K-12 categories where school-age data is thin.

**Time validity:** Tool-specific claims carry an effective shelf life closer to twenty-four months. By roughly 2028, the maturity-tier verdict should be re-run against the then-current model generation.

**Geographic bias:** The under-represented population is the **low-connectivity, low-capacity Global South primary classroom** outside flagship pilots. The Nigeria/Sierra Leone evaluations are donor-funded programs with support scaffolding a typical school lacks, likely over-stating the transferable effect.

**Three falsifiers would overturn the verdict:**
1. A well-powered, pre-registered K-12 RCT showing null/negative tutoring effects would collapse Category C's tier placement.
2. Evidence that sustained tutor use measurably erodes independent problem-solving in primary pupils would invalidate the Pedagogical Learning Value pass.
3. A large offline-first deployment failing without connectivity and teacher-support scaffolding would refute the framework's implicit portability.

**The highest-value study the field could commission before 2028:** a multi-country, offline-first K-12 RCT that varies connectivity and teacher-support scaffolding as explicit treatment arms — simultaneously attacking the sampling bias, scope-import, and transferability gaps. **The single unresolved durability question:** no source establishes whether early-primary pupils *retain* AIGC-supported gains after the tool is withdrawn.

## 13. Conclusion

The central finding reorders the popular narrative. The strongest causal signal does not run from model quality to outcomes; it runs from **teacher orchestration and verification discipline to outcomes, with the generative model functioning as amplifier rather than agent.**

The two evidentiary poles — Edo State's outsized gains from teacher-mediated tutoring in a low-baseline context, and the USA's workload-concentrated 80/20 pattern — establish the report's most important comparative verdict: **generative AI's achievement effect is largest where baseline instruction is weakest and human mediation is strongest, not where technology access is highest.**

The equity verdict is real but strictly conditional. The same tool produces opposite effects depending on whether connectivity and teacher training **precede** deployment. Jurisdictions that invested in teacher capacity before scaling captured gains; those that led with devices did not. **The transferable asset is teacher capacity, not the tool license.**

The twelve categories, nine national cases, and two landmark trials cohere into one verdict: **AIGC is a force multiplier for good pedagogy and an accelerant for bad, and the variable that decides which is not the model but the teacher standing beside it.**

For practitioners, the synthesis is firm enough to act on now: **build the human scaffold first, then let the machine generate inside it.** Scaffold before generation, verification before trust, training before scale. The practitioner who internalizes that sequence will extract the differentiated reach, formative responsiveness, and workload relief the strongest cases document — while avoiding the overreliance, hallucination, and equity traps the risk analysis catalogues.

The field's next frontier is not a better model but a better account of the verification workflows that convert generative capability into durable learning — and a credible K-12 model-disclosure standard that no current source establishes. The tool has arrived; the profession's task is to govern it well, and the cases here show, concretely and repeatedly, what governing it well looks like in a real classroom with a real teacher making the final call on every generated line before it reaches a child who deserves to be taught the truth, verified, by a professional.
