# Contested Pasts: The Ideologisation, Instrumentalisation, and Reclaiming of History in Contemporary Memory Politics

History in the twenty-first century is being rewritten in real time by states, movements, and algorithms competing to control what the past is permitted to mean. The scale is systemic rather than episodic. By 2026 the documented battlefronts span at least six major state actors actively reconstructing national narratives — Britain's invented imperial tradition, Russia's Great Patriotic War cult, Turkey's neo-Ottoman recasting, Italy's fascist "both-sidesing," the United States' Lost Cause monuments, and Croatia's Ustasha revisionism — alongside more than 27 US state-level efforts to restrict the teaching of systemic racism and a UN resolution passed 123-to-3 declaring the transatlantic slave trade the gravest crime against humanity.

Against the liberal claim that post-1980 Europe had achieved a durable consensus on "coming to terms with the past," Gavriel Rosenfeld's 2023 account of **illiberal memory** shows that consensus fracturing into competitive national-victimhood narratives since the 2008 financial crisis. This report treats the reinterpretation of history as a **traceable political mechanism** rather than an abstract cultural drift, asking of each case not merely that a narrative changed but *who* changed it, *through what mechanism*, and *in whose present interest*.

# 1. Framework, Scope, and Vocabulary

The reinterpretation of history is a set of concrete, agent-driven operations performed on the past by identifiable actors using identifiable institutional channels. This chapter fixes the vocabulary, scope, and evaluative rubric by which those operations are judged across twelve national cases on four continents.

## 1.1 The Analytic Object

The report draws on three disciplines that rarely share a table: **memory studies** (how groups remember), **historiography** (how the past is disciplined into evidence-based reconstruction), and **political science** (who holds power and to what end). The case for combining them is that **no single discipline can trace a reinterpretation from political motive through institutional mechanism to historiographical effect.** A memory-studies reading of Argentina's ESMA site, inaugurated as a Dirty War memory site on 19 May 2015 by President Cristina Fernández de Kirchner, illuminates its symbolic charge but cannot explain why a later administration would defund the memory apparatus around it — that requires the political-science question of *cui bono*. Conversely, a purely political account of the Center for Legal and Social Studies' finding that Milei's administration dismantled memory-truth-justice policies within six months by June 2024 would miss the cultural-memory stakes of what was dismantled.

**Four objects must be kept rigorously apart.** The *event* is what occurred; the *narrative* is a structured account of it; *memory* is its socially held retention; *historiography* is its disciplined, evidence-tested reconstruction. The 1973 Chilean coup that ousted Salvador Allende and began a seventeen-year dictatorship is the event. The broadcast of Allende's last speech at the exact hour the bombing of La Moneda began, staged at the 2024 commemoration at the Estadio Nacional, is a narrative performance. The floral offerings from football fan clubs at that ceremony are collective memory. The scholarly reconstruction of casualty figures is historiography. **Conflating these is precisely the operation ideologisation exploits**: by presenting a narrative as the event, or a memory as historiography, actors borrow the authority of the past while evading its evidentiary discipline.

The objects respond to different pressures. The event is fixed. Historiography changes only when new evidence or better method warrants it — legitimate revision. **Memory and narrative change under present political pressure with no evidentiary trigger at all**, and it is this differential vulnerability the report is built to trace.

**The report does not narrate histories.** It analyses how pasts are being reinterpreted now, by whom, and through what channel. Dates and figures — the 1973 coup, the 16 December 2003 decree creating Argentina's Archivo Nacional de la Memoria — appear only as the object on which a reinterpretive operation works.

## 1.2 The Analytical Rubric

Every case is scored against a fixed six-item rubric that privileges **causal traceability over assertion**: a claim that a government distorted history counts for little unless the distorting mechanism and its beneficiary can be named.

| R-2 | Instrumentalisation traceability | 0.20 |

| R-4 | Commemoration–historiography linkage | 0.15 |

**Ideologisation depth** asks how completely inquiry has been subordinated to a closed frame; **instrumentalisation traceability** asks whether tactical deployment can be followed from motive to act; **reclamation nuance** asks whether recovery of a silenced narrative is emancipatory or merely substitutes one silencing for another. The Madres de Plaza de Mayo, whose white headscarf became a symbol after they began gathering in late April 1977, score high on reclamation nuance because their recovery of silenced victims created no countervailing silence.

The weights tilt deliberately toward traceability: **agenda-service clarity and instrumentalisation traceability each carry a fifth, and together with ideologisation depth account for three-fifths of any score.** Accusations of historical manipulation are cheap; what is scarce is a demonstrated pathway from a present interest through a named channel to a changed public account. The Chilean chain illustrates: a far-right parliamentary bloc led deputies of the Partido Nacional Libertario to present, on 5 June 2026, a resolution requesting President José Antonio Kast create a "Museum of Truth" focused on alleged abuses under Allende's Popular Unity government, aiming to relativise the existing Museo de la Memoria — an electoral shift leading to institutional proposal, leading to relativisation, leading to reopened contest.

**Distortion versus legitimate revision** turns on an evidentiary test, not political congeniality: legitimate revision is warranted by new evidence or improved method and survives scrutiny; distortion is warranted only by present convenience and collapses under it. India illustrates both edges. Renaming Bombay to Mumbai, Calcutta to Kolkata, and Allahabad to Prayagraj can be read as restoring indigenous linguistic identity — a defensible warrant. A government minister's 2018 declaration that Darwin's evolution was "scientifically wrong" because nobody had seen an ape turn into a man has no warrant and sits squarely on the distortion side. The rubric refuses congeniality as a criterion and demands the warrant be shown.

## 1.3 Key Terms

The report's vocabulary is a set of **paired oppositions**, each encoding a decision the analysis must make about every case. The working premise, from Maurice Halbwachs's collective-memory tradition, is that all remembering is socially framed.

**Lieu de mémoire vs. silencing.** A *lieu de mémoire* (Pierre Nora) is a site — material, symbolic, or functional — where a community's memory crystallises. *Trouillotian silencing* (Michel-Rolph Trouillot) is the production of historical absence at four moments: fact-making, fact-assembly, fact-retrieval, and retrospective significance. The Museo de la Memoria, with its mandate to dignify victims, is a lieu de mémoire; the BJP campaign to rename roads and monuments bearing Mughal names operates silencing at the retrieval moment, removing the cues by which a past is recalled. A lieu concentrates memory at a point; silencing evacuates it from a field.

**Multidirectional vs. competitive memory.** *Competitive memory* treats remembrance as zero-sum; *multidirectional memory* (Michael Rothberg) treats it as generative, so articulating one history supplies the vocabulary for another. The Chilean far right's proposed "Museum of Truth" is explicitly competitive, built to counter rather than complement.

**Ideologisation vs. instrumentalisation.** *Ideologisation* subordinates inquiry to a closed frame such that evidence is admitted only when it confirms the frame; *instrumentalisation* tactically deploys a historical reference for a present end, leaving the discipline nominally open. The operational test asks what happens to disconfirming evidence. Under ideologisation it is structurally excluded — the NCERT reduction of Delhi Sultanate and Mughal history to passing references removes the occasion to encounter it. Under instrumentalisation it survives but is sidelined — Argentina's 50th-anniversary video foregrounded a recovered granddaughter and a soldier's son as "the victims that wanted to hide", reframing without removing.

**Subaltern historiography vs. illiberal memory.** Both claim to recover a suppressed national past, but they diverge at the evidentiary test: *subaltern historiography* recovers the dominated against the elite record and survives scrutiny, whereas *illiberal memory* recovers a majoritarian identity against the evidentiary record and does not. **Cultural memory** (Jan Assmann) is the long-range, institutionally maintained memory carried across generations; **memory war** is a sustained public contest over which account holds official status. Argentina's Archivo Nacional de la Memoria is cultural memory; Milei's dismantling of memory policy is a turn toward illiberal memory; the contest between them is a memory war.

# 2. Conceptual Framework: Ideology, Memory, and the Production of History

We need a vocabulary precise enough to distinguish five things ordinary language collapses: the *event*, the *narrative*, the *memory*, the *ideology* that shapes which tellings are permitted, and the *historiography* that disciplines all of these against evidence.

## 2.1 History versus Memory: The Nora Distinction

Pierre Nora's "Between Memory and History" (1989) opposes living memory to the historian's reconstruction not in degree but in kind. **Lived memory is unconscious of itself; history is conscious of itself**. A family lighting an annual candle practises memory; it does not footnote the candle. History is the deliberate, evidence-disciplined reconstruction of a past recognised as *gone*.

The decisive move is diagnostic: **we speak obsessively of memory precisely because there is so little of it left**. Commemoration proliferates as a symptom of loss. When a past is still alive — transmitted automatically across generations — communities feel no urge to build monuments to it. **The contemporary explosion of memory politics is evidence not that the past is securely remembered but that its living transmission has broken down, opening the field to deliberate reconstruction.** This predicts that the most aggressive state memory construction clusters at generational thresholds — at the moment the last eyewitnesses die. By the early 2030s, the cohort with firsthand recollection of the Southern Cone dictatorships will have largely passed, and Nora's logic predicts the commemorative contest will *intensify* rather than settle.

Nora's term for the reconstructed anchor is the **lieu de mémoire**, set against the **milieu de mémoire**, the living social fabric in which a past is simply lived. The two are inversely related: **sites of memory crystallise precisely where the living environments dissolve.** The Chilean Museo de la Memoria, whose core was formed when the state donated the Casa de la Memoria archives on 16 June 2009, exists because the dictatorship generation's recollection cannot transmit unaided to Chileans born after 1990. But a substituted memory is *selectable* in a way a lived one is not: once a past must be housed in a museum, *which* version gets housed becomes a live political question — exactly the opening the Chilean far right exploited in 2026.

Nora's sharp binary has not survived intact. Where Nora treats archive and monument as dead residue, **Jan Assmann reclassifies them as *cultural memory* — a genuine mode of remembering**. Rothberg blurs the boundary differently: his **multidirectional memory** (2009) argues that the memory of one atrocity can *enable* rather than crowd out others — Holocaust memory supplied the idiom through which colonial and civil-rights histories found public voice. His later **implicated subject** (2019) challenges the victim–perpetrator–bystander triad, insisting people who are not direct agents may still benefit from regimes of domination. The cumulative effect converts Nora's binary into a spectrum: not memory *versus* history, but a graded field of remembering practices. This report adopts a **qualified Nora** — keeping his diagnostic insight while accepting that sites are themselves a mode of memory, not its grave.

## 2.2 Collective and Cultural Memory Theory

**Maurice Halbwachs** (1920s–30s) argued that human memory can only function within a collective context. The very *capacity* to remember depends on "social frameworks" — shared reference points, language, dates, group affiliations. This yields a causal chain with political consequences: when a group's framework shifts, the reconstructed memory shifts with it, which means **whoever controls the social framework exerts leverage over what a population can remember.** This is why textbook revision and renaming are not cosmetic: a state altering the framework available to a rising generation reshapes the machinery by which it will remember at all.

**Jan and Aleida Assmann** split Halbwachs's insight along a temporal axis. *Communicative memory* is everyday and lasts only as long as living people who can communicate it — roughly 80 to 100 years — whereas *cultural memory*, fixed in texts, rites, and monuments, can span up to three thousand years. The threshold between them is where political action concentrates: a traumatic event remains in communicative memory for about three generations, after which it must either be *promoted* into cultural memory through institutionalisation or fade. **The promotion is never neutral: fixing an event in textbook, monument, or ritual is the moment a political authority decides which version enters the long archive and which dies with its witnesses.** Argentina's 1976–1983 dictatorship sits at the outer edge of communicative memory, and the state has repeatedly acted to fix it before that register expires — Decree 1259/2003 creating the Archivo, the 2015 ESMA inauguration.

The premise uniting these theories — and on which the whole report depends — is that **collective memory is reconstruction oriented to the present, not retrieval of a fixed deposit.** The past is rebuilt each time it is recalled, to the specifications of the moment of recall. This licenses the distinction between the *event* (the bombing of La Moneda on 11 September 1973) and the *mobilised past* (the reconstruction built from it for present use). The same event supports incompatible reconstructions: the 2024 commemoration reconstructed the coup as martyrdom, while the 2026 "Museum of Truth" reconstructs the *Allende years* as the abuse. One event, two present-oriented reconstructions.

A caution: the reconstruction thesis can slide into relativism. The resolution is the hard boundary historiography supplies — reconstruction is present-oriented but **not unconstrained**, because the documentary record can *contradict* a reconstruction even when it cannot dictate a single one. All reconstructions are present-serving, *and* some are answerable to evidence while others are insulated from it; the second half separates legitimate reinterpretation from ideologisation.

## 2.3 The Contested Concept of Ideology

The Stanford Encyclopedia registers the difficulty bluntly: **the uses of "ideology" are so divergent as to make it doubtful there is any conceptual unity**. In its *pejorative* sense, calling a belief ideological is an accusation; in its *descriptive* sense it names any organised belief system. The report uses **ideologisation** in the pejorative sense: the subordination of inquiry to a closed frame insulated from contradicting evidence. The NCERT reduction registers as ideologisation not merely because it expresses a viewpoint (every selection does) but because the selection is insulated from the evidentiary weight of centuries of documented administration.

**Vasiliev's 2024 typology** supplies the operational distinction, separating *ideological distortions* (Eurocentrism, legal idealism) from *errors* and *deliberate fraud*. An error is correctable by better evidence; fraud is knowing fabrication; an ideological distortion is a systematic bias built into the interpretive frame, persisting even when every individual fact is accurate. The blade the report applies throughout: the question is never whether an account serves someone's interest (all do) but **whether its frame remains answerable to evidence.**

Can any history escape ideology? The honest answer is no — Vasiliev states the ideological factor cannot be eliminated completely, and working historians corroborate it from inside the discipline. But the impossibility of *zero* ideology does not imply the equivalence of *all* ideological loadings, any more than friction in every machine makes all machines equally efficient. **The report replaces the unattainable standard of *objectivity* with the attainable standard of *answerability*:** a history need not be free of interest to be disciplined; it need only remain open to correction by evidence it did not choose. This is the escape from the relativist trap.

## 2.4 Historiography as a Field of Power

Trouillot locates silencing at **four moments: fact creation (making sources), fact assembly (making archives), fact retrieval (making narratives), and retrospective significance (making history)**. The power of this schema is that by the time a historian writes, three prior silencings may already have occurred. **Silencing is not a single act of suppression but a cumulative filtering across four stages**, so that by the time a silence appears "natural," it has been produced four times over, and each production point is a site where power, not evidence, did the selecting.

This governs reclamation directly. A recovery mounted only at the narration stage cannot undo a silencing that occurred at the creation stage where no source was ever made. The implication is a hierarchy of difficulty: recoveries targeting retrieval and retrospective significance are tractable, while creation-stage silences face near-irreversible absence.

The **archive** is where power asymmetry becomes structural: it is a *selected* record of what someone with authority chose to keep. The deliberate construction of counter-archives is the structural response. Decree 1259/2003 built the Archivo Nacional de la Memoria to hold evidence the perpetrating regime had every incentive to destroy; the Chilean donation of the Casa de la Memoria archives does the same. But the counter-archive is reversible: six months into Milei's presidency, his administration had dismantled key memory-truth-justice policies, firing most experts and refusing to comply with reparation laws. **A counter-archive is only as durable as the coalition willing to fund it; reversing Trouillotian silencing is not a one-time achievement but a standing political commitment.**

The Foucauldian tradition adds a double reading: the genealogist asks not "is this document accurate?" but "what arrangement of power made it exist, and what did its existence exclude?" The British colonial historiography that divided Indian history into Hindu, Muslim, and British periods across a two-thousand-year linear narrative is precisely a document whose *categories* were products of imperial power. The contest now occurs not only in archives but in the public arena where political actors, courts, media, and social networks compete to define meaning.

# 3. The Ideologisation of History

Ideologisation is the most aggressive of the three narrative moves because it does not merely deploy the past tactically but attempts to redefine what counts as historical truth. **It is distinguished from instrumentalisation by ambition: instrumentalisation borrows the past for a present fight, whereas ideologisation rebuilds the past so that only one present fight is thinkable.**

## 3.1 Mechanisms of Ideological Reshaping

Ideologisation rarely fabricates wholesale; its preferred instruments are **selection and proportion**, operating on materials that are individually defensible so the distortion lives in the aggregate composition. Three channels dominate: **selective emphasis** (foregrounding flattering episodes, silencing inconvenient ones), **myth-making** (converting a contingent event into a sacred origin), and **curriculum design** (the delivery system, since schooling is compulsory and early). A 2026 Routledge analysis documents the European far right instrumentalising twentieth-century authoritarian lieux de mémoire to boost legitimacy and construct exclusionary narratives.

**The decisive feature is that channel-based ideologisation weaponises the credibility of true facts:** every component claim can survive fact-checking while the assembled narrative systematically misleads, which is why piecemeal debunking fails and only structural critique of the selection principle succeeds.

The causal chain runs: a prior worldview supplies criteria of relevance; those criteria drive selection; the selected narrative saturates discourse through schooling, media, and ceremony; and saturation naturalises it so its governed origin becomes invisible. Rosenfeld supplies the input end — right-wing populists developed an illiberal politics of memory opposing global liberal memory culture, a byproduct of rising illiberal democracy since 2008. The broader collection documents the downstream effect: since the 2010s, populist nationalisms have reshaped memory culture toward narratives of national victimhood and masculine racial-religious supremacy. **The naturalisation link is where ideologisation completes itself**: citizens no longer experience a selected narrative as one interpretation but as the unmarked default against which alternatives appear as motivated revisionism.

Not every departure from neutrality is ideologisation. Vasiliev's triage separates *bias* (a directional tilt the discipline's procedures can surface), *error* (an honest mistake), and *distortion* (knowing subordination to a predetermined conclusion). **The operative test is falsifiability of the frame itself**: a biased historiography open to disconfirming evidence is doing normal self-correcting work, whereas an ideologised one treats its conclusion as axiomatic and recodes disconfirming evidence as enemy propaganda. The state narratives below all fail this test.

## 3.2 Nationalism and the Heroic National Past

Nationalism is the dominant supplier of worldview-inputs, and the heroic national past is its characteristic product. **Benedict Anderson's** imagined community of strangers requires a shared narrative to be imaginable at all: absent face-to-face acquaintance, the bond is a common story of origin, continuity, and destiny. Because no living person remembers the nation's founding centuries, this national biography must be carried by cultural memory. **The imagined community is structurally dependent on continuous narrative production, which makes the national past permanently available for political reshaping:** because the story must be retold to survive, every retelling is an opportunity to revise.

**Hobsbawm and Ranger's** invented traditions (1983) are cultural practices presented as ancient but in fact recent and consciously invented. The volume's case studies — Welsh and Scottish national culture, British royal ritual — show invention operating at the symbolic core of a powerful state, and the book has accumulated over seven thousand citations. The load-bearing claim is that **the density of tradition-invention spikes in periods of rapid social transformation**: rapid change dissolves older bonds, generating demand for new integrative symbols, driving elites to manufacture continuity. Where Hobsbawm and Ranger emphasise elite manufacture from above, Anderson grants more popular agency to the act of imagining.

The **school textbook** is the most powerful instrument for converting an invented past into durable common sense, because it reaches every citizen during formative years with the state's imprimatur. **What makes it uniquely dangerous is its combination of universality, authority, and timing: it reaches everyone, speaks with the state's voice, and arrives before the critical faculties that might resist it are fully formed.** By 2030, textbook battles in India, the United States, and the illiberal regimes below are likely to intensify, since the same digital infrastructures that allow rapid dissemination also allow rapid centralised revision.

## 3.3 Illiberal and Authoritarian Memory Regimes

Illiberal memory is best understood as a **reaction-formation** against a prior dominant culture. Rosenfeld dates it as a byproduct of illiberal democracy's rise since 2008. The liberal culture it reacts against is the post-1980s consensus organised around the confession of national guilt. **The defining move is the inversion of the liberal confession of guilt into a performance of victimhood.** Where liberal memory asked nations to confess guilt, illiberal memory performs victimhood; where liberal memory commemorated victims of one's own nation's crimes, illiberal memory commemorates the nation itself as victim. A 2023 Cambridge study documents Central European populists performing victimhood through the political exploitation of historical injustice; a 2020 Frontiers analysis shows the 2008 financial crisis and the EU migrant crisis functioning as narrative devices heightening xenophobic anxieties. A 2026 De Gruyter analysis identifies why the inversion is potent: it supplies populism with the friend/enemy dichotomy its moralising appeal depends on.

### Russia: The Great Patriotic War as State-Managed Ideology

Russia is the most fully developed authoritarian memory regime, a case where a single episode has become the load-bearing legitimation of the entire political order.

- **Context.** A consolidated authoritarian regime whose memory politics legitimise indefinite rule and, since 2014, justify aggression against Ukraine. For Putin's leadership, World War II and the Stalin era have become "increasingly a political weapon used to legitimise their rule and mobilise the population". The Centre for Eastern Studies frames the strategy as memory in the service of "eternal" authoritarianism.
- **Mechanism.** A dense battery of channels: state-managed commemoration (Victory Day), school history, monumental construction, and memory laws criminalising dissent. The mechanism is recursive — each annual cycle re-naturalises the narrative. The war memory holds "cult-like status," and the government has "co-opted and instrumentalised" Soviet heroism and victimhood. The nationalisation of war memory accelerated markedly after 2014.
- **Narrative move.** **Ideologisation in its purest form.** The regime does not borrow the war for a present argument but rebuilds the record around a sacralised core that cannot be questioned. Soviet sacrifice becomes an axiom from which dissent is recoded as treason — the falsifiability failure made total.
- **Actors.** The central state — presidency, education ministry, state media — enlisting veterans' organisations, the Orthodox Church, and youth movements to simulate spontaneous devotion.
- **Flashpoint.** Victory Day, 9 May; the 80th anniversary in 2025 concentrated the state's investment. The Red Square parade, Immortal Regiment processions, and ribbon of St George renew the narrative.
- **Scholarly frame.** The **weaponisation of antifascism**: since the 2014 annexation, Russian discourse has framed Ukraine through the language of "fascism" and "Nazism", mobilising the anti-fascist victory's moral authority against a contemporary adversary.
- **Consequence.** Severe distortion, foreclosure of pluralist debate, and direct effects on aggression. **The conversion of war memory into an unfalsifiable state axiom is the report's clearest demonstration that ideologisation can move from cultural management to active war justification.** As the generation with living communicative memory passes, the entirely cultural-memory character of the cult will deepen, removing the last experiential check.

### Turkey: The AKP's Ottoman Revival

Turkey reaches past the secular republic to an imperial past, reideologising the national story by relocating its golden age.

- **Context.** A competitive-authoritarian regime (AKP in power since 2002) supplanting Kemalist republican identity with a neo-Ottoman, Islamic-conservative one.
- **Mechanism.** State communications, commemoration, monumental construction, and education. A dedicated Directorate of Communications systematically propagates the imperial narrative; the cultural field, from television drama to spectacle, saturates discourse with valorising imagery.
- **Narrative move.** **Ideologisation through revalorisation.** The republican narrative coded the Ottoman past as a backward prelude; the AKP inverts that coding, recasting the empire as a lost golden age and the secular foundation as a deviation from the nation's deeper Islamic-imperial essence. **This is the report's clearest instance of a regime ideologising against itself** — targeting not a foreign enemy but the nation's own immediately preceding regime.
- **Actors.** The AKP and state cultural apparatus, plus religious institutions, pro-government media, and public intellectuals supplying scholarly cover.
- **Flashpoint.** The 1453 conquest of Constantinople; the 2020 conversion of Hagia Sophia from museum to mosque functioned as a lieu de mémoire of supreme density.
- **Scholarly frame.** Invented tradition (a curated imperial nostalgia presented as authentic inheritance) combined with illiberal memory.
- **Consequence.** Polarisation between secular and Islamic-conservative constituencies. **Illiberal memory need not sacralise a recent event in the Russian manner but can relocate the golden age into the deeper past.** As the cohort schooled within the neo-Ottoman frame reaches maturity, the contest will increasingly be settled by generational replacement rather than argument.

Together, the two cases prove **illiberal memory is a transposable template, not a single national pathology**: the same reaction-formation against liberal memory culture attaches to wholly different historical contents.

## 3.4 Culture Wars and the Race/Class Politics of History

The same mechanisms operate in pluralist democracies where no single actor monopolises the narrative, fought most fiercely over the curriculum. The **1619 Project**, reframing the US origin story around enslaved Africans, ignited a sweeping backlash: Chalkbeat tracked at least 27 state-level efforts to restrict discussion of systemic racism, critical race theory, and the Project — more than half the states legislating the boundaries of permitted discussion. An EdWorkingPaper found Americans maintain strong support for antiracist teaching, but support drops sharply when the curriculum is labelled "critical race theory". **The same content commands majority support or opposition depending solely on the label, which proves the conflict is fought at the level of naturalised framing rather than pedagogical substance** — the naturalisation link operating in real time.

**Curriculum battles are proxy wars** because the school is the chokepoint of cultural-memory transmission: whoever controls what is taught controls which version becomes the next generation's unmarked default. The Harvard Allen Lab documents Texas legislators introducing "essential knowledge bills" and "CRT abolition bills" from 2021 — one prescribing a favoured narrative, the other proscribing a disfavoured frame.

A symmetrical analysis is essential to the framework's integrity: **ideologisation is a structural operation, not a partisan property.** Both poles mobilise history through the same channels even as they pursue opposed narratives; the 1619 Project's reframing and the celebratory legislative campaigns are formally parallel. This is *not* a claim that their accounts are equally accurate — they differ in factual claims, evidentiary standards, and relation to the record. What is common is the *mechanism*. The contrast with Russia and Turkey is instructive: there the state monopolises and forecloses; in the American case the very visibility of the conflict signals the falsifiability condition has not been wholly extinguished. Whether American pedagogy can sustain that open contest, rather than hardening into mutually sealed partisan frames, is the open question — and on present trajectories, with 27 states legislating content, the drift toward sealed frames is the more probable path absent a deliberate professional and judicial defence.

**Ideologisation is one transposable mechanism — worldview to selection to discourse to naturalisation — distinguished from honest historiography solely by whether its frame remains open to disconfirming evidence, and from instrumentalisation by its ambition to rebuild the past rather than merely borrow it.**

# 4. The Instrumentalisation of History

Instrumentalisation is the tactical deployment of historical reference for present ends — narrower, more agentic, and more traceable than ideologisation. **Where ideologisation asks what the past must mean, instrumentalisation asks what the past can do.** Jan Rydel and Stefan Troebst's 2022 *Instrumentalizing the Past* examines how historical resentments are used to justify political, economic, and territorial demands in international conflicts.

## 4.1 The Logic of Strategic Use

Strategic use begins from a present interest and works *backward* to a usable history — the inverse of the disciplined procedure that works forward from evidence. **The framework's decisive contribution is to relocate the unit of analysis from the content of a historical claim to the transaction in which that claim is spent.** A memory is instrumentalised not when it is false but when it is deployed: the same true fact about 1945 can be remembered organically at a graveside and instrumentalised at a state podium.

This pairs directly with invented tradition: **where Hobsbawm and Ranger explain how a usable past is manufactured, Rydel and Troebst explain how it is subsequently cashed** — invention at the production end, instrumentalisation at the consumption end of one supply chain.

**The causal pathway is a three-link chain:** a present interest generates a demand for legitimation; the demand drives curation of a selective memory; the curated memory produces a legitimation claim presented as the natural verdict of history. **The causal signature is that the arrow of determination runs backward from the claim to the curated memory, even though the rhetoric presents it running forward.** The legitimation claim then feeds back to strengthen the original interest, which is why instrumentalisation intensifies rather than exhausts itself.

The boundary against **organic remembrance** is directional: organic remembrance begins from the event and lets meaning remain open; instrumentalisation begins from the desired meaning and selects the event to fit it. The difficulty is that the two share a surface — a 9 May commemoration can be either, and the Russian state's appropriation of Victory Day shows the conversion in plain view. A paradox: **a memory's authenticity and its instrumentalisability are positively correlated.** The Soviet sacrifice of 1941–1945 is instrumentalisable precisely because it is real and deeply felt — instrumentalisation *parasitises* authentic grief rather than fabricating it. This is why reclamation movements face asymmetric odds: they cannot deny the instrumentalised memory without denying the authentic grief it rides.

## 4.2 Legitimizing Power and Territorial Claims

Territorial and regime-legitimating claims are the purest application of the chain, because the demand is unambiguous and the historical reference can be matched directly against it.

**Russia** sets the chain in motion with regime continuity itself: the 1945 victory is renewed annually as a charter for present authority. The named channel is commemorative ritual fused with state media and memory diplomacy, operating through an *annual calendar* that refreshes the claim. The terminal link is made visible in the weaponised-antifascism move: the curated memory of antifascism is spent as a claim that the 2022 invasion is itself antifascist. The actor structure is the limiting case of **state monopoly** — non-state entrepreneurs operate only within the space the state permits.

**Turkey** drives the same chain through a different channel. The present interest is a post-Kemalist conservative identity anchored in the imperial rather than the secular past. The named channel is **state communications and cultural production** — the Directorate of Communications as engine — distributed across the communications environment rather than concentrated in one ceremony. This is the chapter's clearest contrast with Russia: **a parade-led versus a communications-led variant of the same centralised instrumentalisation.** Where Russia has a single master lieu de mémoire in 9 May, Turkey's 1453 conquest reference is spread across television drama, oratory, and foreign-policy posture.

**Founding myths** generalise the logic. Hobsbawm and Ranger showed the elaboration of British monarchical ritual was an invention of tradition serving regime legitimacy *within a parliamentary monarchy* — which matters for the democracy argument below. A tradeoff structures case selection: **the most vivid memory is the least malleable** (1945 is too recent and documented to reshape freely) **while the most malleable past is the least vivid** (a distant past requires more invention to animate). Regimes choose their founding reference by which constraint binds less.

The pair proves the mechanism is **channel-independent**: parade or broadcast, the backward determination from present demand to curated memory is constant.

## 4.3 Mobilizing Identity, Grievance, and Trauma

Here the curated memory is a *wound* rather than a victory, and the legitimation claim is a demand for redress rather than expansion.

**Romania's AUR** locates the chain in a consolidated democracy and a non-state party actor, testing whether instrumentalisation is uniquely authoritarian. The present interest is electoral mobilisation of grievance; the curated memory is a narrative of national humiliation. The distinctive feature is that **a party, not a state, performs the curation**, so the reinterpretation competes in a plural memory field rather than commanding it. **This is the genuine test of the authoritarian-uniqueness thesis: instrumentalisation operates here through electoral competition, not state command**, converting diffuse anxiety into focused historical grievance — the specifically populist application. Party-driven grievance tends to provoke counter-mobilisation rather than settle the narrative, so the memory war intensifies as vote share rises.

**Argentina** shows trauma memory as the most volatile resource, recruited in opposite directions on the same trauma. The state under Kirchner institutionalised the 1976–1983 dictatorship's trauma via the 2015 ESMA inauguration and Decree 1259/2003. The opposite direction is now visible under Milei: within six months his administration had dismantled key memory policies, and on 24 March 2026 released a 74-minute video accusing kirchnerism of imposing a "narrative" and promoting a "complete memory," presenting a recovered granddaughter raised by an ex-police officer and a soldier's son killed by the ERP as "the victims that wanted to hide". **The chain runs in reverse of reclamation:** a present libertarian-right interest in discrediting kirchnerism reframes the state's trauma memory as a partisan "narrative," then recruits victims of left-wing guerrilla violence to relativise the dictatorship's crimes. The same trauma the Madres made into a reclamation resource is now recruited by the state to relativise the very crimes it memorialised.

**The central insight: institutionalising a trauma memory creates a durable asset a successor government can seize and redirect, not a settled verdict.** Memory institutions are only as durable as the coalition that sustains them. Victimhood is not a fixed property of a group but a **narrative position that can be claimed, contested, and redistributed** — which is why it is so powerful a nation-building resource. The Milei government's recruitment of ERP victims attempts to convert a multidirectional field, in which multiple traumas can be mourned together, into a competitive one in which recognising one set requires relativising another.

# 5. Reclaiming Silenced Narratives

Reclamation completes the analytical triad: the emancipatory recovery of narratives that dominant historiography rendered unhearable. **It is not the simple opposite of ideologisation** but a distinct mechanism with its own capacity to be captured by power. The governing claim: **silencing and reclamation are both productive operations — each manufactures a version of the past, and recovering a silenced narrative is never a neutral retrieval.**

## 5.1 How Narratives Are Silenced

Trouillot's four moments insist silence is **cumulative, not a single event**. An enslaved person's resistance may generate no source (creation); a ledger noting it may be discarded as worthless (assembly); a historian may never consult what survives (retrieval); the national story may assign it no significance (significance). **By the time history is written, silence has been laid down four times over, and no single act of recovery can reverse all four at once.** When Chile donated the Casa de la Memoria archives in 2009 and Argentina created its Archivo in 2003, both intervened at the second moment — operating archive-making *in reverse* to break a silence rather than enter one.

**Epistemic violence** specifies the mechanism at the fourth moment: a narrative can be fully documented and still silenced if the interpretive frame has no place for it. The violence of *omission* is distinct from falsification: omission declines to register something true, leaving no positive trace to contradict. The 2025 NCERT revisions reduced Delhi Sultanate and Mughal chapters to passing references and omitted them outright from Class 7. **The move is not to state the Mughals were wicked but to render them marginal**, so a generation acquires a frame in which centuries of Indo-Islamic history do not signify. The criterion separating ordinary editorial cuts from silencing is *systematicity*: omission becomes silencing when it follows a pattern aligned with a present political interest, as the coordinated reduction across grade levels demonstrates.

**Archival absence** is structural, not accidental: colonial administrations documented what they governed and taxed, so the colonised appear as objects of administration, never as authors. The absence is a shape to be *read*, not a gap to be filled. **Because archival recovery depends on recurring state commitment, the durability of any memory institution is conditional on each electoral cycle**; the Argentine case under Milei suggests defunding rather than destruction is now the primary threat.

## 5.2 Subaltern and Postcolonial Historiography

**Ranajit Guha's** Subaltern Studies challenged elite-centred narratives by emphasising peasant agency. The methodological problem was structural archival absence: the historian seeking peasant consciousness must work from documents produced by those who governed it. The collective's solution was **to read the archive of repression against its grain**, treating the colonial record's account of "disorder" as distorted evidence of a coherent political agency. **Dipesh Chakrabarty** extended this in *Provincializing Europe*, arguing that the conceptual vocabulary of modern history — citizen, state, civil society — is a European product universalised through colonial power. The two stand in tension: **Guha recovers a silenced subject within a frame; Chakrabarty attacks the frame itself.**

**Gayatri Spivak's** "Can the Subaltern Speak?" delivered the tradition's most searching internal critique, concerning *structural audibility* — the conditions under which a voice, even when it speaks, can be heard within available frames. The historian who claims to "give voice" risks ventriloquising the subaltern in the historian's own categories. Her positive method, **imaginative activism**, substitutes disciplined identification — to "go inside the text and try to wish what the text wishes" — for the presumptuous claim to speak on another's behalf, a programme grounded in the four elementary schools she has run in West Bengal since the mid-1980s. **Against the consensus that recovering silenced voices is straightforwardly emancipatory, Spivak's scepticism holds that recovery can itself become an act of epistemic capture.**

**Decolonising historical method** collides with nationalist politics that claims the decolonising mantle for itself. British colonial historiography divided Indian history into Hindu, Muslim, and British periods — a tripartite periodisation that naturalised communal division. Contemporary nationalist rewriting does not dismantle this frame but *repurposes* it: the NCERT reductions and the renaming campaign retain the colonial premise that the "Muslim period" is discrete and separable, while morally reweighting it from neutral epoch to foreign interruption. **The nationalist project borrows the colonial map and changes only the value signs.** The renaming campaign resists simple classification: Bombay→Mumbai and Allahabad→Prayagraj plausibly shed colonial nomenclature, but the June 2026 proposal to rename Bhopal's Barkatullah University — honouring an anti-colonial Muslim freedom fighter — after a Hindu goddess replaces an anti-colonial name with a religious one. **The same instrument performs genuine decolonisation in some instances and communal erasure in others.**

## 5.3 Movements Reclaiming Marginalized Pasts

**Slavery and reparations.** In March 2026 the UN General Assembly adopted a resolution declaring the transatlantic slave trade "the gravest crime against humanity" and calling for reparations, with 123 in favour, 3 against (US, Israel, Argentina), and 52 abstentions including the UK and EU states. **The vote converts a silenced history into a standing legal claim, which is why the states most exposed abstained.** The memory of the enslaved has become audible enough to command near-universal moral assent and contested enough that its material consequence remains blocked — a reclamation that has won the narrative and not yet the redistribution.

**Identity-based recovery** (Indigenous, women's, LGBTQ+ history) shares the subaltern problem — the group left few records in the dominant archive — enacted as political movement. **These movements convert a fading collective memory, held in living communities, into durable cultural memory inscribed in archives, curricula, and monuments.** Whereas Chilean and Argentine institutions recover a recent, well-documented repression, indigenous and women's recovery reaches into periods where silencing was laid down across all four moments, making it more interpretive and more vulnerable to the charge that it constructs as much as it retrieves.

**Rhodes Must Fall** crystallised the impulse into one iconographic target. The Cape Town statue was removed by institutional decision in April 2015 after the campaign; the Bristol Colston statue was toppled by a crowd on 7 June 2020; Oxford's Oriel College faced protests on 9 June 2020 amid the global wave following George Floyd's murder. **By targeting the statue, the movement made visible that the university's everyday space was itself a silencing device.** The tradeoff is acute: removing a statue is fast and measurable, whereas decolonising a curriculum is slow and diffuse. The risk is winning the symbol and losing the substance — **once the statue falls, sustaining the harder curricular campaign requires an organisational discipline the spectacle does nothing to supply.**

## 5.4 Recovery versus Construction: The Reclamation Paradox

The paradox is the tension between recovering a past that genuinely existed and constructing one that serves present need. **Jørgensen** warns that subaltern agency in "history from below" is often as much constructed as retrieved, and that instead of "giving voice," the historian must **stage silence** to expose the conditions that render certain voices unhearable. **Both Jørgensen and Spivak identify the same failure mode: the well-intentioned recovery that ventriloquises the subaltern, substituting the historian's desire for the silenced subject's unrecoverable one.** A recovery which supplies the subaltern with a convenient present-day voice performs a *second silencing* by overwriting the unrecoverable original.

**Staging silence versus giving voice** marks two postures. Giving voice presses the archive to yield a positive account and risks manufacturing one where evidence is thin; staging silence reads the archive's gaps as primary evidence. The choice is **evidential, not ideological**: Chile and Argentina largely give voice (the repression was recent, the documentary base rich once assembled), whereas a deep colonial past more often requires staging silence.

**Inclusion versus tokenisation** is the political form: genuine inclusion reorganises the account; tokenisation appends a marginalised narrative that leaves the dominant frame intact, distinguishable only retrospectively by whether the included narrative proved load-bearing or detachable. The counter-reclamation cases add a darker variant: when the Chilean far right demands "all" victims be remembered, or the Milei government promotes "complete memory," the language of inclusion is turned against the reclamation it pretends to complete. **This is tokenisation's inverse: not the marginal inclusion of the silenced, but the strategic inclusion of the perpetrator's preferred victims to dilute an established reckoning** — showing that the vocabulary of inclusion is available to illiberal memory as readily as to emancipation.

**Construction is not a corruption of recovery but an ineliminable feature of it, and the honest recovery is the one that knows this.** Reclamation cannot be evaluated by its intentions but only by its method and structural effect.

# 6. Commemoration and Historiography

Commemorative practice is not a passive mirror but a **productive force** that actively shapes what societies take their history to be. **Monuments, museums, anniversaries, and truth commissions do not merely record historiography; they intervene in it, setting the agenda that scholars, publics, and states subsequently argue over.**

## 6.1 Commemorative Practices as Producers of Meaning

Commemorative objects are arguments in material form. A monument does not note an event occurred; it asserts the event deserves honour, fixes a particular interpretation in durable material, and places it in public space as a standing premise. Hobsbawm and Ranger's invented-tradition thesis exposes the direction of causation: **the commemorative form comes first and the historiographical claim is manufactured to fit it**. A monument and a peer-reviewed article both make historiographical claims, but they differ in *revisability*: the article's claim is provisional and contestable within the discipline; **the monument's claim is materially entrenched and can be revised only by physical removal**, which is why monument controversies escalate into legislative and street confrontation.

The relationship is **circular, not linear**: a commemorative act elevates an event into public memory, which directs scholarly attention toward it, which generates authoritative knowledge that future commemoration cites as warrant. Russia illustrates each link. The annual Victory Day observance actively shapes which aspects of the war enter consciousness; the nationalisation of war memory since 2014 narrowed permissible historiography, which then supplied the antifascist framing later deployed against Ukraine. **The loop is self-reinforcing because each pass raises the cost of dissent from the commemorative orthodoxy.**

Ritual remembrance is **performative**: it does not describe a pre-existing state of affairs but brings one into being. The 2024 Estadio Nacional observance broadcast Allende's final speech at the exact hour the bombing began on 11 September 1973. By synchronising the broadcast with the original hour, the ceremony positions participants as *witnesses* rather than students. **Because ritual constitutes rather than reports, control of the ritual is control of the event's public meaning** — which is why commemorative practice is so readily captured.

## 6.2 Monument Controversies

**US Confederate monuments** were built to install a specific interpretation. The American Historical Association documents that they were intended to perpetuate the Lost Cause — to honour Confederate soldiers and to vindicate the white South as having acted nobly. **The monument was an ideological instrument from inception, a case of ideologisation materialised in bronze.** Karen Cox documents conflicts more intense today than ever before, escalating into legislative and legal battles. Virginia's House Bill 1344, ending licence plates honouring Robert E. Lee, marks a decade-long retreat from "the Lost Cause's sacred ground" — showing de-ideologisation operating through administrative instruments, not only statue removal.

**Colonial statue toppling** differs in method. The Bristol Colston statue was toppled by a crowd on 7 June 2020 and rolled into the harbour; the empty pedestal itself became a new lieu de mémoire. The Cape Town Rhodes statue was removed by *institutional decision* in April 2015. **Whereas the crowd route delivers immediate symbolically charged removal but contested legality, the institutional route is slower but produces an authorising record** — opposite tradeoffs that shape downstream durability.

**Renaming/reinscription** revises a landscape without demolition, applicable at scale through administrative decree.

| Method | Speed | Documentary record | Reversibility | Exemplar |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Crowd toppling | Immediate | Thin/contested | Low | Colston, 2020 |
| Institutional removal | Slow | Strong | Moderate | Rhodes, 2015 |
| Statutory withdrawal | Slow | Strong | Low | HB 1344, Virginia |
| Reinscription | Variable | Administrative | High | Decommunisation renaming |

## 6.3 Museums and the Curation of Memory

The museum concentrates the commemorative function into one institution whose curatorial choices constitute historiographical claims. **Chile's Museo de la Memoria** carries an explicit mandate to make visible human-rights violations committed by the Chilean State between 1973 and 1990 — not neutral presentation but a committed position naming the state as perpetrator. Its foundation rests on the 16 June 2009 archival transfer. The case demonstrates museum historiography is **contestable precisely because it is constitutive**: the 5 June 2026 Partido Nacional Libertario resolution requesting a "Museum of Truth" is an attempt to install a rival claim through the same institutional form. **Despite the assumption that memory museums secure a settled consensus, the resolution shows the museum form remains a contested weapon: the far right erects a symmetrical rival rather than attacking directly**, relocating the memory war onto institutional terrain rather than terminating it.

**Restitution debates** make physical possession itself a historiographical claim. France's 2026 restitution law avoids recognising colonial violence as the central context of dispossession, relying instead on narrow categories — theft, looting, transfers under duress — that isolate individual acts from systemic domination. **The law's silence on colonial violence is a historiographical choice masquerading as legal-technical.** Digitisation can simulate restitution while entrenching the asymmetry it should redress unless it serves source communities; the binding constraint is whether the source community controls the terms.

**Every curatorial decision is a historiographical claim.** There is no residue of neutral display beneath the selection: the Museo de la Memoria and the proposed Museum of Truth would curate the same 1970–1990 span around opposite evidence and captions. Whereas a monument makes a single fixed claim, **the museum makes a composite, sequenced claim through many objects, giving it greater capacity to embed silences invisibly.**

## 6.4 Anniversaries and Truth Commissions

**Argentina's Nunca Más** fixes the claim through a judicial verdict rather than an object. A museum asserts a narrative; **a trial establishes a fact under evidentiary standards and attaches consequences.** When a truth commission's findings are ratified by conviction, the claim acquires a juridical solidity that survives adversarial testing. **A rival museum can be erected, but a rival verdict cannot simply be declared** — it would require its own adversarial process with its own evidentiary burden. **Trial-based memory therefore raises the cost of revisionism in a way museum-based memory does not**, and is likely to prove more durable against populist pressure, conditional on convictions not being overturned or amnestied.

**Bicentennials** concentrate the anniversary's power on the origin narrative: a round-number anniversary licenses a fresh construction of national lineage. Turkey shows this through commemorative and institutional channels at once — the neo-Ottoman reorientation away from the Kemalist founding toward an Ottoman imperial lineage, with the Directorate of Communications actively producing the narrative. The causal chain links commemoration to foreign policy through identity: elevated Ottoman commemoration furnishes the identity frame legitimating an assertive foreign policy, which feeds back as justification for further commemorative investment.

# 7. Historical Memory in the Service of Current Agendas

**Historical memory becomes a strategic resource because it converts contested facts about the mobilised past into uncontested claims about who belongs, who rules, and by what right.** This chapter specifies the causal logic: the stakes (§7.1), the legal machinery that codifies victory (§7.2), the digital arenas that accelerate contest (§7.3), and the historian caught between method and mobilisation (§7.4).

## 7.1 Power, Identity, and Legitimacy as Stakes

When actors fight over how 1973 or 1945 is remembered, they fight over three entangled resources. **Control of the past confers authority** because tradition, once accepted as ancient, removes the present arrangement from debate: a practice read as traditional silences the question of who designed it and why. **The belonging function is its obverse**: invented traditions arise when rapid transformation weakens older patterns and actors require new anchors.

**Memory is the cheapest means of manufacturing legitimacy** an illiberal regime commands. Russia's apparatus places the regime beyond electoral or generational turnover, with WWII and the Stalin era as a "political weapon". The legitimacy chain: the Great Patriotic War furnishes an unambiguous moral victory; ritually re-enacted each 9 May, it positions the state as defender of civilisation against fascism; that positioning licenses branding present adversaries as fascists, transferring 1945's moral authority onto a 2022 war. A contrarian note: this is not purely top-down manipulation but co-produced — Stephenson argues the appropriation works because it answers a real popular hunger for significance, a "drama of eternal recurrence".

**Identity is constituted as much by what is forgotten as by what is recalled.** Selective remembrance is identity's manufacturing process, not its by-product; cultural memory fixes a generation's choice into a civilisation's inheritance. The forward implication: **societies that lock identity to a single remembered trauma through 2030 are structurally primed for memory wars**, having foreclosed the multidirectional option.

The three stakes are three outputs of a single mechanism: control of remembrance simultaneously removes the present order from debate, draws the boundary of the collective self, and licenses the claim to rule. Russia fuses all three in one object — the 9 May ritual — which is why it repays such heavy investment.

## 7.2 Memory Laws and Transitional Justice

Memory laws prescribe or proscribe interpretations of the past, marking where commemorative preference acquires the coercive force of the state. **The memory law makes a historiographical conclusion non-negotiable by attaching penalties to its contradiction.** Poland's law protected the nation's image against imputations of complicity; Russia's protects the Great Patriotic War narrative. The tradeoff is acute: a statute protecting victims (a ban on Holocaust denial) becomes, when turned to protect a nation's self-image, the suppression of legitimate inquiry. The distinction is between **protecting the dignity of identifiable victims and protecting the reputation of an abstract collective** — yet both use identical machinery, so no drafting technique cleanly separates them.

**Transitional justice inverts the illiberal memory law: where the latter silences to protect power, the former commemorates to repair it.** Decree 1259/2003 created the Archivo to preserve the evidentiary basis for accountability; the 2015 ESMA inauguration converted a torture site into a recognised memory site; "Nunca Más" compresses remembrance in service of non-repetition. But the same infrastructure can be dismantled when the agenda changes. **Transitional-justice memory, because it depends on continued state endorsement, is structurally more reversible than the illiberal memory law embedded in criminal statute.** The codification's durability is a function not of legal technique but of the regime's capacity to foreclose alternation: Russia's protection operates where no turnover threatens; Argentina's flips with each government, as Milei's 2026 "memoria completa" doctrine demonstrates.

## 7.3 Media, Digital Memory, and Public Debate

Social media restructure memory disputes by **detaching the contest from national territory**, recruiting diaspora, foreign publics, and algorithmic amplification into a single transnational field. The amplification mechanism: a claim is posted, an engagement-optimising algorithm promotes it in proportion to outrage, which selects for extreme framings, crowding moderate accounts out of visibility. A 2024 study found political uses of the ancient past on social media are predominantly negative and extreme. **The selection pressure of the medium itself biases surviving memory toward the extreme** — a different distortion from top-down state distortion. Yet the same platforms enable emancipatory work: hashtag commemoration, memetic memory, digital activism let marginalised groups circulate silenced narratives without permission. The net direction depends on which actors master the affordances first.

Commemoration is now **mediatized**: Holocaust memory has moved from the "era of the witness" to the "era of the user", with authority migrating from the survivor to the user whose repost recontextualises at will. Russia's 80th-anniversary preparations stage spectacle for virality, **laundering the official narrative through apparently spontaneous popular sharing**. The identical technology serves opposite agendas depending on who holds the microphone.

The digital turn has **collapsed the gate academic historiography once kept**, precisely when political demand for usable history is highest. The tradeoff does not resolve cleanly: democratising history delivers wider participation and recovery of silenced voices, but removing the gate also removes the filter that screened out fabrication. **No mechanism currently distinguishes emancipatory public history from extremist pseudo-history at the speed and scale of the platform.**

## 7.4 Scholarly Historiography versus Public Memory

The historian occupies an **untenable middle position**, committed to critical method yet recruited into affective public contests method cannot win on its own terms. **Historiography and public memory obey incompatible truth conditions:** historiography holds conclusions provisional and revisable; public memory seeks a fixed, emotionally satisfying account that anchors identity. **The scholar's commitment to revisability is exactly what public memory experiences as betrayal.** One cannot defeat an affective claim with an epistemic one, because the affective claim was never making an epistemic bid; the most the historian can do is contest the factual substrate while accepting the affect lies outside the discipline's jurisdiction.

The arbiter posture is increasingly unavailable: **neutrality becomes a position in the war rather than a vantage above it**, because one combatant will read the refusal to endorse as endorsement of the enemy. Poland coerces through statute (legal jeopardy); the US through spectacle (reputational jeopardy). **By 2030, in jurisdictions that have codified official narratives, the neutral-arbiter historian is likely to be a structurally extinct professional type**, replaced by the engaged public intellectual or the quiescent archivist.

The gravest danger is **academic capture**: the discipline winning the memory war by becoming indistinguishable from the agenda it serves. Turkey's Directorate of Communications undertook a neo-Ottoman recasting marshalling state resources, raising professional returns to historians who supply a glorified narrative, marginalising critical scholarship on Ottoman violence. **Capture is the point at which the state no longer needs to criminalise dissent, because it has bought the production of the history it wants.** The defence is not regime change but institutional insulation: the more a scholarly community's appointments, funding, and venues are independent of the state's preferred narrative, the higher its resistance.

# 8. Regional Case Studies

The twelve cases convert the conceptual apparatus into observable practice across four clusters. Each is profiled against the same seven axes so cross-case comparison yields patterns rather than anecdotes. **The central finding: the distinction between state-led ideologisation and grassroots reclamation is the master variable separating distortion from democratisation — and mechanism predicts consequence better than geography does.**

## 8.1 Post-Soviet and Central-Eastern Europe

This is the clearest laboratory for **reciprocal incitement**: Russia's victory cult emerged in direct conversation with its neighbours' mnemonic policies, a self-perpetuating cycle.

**Russia** is the purest instance of state ideologisation, with inquiry subordinated wholesale to a regime-legitimating frame. Channels: commemorative ceremony, education, memory law. PONARS dates the decisive shift to post-2014, when WWII commemoration was recalibrated from a shared Soviet inheritance into exclusively Russian national property. Victory Day directly helps the Kremlin justify the war in Ukraine. **The case passes Ideologisation depth without qualification.** So long as the war continues, the victory cult is likely to intensify, because each mnemonic move by a neighbour supplies fresh material for the antifascist frame.

**Ukraine** is the case where reclamation and state policy most nearly coincide, because wartime conditions align official and popular goals. Channels: decommunization statutes and monument politics. Since the invasion, East European states have accelerated Soviet-monument removal while Russia has erected new memorials to Soviet soldiers and even Stalin. The move is reclamation with an instrumental edge — decommunization recovers a suppressed national historiography, yet involves selective emphasis like all state-aligned memory work. **What distinguishes Ukraine is state-activist *convergence*** — directly opposite to the state-versus-activist opposition seen in Italy and the US. The wartime convergence secures solidarity but narrows internal pluralism; because the narrowing is driven by external danger, the aperture for plural debate widens again only as threat recedes.

**Romania (AUR)** illustrates the **populist-party variant**: bottom-up commemorative mobilisation seeking to displace the institutional consensus from *outside* the state. The move is populist instrumentalisation dressed as reclamation — a majoritarian nationalist narrative claiming the status of the silenced subaltern, borrowing the moral authority of genuine reclamation. **This party-actor structure is the genuine test of the authoritarian-uniqueness thesis**: instrumentalisation operates through electoral competition, not state command. Each nationalist commemoration provokes counter-mobilisation, reproducing reciprocal incitement at the domestic scale.

**The cluster proves actor configuration, not narrative content, is the decisive variable:** the same coupled system houses a vertical state monopoly, a state-activist convergence, and an oppositional party insurgency, each producing a distinct democratic consequence.

## 8.2 Post-Authoritarian Reckonings in the Global South

The museum and the tribunal are the signature mechanisms of Global South memory work — yet this cluster contains both the most institutionalised reclamation *and* the most aggressive state ideologisation.

**Chile** is the paradigm of state-institutionalised reclamation. The 2009 donation transferred dispersed traces into permanent public heritage — itself a historiographical act, because it fixes which traces survive. Here state power is placed *in the service of memory* rather than memory in the service of state power. **By lodging the repression archive in a permanent institution, the state made the memory of dictatorship structurally resistant to reversal**: a standing institution is far harder to silence than a dispersed private memory.

**Argentina** is the case of **juridical reckoning**, where reclamation proceeds through tribunal and investigative report. The dictatorship's method of disappearance was designed to silence at the moment of fact-making itself; the Nunca Más report reverses this by reconstituting the record at the moment of retrieval. The Madres are paradigmatic memory activists. **Truth-commission reckonings coupled to prosecution prove more resistant to revisionism than those that stop at documentation, because the juridical finding creates a legal fact costly to overturn**.

**Turkey** is the second major instance of state ideologisation, distinguished from Russia by its **imperial rather than wartime register**. The mechanism is top-down and communications-led: the state narrates the imperial past as glorious and continuous, with education and ceremony distributing it downward. **Two otherwise dissimilar states (Russia and Turkey) arrive at the same vertically integrated actor structure because both pursue regime-legitimating ideologisation — confirming the narrative goal predicts actor configuration more reliably than regional geography.**

**The cluster establishes that region is analytically shallow:** Chile and Argentina institutionalise reclamation through museum and tribunal, while Turkey pursues state ideologisation indistinguishable in actor structure from Russia's. **Mechanism predicts democratic consequence far better than shared experience of authoritarian rule.**

## 8.3 Western Democracies and the Far-Right Past

These democracies confront not the recovery of silenced victims but **the contested public standing of a discredited far-right past that retains active defenders** — fought over relativisation of an already-condemned past.

**Italy** is the case of governing-party relativisation. Under Meloni, leading figures conduct a "subtle campaign to commemorate fascist figures" through calibrated ambiguity. Senate president Ignazio La Russa claimed the Constitution does not contain the word "anti-fascism" and announced he would spend Liberation Day paying tribute to Jan Palach alongside Nazi victims, flattening the distinction. The move is **instrumentalisation through "both-sidesing"** — presenting fascism and anti-fascism as morally comparable partisan positions, with the governing right casting itself as the excluded party, borrowing the grammar of reclamation to perform relativisation.

The **United States** (Confederate monuments) and **Croatia** (Ustasha/anti-fascist contestation) complete the cluster, showing insurgent and governing actors working to rehabilitate or defend discredited legacies against an established anti-fascist or anti-racist consensus. The cluster's signature is that **the memory war is fought over the relativisation of an already-condemned past** rather than the recovery of silenced victims.

## 8.4 Coupling Paths Among the Mechanisms

The four mechanisms operate not in sequence but **in circuit**: each output becomes another's input, and the *direction* of coupling determines whether a regime stabilises, escalates, or opens to revision. **The mechanisms form directed loops, not a linear pipeline, and the sign of each coupling — amplifying or constraining — predicts a regime's trajectory.**

**Ideologisation feeds instrumentalisation and provokes reclamation.** In India, the NCERT reduction narrows the classroom archive, which lowers the public cost of a parallel deployment — the renaming campaign. (These are mutually reinforcing instruments drawing on a shared frame, not a proven sequence.) The renaming instrument is not monovalent: Bombay→Mumbai is decolonial reclamation, while the Barkatullah University proposal turns the same grammar against a freedom fighter. **The same grammatical move serves opposite ends, defeating the assumption that the instrument carries a fixed political valence.**

**Commemoration and historiography form feedback loops.** Chile's archive becomes a museum, the museum stages commemoration, and the commemorative output loops back: the 2024 broadcast at the exact hour performs a historiographical claim about chronology. The proposed Museum of Truth would open a *competing node* in the same loop. **By 2028, the survival of the Allende-era consensus will depend less on the documentary strength of the Museo's archive, which is secure, than on whether a Kast presidency funds a competing node.**

**The amplify/constrain sign is a function of whether the institution carrying the constraint is staffed and funded.** Argentina built one of the world's most elaborate memory infrastructures yet saw it hollowed within a single cycle. The "complete memory" rhetoric is precisely an *additive*, not a negationist, strategy — where a documentary record is robust, the revisionist argues the record is incomplete rather than false. **Reconciliation infrastructure is a flow, not a stock:** documentation assembled across two decades can be defunded in six months, so the durable variable is the recurring political will to resource it.

## 8.5 Competitive versus Multidirectional Memory

**Neither model is universally correct; whether memory becomes zero-sum or negotiated is a function of institutional incentives, not an intrinsic property of memory.**

The **competitive model** finds its sharpest expression in the CEE reciprocal-incitement cycle: a removal in one jurisdiction is read as provocation in another, licensing a compensatory erection. Memory laws formalise the contest — once codified in law, the cost of de-escalation rises, because retreat requires repealing a statute.

Against this stands **Rothberg's multidirectional memory**: memories borrow rhetorical and moral resources from each other, so articulating one atrocity furnishes vocabulary for naming a second. The competitive model's failure mode is a numerical auction of suffering — Walter Benn Michaels' claim that "the black holocaust was 100 times worse" is the corrosive extreme the multidirectional frame is designed to avert. The **implicated subject** dissolves discrete mnemonic camps, predicting de-escalation precisely because it denies that mnemonic territories are bounded.

| Dimension | Competitive | Multidirectional |
|---|---|---|
| Core premise | Memory is bounded, rivalrous | Memory is generative |
| Mechanism | Reciprocal incitement; removal/erection cycle | Borrowing across histories |
| Subject model | Discrete victim/perpetrator camps | Implicated subject |
| Driver | Memory laws lock the contest | Joint commemoration, inclusive curricula |
| Trajectory | Escalation | Possible de-escalation |

**The two models describe the same phenomenon under different institutional incentive structures.** Where the environment scores commemoration against a rival, the competitive model obtains; where it scores inclusion, the multidirectional model does. **The lever for moving a regime toward negotiation is not persuasion about the past but redesign of the incentives that score commemoration in the present.** By 2030, illiberal memory regimes suggest the competitive model will dominate where populist incumbents control memory law, while the multidirectional model survives mainly in transnational and civil-society spaces insulated from statutory capture.

## 8.6 Maturity Tiers of Mitigations

**The mitigation frontier has shifted decisively from the institutional domain, where mature tools exist, to the epistemic domain of digital disinformation, where none yet do.**

**Mature mechanisms — truth commissions and critical historiography.** The Chilean Museo and Argentina's Archivo are the institutional residue. Their maturity lies in a tested causal property: a documented, state-acknowledged archive raises the evidentiary cost of denial, pushing revisionists toward additive "complete memory" arguments rather than negation. **The defining feature is that these instruments work through the evidentiary threshold they impose on revision, not through any guarantee of permanence** — as the Milei reversal showed.

**Partial mechanisms — memory laws and museum reform.** Their sign is *not fixed*: the same statutory form can protect a vulnerable historiography or codify a state's preferred myth; the same museum form serves reconciliation or revisionism (the rival "Museum of Truth"). **The diagnostic marker is sign reversibility**, which is why these tools cannot be recommended unconditionally. The resolution lies in coupling them to mature-tier documentation: a memory law built atop a rigorously documented archive inherits the archive's constraining sign.

**Open problems — digital disinformation and polarization.** In 2026, generative AI enables synthetic historical events and deepfake archival footage, threatening epistemic exhaustion. MEMRI reported on 14 April 2026 that Holocaust deniers have dramatically expanded online efforts with AI tools. LLMs can elicit prosthetic memories but introduce denialism risks when outputs align with revisionist narratives; a July 2024 UNESCO report warned generative AI could distort the Holocaust's record. On 27 January 2026, RFI reported a fabricated photo naming a non-existent girl as an Auschwitz victim — **a single fabricated testimony degrades the evidentiary baseline precisely as the survivor generation that could contradict it dwindles.** Index on Censorship warned in 2025 that "history is being written by the AI victors": unlike historians, AI cannot evaluate credibility or identify gaps. **That the frontier countermeasure is a 500-event detection benchmark (HistoricalMisinfo, 45 countries) — not a deployed filter — precisely marks digital disinformation as an open problem.** Polarization compounds it as a "hyper-problem": decreasing social cohesion, contributing to a culture of impunity, eventually inciting mass atrocity.

**The memory field's defensive capability is inversely distributed against its threats:** the most mature instruments address the oldest threats, while the newest and fastest — AI-enabled fabrication — faces only a research-stage benchmark.

## 8.7 Consequences: Democratization, Distortion, or Reconciliation

**The same mechanism produces opposite consequences depending on whether it widens or narrows the archive.** Democratization occurs when reinterpretation *widens* the circle of remembered actors — the Madres' headscarf becoming a national emblem, ultimately ratified through the ESMA site. Distortion occurs when the same energy *narrows* the archive around a mobilizing myth — the far right instrumentalising lieux de mémoire to construct exclusionary narratives, the NCERT subtraction. **Democratization and distortion are not different mechanisms but opposite vectors of the same mechanism of reinterpretation.**

**Polarization** occupies the middle, hardening society into mutually unintelligible camps. The driver is the performance of victimhood, intertwined with identity politics to construct friend/enemy dichotomies; external shocks like the 2008 crisis accelerate the chain. **Its distinctive danger is that it is self-reinforcing: each performance of victimhood raises the cost of the reciprocal recognition that would de-escalate it.** The CSVR treats it not as a symptom but a cause in its own right — a hyper-problem that makes others harder to solve.

**Reconciliation** is the rarest outcome, requiring the most specific conditions: a documented archive with state acknowledgment, an institutional incentive structure rewarding inclusive rather than rivalrous commemoration, and the conceptual shift the implicated subject makes available.

# 9. Limitations, Falsifiers, and Scope

Every framework that classifies memory politics purchases clarity at the price of three blindnesses: uneven evidence, selected cases, and a moving present.

## 9.1 Granularity and Evidentiary Constraints

**Source richness is unevenly distributed; the two Southern Cone cases are documented at a level of dated primary acts that most others are not.** Argentina yields Decree 1259/2003, the 2015 ESMA inauguration, the 2026 video; Chile yields the 2009 donation and the 2026 resolution. Croatia, Romania, and Cabo Verde are not resolved to comparable instruments. **Where evidence resolves to a decree, the report can trace instrumentalisation as datable state acts; where it does not, it can only characterise a tendency.** A reader should weight the Southern Cone findings as evidentially firmer.

The report is built from **conspicuous flashpoints** and systematically under-samples everyday, low-visibility transmission — what a teacher says about 1973 on an unremarkable Tuesday. This follows from the nature of the lieu de mémoire: sites designed to be remembered generate records; the diffuse work of cultural memory in home and classroom mostly does not. **The reliance on prominent cases risks making memory politics look more like staged confrontations than continuous quiet habituation.**

A substantial portion of the interpretive load rests on **evaluatively committed secondary sources**. Frontiers describes the BJP textbook project as nation-building and secularising; Vibes of India describes the renaming as erasure — incompatible registers. **When the only characterisation already classifies a case as "erasure" or "secularising," the narrative-move taxonomy risks laundering the source's politics into an apparently analytic category.** Where the report rests on a single committed source, its ideologisation verdict should be read as provisional. **The report's confidence should scale with the resolution of its evidence, not the vividness of its cases.**

## 9.2 Scope and Coverage Caps

**Entire memory fields of the first rank fall outside the frame by design.** The East Asian field (wartime atrocity, shrine visits, comfort-women disputes), post-genocide Rwanda, and the Levant are not cases. The twelve entities cluster in Europe, the Americas, South Asia, and the lusophone Atlantic. The cost is specific: **the triad was refined against cases where conflict runs *within* a polity; whether it survives contact with a memory war conducted primarily *across a border* is a question this report cannot settle.** The triad is validated for intra-state contestation and untested for the inter-state variety.

The analytic instruments are **disproportionately European in origin** — Nora, the communicative–cultural distinction, illiberal memory. This double overrepresentation produces a subtle framing effect: **because the instruments were designed for European national memory, the report tends to treat the European pattern as the baseline against which other regions register as variations.**

By selecting for contestation, the report **guarantees it sees reinterpretation wherever it looks.** The apparent circularity — that a study of memory wars concludes memory is everywhere a war — is resolved not by denial but by bounding the claim: the report does not assert all historiography is contested, only that *where* contestation occurs it follows traceable patterns. The chronological-narration genre it excludes is the silent majority against which the war cases should be read.

## 9.3 Temporal Validity

**The direction of a national memory regime can reverse within a single electoral cycle.** The Argentine state that created the Archivo in 2003 and inaugurated ESMA in 2015 had, by 2024, dismantled key policies within six months and, by March 2026, released a video promoting "complete memory". **Any present-tense description of Argentina's regime is a snapshot of a regime that demonstrably inverted between 2015 and 2026.**

Several cases are **open controversies, not historical episodes with known endings.** The "Museum of Truth" proposal is unresolved: a proposal that never materialises reads as a rhetorical gesture; the same proposal realised reads as a durable counter-institution. **The report's reading is a bet on a trajectory, not a verdict on an outcome.**

**The rule for reading the report across time: trust the mechanisms, discount the instances.** Elections reverse regimes, open controversies resolve unpredictably, and 2025–2026 events have not shown their downstream effects. **The structural findings — that memory institutions change hands with governments, that flashpoints attract rival claimants, that textbook revision is a standard channel — should outlast any particular government.** On the evidence of the Argentine reversal, a meaningful fraction of present-tense regime characterisations tied to the current incumbent will be outdated within two electoral cycles.

## 9.4 Case Selection and Sampling Bias

The cases were selected for salience, which systematically tilts the argument toward conflict. Every case earned its place by generating a flashpoint; quiet cases never enter the sample, which is self-confirming. **Whereas a random sample would answer "how common is memory conflict?", this purposive sample answers "where it occurs, what form does it take?"** The report answers the second and must not be read as answering the first.

The report contains almost no cases of **consensual memory** — the 2024 Estadio Nacional commemoration, drawing broad participation in shared mourning, is a partial exception showing even a bitterly contested past can support consensus. **By sampling the mobilising end and neglecting the reconciling end, the report can make commemoration appear more uniformly divisive than the full range supports.** A purposive sample licenses claims of the form "when X occurs, it operates through mechanism Y" but not "X occurs in Z percent of nations." **The report is a study of mechanisms, not a census of outcomes.**

## 9.5 Falsifiers and Counter-Evidence

The instrumentalisation thesis earns its standing only by specifying what would refute it. **It would be falsified by:** (1) evidence that incoming governments of opposed ideology systematically *preserve* predecessors' memory institutions unchanged — the Argentine sequence is consistent with the thesis rather than against it; (2) the regular observation of contested pasts generating *no* counter-institution over sustained periods — the "Museum of Truth" instantiates the predicted rivalry; (3) textbook content remaining *stable* across sharp ideological transitions — the NCERT revisions support the thesis.

**At least one instance shows commemoration de-escalating rather than mobilising:** the 2024 Estadio Nacional ceremony performed a wide integrative function without becoming a site of partisan capture. This forces a *refinement*, not abandonment: **the same commemorative object can mobilise or de-escalate depending on whether a political actor chooses to contest it.** The 2024 ceremony consolidated while the 2026 resolution divides; commemoration is a medium that can carry either, and the thesis is properly read as a claim about what instrumentalising actors do with that medium.

**The report's most consequential contrarian exposure** concerns its tacit liberal baseline. The concept of illiberal memory presupposes a liberal norm against which illiberality registers as departure. But the AHA's own reflection — that all scholarship is implicated in power and has never been fully objective — undercuts the assumption that the liberal account is neutral. **The report does not resolve this by claiming false neutrality; it grounds its ideologisation verdicts in the traceable subordination of inquiry to a closed frame — a procedural test that can in principle catch liberal and illiberal actors alike** — rather than in the prior assumption that one side holds the truth.

**The report's strongest epistemic claim is not that its thesis is true, but that it is the kind of thesis that could be shown false and has not been.** The claims are bounded by evidence, geography, time, and sampling, and remain standing against the tests that would refute them.

# 10. Conclusion: Memory, Democracy, and the Politics of the Future

**The reinterpretation of historical narratives is not an aberration of bad actors but a structural feature of how political communities secure legitimacy in the present. Memory cannot be depoliticised, only better or worse governed.**

## 10.1 The Recurring Logic of Contested Pasts

The contestation of the past recurs because the conditions producing it are permanent. **The causal spine runs in one direction: a present political need generates a demand for a usable past, which activates a named mechanism, which reshapes the historiographical record publics inherit.** Reinterpretation is *pulled by present conditions, not pushed by new archival discovery.* India makes the chain legible: an ideological demand for a civilisational self-image leads to the 2025 NCERT reduction, narrowing what a generation recognises as constitutive of the national story. Each link is **mediated by an institution** — a textbook board, museum charter, memory law — and that mediation is where agency and responsibility locate. The most consequential extension through 2030 is the insertion of generative AI as a new mediating institution that lowers the cost of the mediating step, letting the same demands reach the record faster and at greater scale.

Beneath every case lies a constant triad: **power, identity, and legitimacy are the three goods the mobilised past is recruited to secure.** The Chilean museum's mandate is a legitimacy claim for the post-dictatorship order; the 2026 "Museum of Truth" is a bid to reattach legitimacy to a different genealogy. A named-alternative comparison sharpens it: **Chile's far right seeks to *build* a rival institution, while Argentina's Milei worked by *dismantling* an existing one** — construction versus demolition are two routes to the same legitimacy objective, the choice tracking whether the contested institution is already state-controlled.

**Every resolution is provisional, vulnerable to reopening whenever power shifts.** Argentina demonstrates reversibility: what looked settled after the 2003 decree and 2015 inauguration became, two decades later, an occasion for contestation. **The battleground's governing tradeoff:** a state that legally fixes one account protects victims from denialism but risks foreclosing open inquiry, while a state that refuses any fixed account leaves victims exposed to revisionism. The Argentine reversal shows the account holds only as long as the coalition that installed it retains power — **the binding constraint is not evidentiary but electoral.**

## 10.2 Paradoxes and Unresolved Tensions

The most honest conclusions are paradoxes the evidence sharpens without settling.

**The recovery-construction paradox.** Every act of recovering a silenced narrative is simultaneously an act of constructing a new one. The 2009 Chilean donation recovered silenced testimony *and* built an authoritative account; the "Museum of Truth" exploits this, presenting its construction as a recovery. This is **not a licence for relativism.** Acknowledging that recovery involves construction does not collapse the distinction between accounts answerable to evidence and those insulated from it. **What separates warranted from unwarranted construction is procedural, not substantive: openness to correction.** A reclamation that invites evidentiary challenge and revises under it occupies a different epistemic category than an ideologisation that forecloses inquiry.

**The eliminability of ideology.** Ideology cannot be eliminated from historiography, only made visible and contestable. The realistic aim is not a "neutral" historiography but one whose commitments are *declared and defensible*. The Nehruvian curriculum was also ideologically committed; the contrast with Hindutva revision is not ideology versus neutrality but **one declared nation-building project against another.** The defensible distinction: reducing the Mughal chapters to passing references *removes evidentiary material* students would need to evaluate the narrative — an analytically different operation from adding an interpretive frame over retained evidence.

**The liberal baseline as itself partisan.** The liberal commitment to open inquiry — the very baseline against which the report measures ideologisation — is itself a substantive position, not a neutral vantage. An illiberal regime can reply that the liberal insistence on keeping the past open is merely the ideology of a cosmopolitan professional class. The report does not evade this. It concedes the baseline is partisan in privileging revisability, contestability, and the protection of minority accounts over finality. **What it denies is that this makes all positions equivalent.** The liberal position's partisanship is *self-limiting*: it takes sides only on the meta-question of whether the past stays open to revision, deferring on first-order questions to evidence. **It is the only position that, by protecting the conditions for its own contestation, leaves room for its opponents to win** — the property illiberal regimes, by fixing the past legally and curricularly, explicitly refuse.
