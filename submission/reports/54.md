# From Mean-Variance to Deep Learning: Comparing Risk, Return, and Allocation Paradigms and Building a Hybrid Portfolio Framework

No single modeling paradigm dominates portfolio construction across its three load-bearing tasks—risk measurement, return prediction, and allocation mechanics—and the empirically defensible path forward is an explicitly hybrid stack that assigns each task to the family that solves it best. Across more than 60 sources, from Rockafellar & Uryasev's CVaR foundations to the 2025 ICML Bayesian Black-Litterman result that improved Sharpe by 50% and cut turnover by 55%, the dispersion in reported performance is enormous: documented Sharpe ratios run from roughly **0.78 for a passive 60/40 benchmark to 2.18 for a regime-aware Wasserstein-HMM allocator**.

Despite the claim that deep learning's non-linear capacity renders classical optimizers obsolete, the evidence shows classical posterior-stabilizers and tail-risk measures remain decisively superior on interpretability, turnover, and out-of-sample stability—and regulation effective April 17, 2026 makes that interpretability a binding constraint, not a preference.

**Committed verdict: Black-Litterman-family Bayesian blenders rank first for general-purpose deployment, CVaR/robust optimization second, regime-aware and decision-focused learners third, pure deep-RL allocators fourth—and a hybrid framework that stacks all four beats any single family.**

The foundational tension is quantitative. DeMiguel, Garlappi, and Uppal (2009) estimated that a sample-based mean-variance strategy requires roughly **3,000 months of data to beat naive equal-weighting with 25 assets, and about 6,000 months with 50 assets**. Bock (2018) found that none of sixteen recent strategies consistently beat 1/N or minimum-variance on Sharpe ratio, certainty-equivalent return, or turnover.

## 1. Research Framework, Scope & Evaluation Rubric

### 1.1 The Model Universe

The study covers seven families that convert beliefs about expected excess return and a risk measure into portfolio weights:

| Model Family | Risk Measure | Return Prediction | Allocation | Interpretability | Best-Fit Regime |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Mean-Variance (Markowitz)** | Variance (symmetric) | Sample mean, error-prone | Quadratic program | Fully analytic | Stable, elliptical |
| **Black-Litterman** | Posterior covariance | Bayesian posterior mean | MVO on posterior | Traceable views | View-rich, institutional |
| **CVaR/Robust** | Tail (CVaR) | Scenario-based | Linear/convex program | Moderate | Skewed, crisis-prone |
| **Deep Learning (CNN-LSTM/Transformer)** | Implicit/learned | Nonlinear forecast | Predict-then-optimize | Black-box | Data-rich, trending |
| **DRL Allocators** | Reward-embedded | Implicit policy | Direct policy map | Opaque | Stationary, data-rich |
| **Decision-Focused/E2E** | Decision-aligned | Joint with decision | End-to-end | Low | Stable objectives |
| **Hybrid BL+ML** | Posterior + tail | ML-generated views | BL blending | Partial | Most regimes |

The report is **bounded strictly to asset allocation**: turning capital across a fixed universe into rebalanced position weights, net of transaction costs. Credit scoring, fraud detection, and market-making are out of scope.

**The shared output is what makes the matrix valid.** Because every family produces the same terminal object—a weight vector on the simplex—the seven can be compared on identical axes. This commensurability is the precondition for tier ranking.

The output column hides a structural bifurcation. MVO, Black-Litterman, and CVaR/robust are **predict-then-optimize** pipelines—they estimate moments, then solve a separate optimization. DRL and decision-focused learning collapse those stages, outputting weights or a policy directly. **This two-stage-versus-end-to-end split is the most consequential architectural distinction in the matrix**, driving every downstream comparison on interpretability and governance: predict-then-optimize exposes an auditable intermediate forecast; end-to-end methods expose only the decision.

### 1.2 Evaluation Rubric

Six weighted criteria govern the tier ranking, summing to 1.0:

| Criterion | Weight | What it scores |
|---|---|---|
| Risk Measurement Fidelity | 0.20 | How faithfully the family represents the loss distribution |
| Return Prediction Reliability | 0.20 | Stability of the expected-return estimate, penalizing overfitting |
| Allocation Robustness & Cost Efficiency | 0.20 | Weight stability and turnover, with costs explicit |
| Interpretability & Governance | 0.15 | Structural transparency vs. black-box opacity |
| Regime Adaptability | 0.15 | Performance persistence across market states |
| Implementation Practicality | 0.10 | Data, compute, operational cost |

The weighting reflects the thesis that **out-of-sample risk-adjusted performance, net of transaction costs, is the binding constraint**. It deliberately caps performance criteria at 0.20 each so that interpretability and regime adaptability together (0.30) cannot be swamped—a governance-aware posture distinguishing it from backtesting-only comparisons.

### 1.3 The Central Structural Claim

The seven families are not seven rivals but **two complementary stacks**: an auditable predict-then-optimize tradition (MVO, Black-Litterman, CVaR) strong on governance but weak on nonlinear signal, and a machine-learning-native tradition (deep learning, DRL, decision-focused) strong on signal but weak on governance. **This is exactly why the hybrid that routes ML signal through Bayesian blending dominates the rubric.**

The quantitative spine: DeMiguel et al.'s 3,000-month requirement shows why raw estimation fails; Yuan and Zhou's combination result shows why disciplined blending succeeds; Bock's null result shows why no standalone family wins outright.

## 2. Mean-Variance Optimization: The Baseline

Mean-Variance Optimization formalizes portfolio choice as a two-moment problem—weighing expected return against variance and nothing else. The MOSEK Cookbook describes it as convenient and useful while cautioning that the optimal solution is **extremely sensitive to changes in its inputs**. The core finding: **MVO is maximally transparent about its mechanism and maximally opaque about its reliability.**

### 2.1 Assumptions, Inputs, and Mechanics

**Inputs and objective.** MVO consumes a vector of expected returns and a covariance estimator, reducing N assets to a length-N mean vector and an N×N covariance matrix. This two-moment summary is exact only under elliptical (canonically normal) returns. The output is a weight vector. **Tractability is purchased by collapsing the full distribution to two moments—and that same collapse is the source of fragility.**

**Risk as variance.** Variance weighs equal-magnitude upside and downside identically. Strikingly, the full-distribution analysis shows that **errors in correlations have a larger impact on optimal portfolios than errors in means or variances**—undercutting the common practitioner focus on return forecasting.

**Return prediction.** MVO predicts nothing; it consumes an externally supplied mean, typically the sample mean—a single unconditional number per asset. The realized-return series is a noisy proxy for the expected-return quantity the optimizer requires. **The quality of MVO's output is bounded above by an input the model provides no mechanism to improve.**

**Allocation.** The allocation reduces to the inverse covariance matrix applied to the mean vector (the Σ⁻¹μ form). Three quadratic optimization problems common in portfolio theory are mathematically equivalent, so the fragility is structural, not cosmetic. Embedding bid-ask-spread transaction costs substantially changes composition.

**Interpretability.** MVO sits at the structural-interpretability extreme: every weight traces analytically to an input. On Interpretability & Governance it earns a clear pass. The subtle cost: transparency of mechanism does not imply reliability of output, and **auditable noise is still noise.**

### 2.2 The Markowitz Optimization Enigma

Michaud's 1989 article poses the question of whether the "optimized" portfolio is genuinely optimal, documenting practitioner indifference despite theoretical appeal. The enigma resolves into two separable defects.

**Normality is not the binding flaw.** Lai, Xing, and Chen locate the primary difficulty not in distributional shape but in the fact that parameters must be estimated at all. Even under perfect normality, plug-in use of estimated moments degrades the portfolio—making **estimation error, not non-normality, the deeper flaw.**

**Estimation error alone causes failure.** Mynbayeva, Lamb, and Zhao isolate estimation error as the operative cause. The mechanism chains cleanly: the sample mean is a high-variance estimator → the optimizer treats noise as signal → concentrated, extreme weights → realized underperformance the 1/N benchmark routinely beats. **No realistic investor has 250 years of stationary data**, so the theoretical superiority is unreachable in practice.

**The 1/N debate.** Bock's sixteen-strategy test found none consistently outperformed 1/N or minimum-variance. The contrarian position—the FAJ article "In Defense of Optimization: The Fallacy of 1/N"—contends criticisms reflect mis-implementation, not inherent flaw. Zakamulin sharpens this from the other side: apparent optimization wins on Kenneth French data may stem from incidental low-volatility-factor exposure.

**The defensible synthesis: raw sample-mean MVO is fragile, but MVO augmented with a better conditioning signal can recover the theoretical edge.** Yuan and Zhou show that combining estimated rules with 1/N in small universes, or with machine-learning portfolios in large ones, can beat 1/N when those components are profitable.

**Repair strategies.** The field's center of gravity has shifted from trusting the in-sample optimum to validating against held-out data. The Michaud resampling approach treats estimation error explicitly; Barroso brings realized out-of-sample errors into the construction step.

### 2.3 MVO Scorecard

| Axis | Verdict |
|------|-------------|
| Interpretability & Governance | **Pass** — analytic traceability unmatched |
| Risk Measurement Fidelity | Conditional — variance biased low in high dimensions |
| Return Prediction Reliability | **Fail** — no forecasting mechanism; noisy sample mean |
| Allocation Robustness | Raw fails, repaired passes (resampling, out-of-sample) |
| Regime Adaptability | **Fail** — no state-conditioning channel |
| Implementation Practicality | Mixed — trivial compute, impractical data demand |

**MVO's scorecard is the mirror image of the deep-learning families: strong where they are weak (governance), weak where they are strong (return prediction, regime adaptation).** It is best understood not as a strategy to deploy raw but as an allocation engine whose inputs every later family is built to repair.

## 3. Black-Litterman: The Bayesian Blender

Black-Litterman begins where MVO breaks down: rather than feeding noisy sample means into a quadratic program, it starts from the market's implied equilibrium returns and treats investor opinion as a Bayesian update, producing a posterior **far more stable than raw sample estimates**. **Its central design move is to replace MVO's statistically fragile sample-mean input with a market-implied prior that requires no return forecast to get started.**

### 3.1 Equilibrium Priors and Reverse Optimization

**The market-implied prior.** PyPortfolioOpt frames the model as combining a prior estimate of returns—canonically the market-implied returns—with views to yield a posterior. If the market-cap portfolio is the optimal mean-variance portfolio for the representative investor, reverse optimization solves the first-order condition backwards to recover implied excess returns. **The analyst contributes only deviations from that baseline rather than the whole return vector.**

This shifts the estimation burden. Where MVO amplifies mean-estimation error through the optimizer, **Black-Litterman's prior sidesteps the mean-estimation step entirely, importing the market's collective estimate instead of a single desk's.**

**Risk side unchanged.** Black-Litterman inherits MVO's variance-based risk; the scalar τ (scaling prior uncertainty) is the only new risk parameter. The covariance estimator still carries its own error, so the high-dimensionality risk-underestimation problem persists. **The model addresses only the mean-input half of the estimation problem**; the resolution is to pair it with a shrinkage covariance estimator.

**The no-view limit.** With no views, the posterior equals the prior and the optimizer reproduces market-cap weights. This deliberate default avoids the concentrated corner solutions raw MVO generates, yielding diversified, **low-turnover weights robust to input noise by design**. On Allocation Robustness, the prior stage earns a strong pass.

### 3.2 Investor Views and the Bayesian Posterior

A Black-Litterman view is specified by three objects: a pick matrix **P** (which assets a view touches), a vector **Q** (the asserted returns), and a matrix **Ω** (view uncertainty). **The relative-view capability most distinguishes Black-Litterman from MVO**: an analyst rarely has a confident absolute forecast but frequently has a confident relative ranking.

**Ω is the most consequential and criticized parameter.** Idzorek's contribution converts an unintuitive variance into a 0–100% confidence statement. The frontier is empirical estimation: data-estimated Ω removes subjective specification, **significantly increasing historical allocation performance**.

**Graceful degradation.** A high-confidence view moves the portfolio substantially; a low-confidence view barely perturbs market-default weights. Where naked MVO requires ~250 years of data to beat 1/N at 25 assets, **the posterior's shrinkage toward the prior delivers stable weights immediately**, at the cost of diluting any genuine signal. This positions Black-Litterman as one disciplined way to perform the blending Yuan and Zhou identify.

### 3.3 Subjectivity: Feature or Flaw

The contested attribute is subjectivity—simultaneously the model's most criticized feature and its principal design advantage. **The honest resolution: Black-Litterman moves subjectivity from the opaque uncertainty parameters, where it did the most damage, to the transparent view vector, where it is at least auditable.**

The deeper point is agnosticism. **Black-Litterman is agnostic about the source of its views, which is exactly what makes the Hybrid BL+ML Framework possible: swap the human view for a model view and the Bayesian blend is unchanged.** A fragile ML forecast enters as a view, which the posterior shrinks toward equilibrium in proportion to its estimated uncertainty—capturing part of the signal while retaining out-of-sample stability. This is the analytical core of why the hybrid is more than the sum of its parts.

**The governance verdict: explicit subjectivity is strictly preferable to hidden subjectivity.** A documented view can be challenged, revised, and attributed; a black-box tilt cannot.

## 4. CVaR and Robust Optimization: Tail Awareness

Where MVO trusts a single covariance and Black-Litterman stabilizes the return vector, this family attacks a different vulnerability: the non-normal, fat-tailed loss distribution. Two related entities populate it—**CVaR optimization** (replacing two-sided variance with a downside tail measure) and **robust optimization** (treating inputs as uncertain rather than known).

### 4.1 CVaR as a Downside-Risk Allocator

**Distribution-free inputs.** CVaR abandons normality, working directly on a scenario panel—historical, bootstrapped, or Monte Carlo—plus a confidence level. Because risk is defined on the empirical loss distribution, no parametric form is assumed.

**The coherent tail measure.** VaR reports a loss threshold; CVaR (Expected Shortfall) reports the average loss conditional on exceeding it, so at 95% it describes the mean of the worst 5% of outcomes. CVaR is a **coherent** risk measure satisfying subadditivity, which VaR can violate—**only the latter rewards diversification in every case**. Where variance is blind to skew and kurtosis, CVaR reads the tail shape directly.

**Tractable allocation.** CVaR is convex in the weights, so the Rockafellar–Uryasev linearization recasts the problem as a tractable **linear program** rather than a quadratic one. The linear structure scales gracefully and accommodates transaction-cost and turnover terms directly.

**Regulatory alignment.** The Basel Committee's Fundamental Review of the Trading Book replaced VaR with Expected Shortfall as the market-risk capital measure, giving CVaR a supervisory meaning risk committees already understand.

**The signature failure mode is scenario dependence.** A historical window excluding a crash yields a dangerously optimistic shortfall. **The paradox—that the measure built to capture catastrophic losses is blindest to catastrophes absent from its sample—resolves not by fixing the measure but by enriching the scenario generator.** A second limitation: tail estimates from short samples are noisier than variance estimates, so 99% CVaR is far less stable than the 95% figure.

### 4.2 Robust and Worst-Case Formulations

Robust optimization attacks input fragility directly: rather than trusting a point estimate, it optimizes against the worst case within an uncertainty set (typically ellipsoid or box) around the estimate.

**Two-layer structure.** A worst-case CVaR program minimizes tail loss under the least favorable distribution in the set—**hedging against being wrong about the distribution**, where nominal CVaR trusts the distribution it is given. Three named disciplines stabilize the return input: Barroso's out-of-sample errors, Michaud's resampling, and Mynbayeva et al.'s uncertainty-set discipline.

**The 1/N convergence tension.** Robust weights shrink toward minimum-variance or equal-weight as the uncertainty set widens. DeMiguel et al. questioned whether optimized strategies beat 1/N at all, and Bock found none consistently did. **Robustness that collapses entirely to 1/N has not obviously improved on doing nothing; the resolution is calibration—sizing the set to the confidence region the data support, so conservatism is earned rather than arbitrary.** The uncertainty set's auditability is a governance advantage that a trained DRL policy cannot match.

**Chapter verdict:** the CVaR/Robust family does not predict markets better than its predecessors—**it distrusts its own inputs better.** Both buy governance and tail-awareness at the cost of scenario dependence or over-conservatism, converging on a common insight: the binding constraint in real allocation is estimation error, not the choice of objective.

| Entity | Risk measure | Defense | Best regime |
|---|---|---|---|
| CVaR | Expected Shortfall | Scenario enrichment | Fat-tailed, crash-prone |
| Robust | Nested worst-case | Uncertainty set | High-dim, data-scarce |

## 5. Deep Learning Forecasting Models

Deep learning forecasters enter as flexible function approximators mapping high-dimensional, noisy inputs to predicted returns or directly to weights. The headline claim is concrete: a hybrid CNN-LSTM model combined with LLM sentiment achieves a **1.623 Sharpe ratio, reported to outperform MVO, MAD, and CVaR by 104–300% on ETFs**. That performance, and the opacity that comes with it, defines the family.

### 5.1 CNN-LSTM and Transformer Predictors

**CNN-LSTM price-and-sentiment forecaster.** The convolutional layer extracts local price patterns; the LSTM carries them across longer sequences; LLM sentiment adds a second input stream. The forecaster owns the return estimate and the downstream convex program owns the tail—**the defining architectural fact of the predict-then-optimize pipeline.** Its named failure mode is **overfitting under nonstationarity**: financial series are among the least stationary data any learner faces. On Interpretability & Governance, the raw CNN-LSTM **fails without a bolted-on explanation layer.**

**Temporal Fusion Transformer (TFT) allocator.** Attention makes the relevance weighting of timesteps and features explicit and inspectable. The XAI framework built on TFTs and SHAP reports Sharpe ratios of **1.47 (conservative), 1.28 (balanced), 1.09 (aggressive) against 0.78 for a 60/40 benchmark, with a 35–45% reduction in maximum drawdown**. **That drawdown reduction, not the Sharpe alone, is the more governance-relevant statistic.** The TFT-plus-SHAP design earns a **conditional pass** on interpretability where the raw CNN-LSTM fails—but post-hoc attribution explains a fitted model's behavior, not the economic truth, so a convincing SHAP plot over a spurious feature is a governance hazard.

### 5.2 Feature Engineering, Non-Linearity, and Signal Decay

**The sentiment channel** shows the strongest recent gains. Sentiment-augmented PPO using LLaMA 3.3 raises Sharpe from **1.55 to 1.90** in a three-asset portfolio; a Refinitiv-news configuration lifts Sharpe from **1.67 to 2.07 and annualized return from 57% to 83%** on ten assets.

**The signature limitation is signal decay.** The causal logic: a feature with genuine predictive content is discovered → imitation as participants trade it → spread erosion until marginal contribution approaches zero. **Any backtested Sharpe from a sentiment signal should be treated as an upper bound on forward performance, not a central estimate.**

**The data-hunger constraint** is the quietest and most binding. DeMiguel et al.'s ~3,000-month benchmark for even a simple estimator implies a network with far more free parameters needs at least comparable effective data—which monthly histories cannot supply. Bock's null result is the sobering baseline against which deep-learning Sharpe figures must be read.

### 5.3 The Interpretability Problem

Opacity is itself a risk—**model risk—and it is the one risk the network cannot measure about itself.** A variance or CVaR number quantifies market risk; nothing in the raw CNN-LSTM quantifies the risk that its learned mapping is spurious. **This governance asymmetry makes deep models uncomfortable for regulated institutions: the model reports market risk precisely and its own model risk not at all.**

The explainable-AI response—integrated gradients and SHAP attribution—converts a structural fail into a conditional pass. **But the contrarian caution is sharp: a convincing attribution over a spurious feature is more dangerous than honest opacity, because it manufactures false confidence.** The practical resolution many institutions adopt is the hybrid route: route the opaque signal through a transparent Black-Litterman view step, keeping allocation mechanics legible even where signal generation is not.

| Model variant | Sharpe | Benchmark | Key claim | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| CNN-LSTM + LLM sentiment | 1.623 | MVO/MAD/CVaR | 104–300% improvement | |
| TFT + SHAP (conservative) | 1.47 | 0.78 (60/40) | 35–45% drawdown reduction | |
| SAPPO (LLaMA 3.3, 3-asset) | 1.90 | 1.55 (PPO) | sentiment lift | |
| SAPPO (10-asset, Refinitiv) | 2.07 | 1.67 (PPO) | 57%→83% ann. return | |

**Chapter verdict:** the family buys headline performance with two coupled liabilities that are the same flexibility viewed from different angles—**signal decay over the deployment horizon and structural opacity at every horizon.** The flexibility that fits non-linear structure at discovery is the same flexibility that fits noise and resists audit. None of the cited Sharpe figures is reported under an identical-data, identical-constraint protocol with full transaction-cost accounting, so whether the edge survives realistic frictions remains open.

## 6. Deep Reinforcement Learning Allocators

DRL recasts portfolio choice as sequential control: an agent observes market state, sets weights, and learns a policy that maximizes cumulative reward—without ever writing down the market's law of motion. A systematic review of 167 articles (2017–2025) finds reinforcement learning offers advantages over traditional methods, most clearly in market-making.

### 6.1 Policy Learning Without Known Dynamics

**Model-free premise.** The agent trains directly on historical data, consuming a state representation plus a scalar reward rather than a mean vector and covariance. **The return estimate is implicit, embedded in the policy network rather than exposed as a vector a risk committee can inspect**—a trade made deliberately.

**Risk inside the policy.** The most sophisticated designs model the full return distribution: the VD-MEAC algorithm has the critic learn the complete distribution of future returns for risk control. **This captures the same tail sensitivity CVaR targets—but by learning the distribution rather than imposing a constraint analytically**, burying the tail logic inside a network.

**Return prediction as byproduct.** Because the agent optimizes realized cumulative reward on training data rather than a predictive loss on held-out returns, **it is structurally exposed to overfitting the specific price path it trained on.** Retrained across different windows, roughly the same agent can swing from top-quartile to bottom-quartile depending on whether the evaluation window resembles training. Verdict: conditional pass in stationary regimes, fail under structural breaks.

**Allocation mechanics.** Continuous-control actor-critic agents (DDPG, PPO, SAC) output weight vectors directly via a softmax projection onto the budget-constrained simplex, fitting the problem more cleanly than discrete-action agents (DQN), which scale combinatorially. The closed loop lets DRL **adapt turnover endogenously.**

### 6.2 Reward Design, Costs, and Governance

**The reward is the true objective function.** It plays the role the Black-Litterman view plays for the Bayesian family—the explicit injection point for human judgment. The contrarian reframing: **DRL is not more subjective than Black-Litterman, it is less legibly subjective**—Black-Litterman exposes preference as auditable parameters while DRL compiles it into network weights.

**Endogenous cost control.** Transaction costs can be written directly into the reward. **Because the cost term sits inside the reward, the agent learns to trade only when expected improvement exceeds the cost—producing endogenous turnover control rather than a hand-tuned penalty.** This is the family's strongest point.

**The governance problem.** DRL is the hardest family to govern, because different algorithms trained on the same data do not converge to the same behavior. **A risk committee can reproduce a Markowitz weight vector exactly from its inputs, but cannot reproduce a DRL policy without the exact training seed, data window, and hyperparameters.** On Interpretability & Governance it returns the weakest verdict of any family.

**Regime adaptability is its strongest pass.** A closed-loop RL system that re-optimizes as regimes change does structurally what a statically estimated covariance cannot. The named failure mode is **silent regime overfitting**: a policy that backtests brilliantly on the training regime and degrades without warning when it turns.

**Chapter verdict:** DRL is **the most regime-adaptive and most cost-aware family, and simultaneously the least reproducible and least interpretable**—not independent defects but the two faces of the same model-free design choice. It earns its place not as a general-purpose replacement but as the **adaptive specialist whose governance cost is justified only where regime shifts defeat the classical families.** By 2030, the likely production configuration is a constrained, regime-switching component embedded in a closed-loop system with explicit guardrails.

## 7. Decision-Focused and End-to-End Learning

Decision-Focused Learning rejects the premise every other family shares: that prediction and allocation are separable. It collapses the two into one trainable object, training the predictor against the **downstream decision loss rather than a prediction loss**.

### 7.1 Prediction Error vs. Decision Quality

**The training-target mismatch.** Crego, Kvaerner, and Stam document that models trained to predict realized returns recover a different object than the conditional expected return an investor requires. The causal core: a decision loss penalizes the predictor for portfolio regret, which leads it to allocate limited capacity toward the assets and moments the optimizer is most sensitive to—producing allocations less destabilized by that sensitivity. **By aligning the training target with the allocation it serves, this family delivers higher decision-relevant value than an error-minimizing model of equal nominal R².**

The optimization-defense reading frames the family as one response to the "mis-implementation, not inherent flaw" argument[^9]: if the problem is implementation, training the predictor against the decision aligns implementation with objective.

### 7.2 Embedding the Optimizer in Training

The defining move is placing the portfolio optimizer **inside the computational graph** so gradients flow backward through the allocation step into the predictor's parameters. This requires the downstream problem to be convex and smooth.

**Exact constraints.** Because the forward pass is a genuine convex solve, hard constraints—no shorting, sector caps, leverage limits—are satisfied exactly at every step, producing an **auditable constraint trace that penalty-shaped DRL objectives cannot match.** A convex CVaR term embeds cleanly (VaR, being non-convex, does not).

**Limitations.** The moment the problem leaves the convex world—integer lots, cardinality limits, discrete regime switches—the smooth derivative may cease to exist. The named failure mode is **gradient collapse at active constraints**: when the solution rests on a constraint face, the derivative can vanish, starving the predictor of signal. A second cost: solving an optimization at every training step is far costlier than a network forward pass.

| Family | Training target | Constraints | Solver cost | Best regime |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Predict-then-Optimize | Statistical error | Downstream only | Low | Stable, small universe |
| Decision-Focused | Decision regret | Exact (convex) | High | Large, convex universe |
| DRL allocators | Cumulative reward | Penalty-shaped | Medium | Adaptive, regime-shifting |

Embedding the optimizer buys exact convex feasibility and decision-aligned prediction at the price of per-step solver cost and gradient fragility. The verdict on Allocation Robustness is strong; on Implementation Practicality it places below the hybrid for near-term deployment.

## 8. Cross-Model Comparison: Risk Measurement

Risk measurement is where the families part ways most cleanly, because the choice of risk functional is logically prior to both return prediction and allocation. **The decisive split is not classical-versus-learned but symmetric-second-moment versus asymmetric-tail-aware, with a third orthogonal axis—whether the model treats risk inputs as uncertain—separating static optimizers from regime-aware and Bayesian ones.**

### 8.1 Variance vs. Tail and Coherent Measures

The move from variance to CVaR is **a trade of estimation ease for tail fidelity, not a free improvement.**

| Risk functional | Shape | Coherent? | Estimation difficulty | Tractability |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Variance (MVO) | Symmetric, 2nd moment | No | Low | Convex QP |
| VaR | Quantile, one-sided | No | Moderate | Non-convex |
| CVaR | Conditional tail mean | Yes | High (sparse tail) | Convex LP |
| Learned risk (DL/DRL) | Implicit, data-shaped | Not guaranteed | Very high | Non-convex |

Rockafellar and Uryasev's foundational result—**low-CVaR portfolios necessarily have low VaR**—means optimizing the conditional-tail average obtains quantile control as a by-product. The open operational question is whether the risk-measure choice reorders which assets look riskiest[^47]: for near-elliptical equities the ranking is largely stable (measure choice second-order); for options, credit, or emerging-market exposure it diverges (first-order). CVaR is **coherent but fragile**—its tail estimate is statistically harder to estimate than a quantile because it averages over the sparsely-sampled tail.

### 8.2 Covariance Instability and Model Uncertainty

Dropping the fiction that inputs are given reframes every functional as conditional on an estimator that may be wrong. El Karoui's analysis identifies a specific distortion: when assets are many relative to the sample, **the optimizer systematically underestimates realized risk**. This is structural, not a tuning issue.

The three remedies—**shrinkage, resampling, and Bayesian blending**—all work by treating inputs as uncertain. Resampling averages the risk number across simulated draws; out-of-sample tuning calibrates against realized error; Black-Litterman anchors the posterior to equilibrium, dampening the inverse-covariance amplification. The latent-variable Bayesian approach removes reliance on subjectively specified views entirely. **Model uncertainty is itself a risk dimension, orthogonal to the choice of functional.**

### 8.3 Handling Non-Normality

**Deep learning forecasters** can capture fat tails, skewness, and volatility clustering that variance cannot, with risk learned implicitly—but the risk object is opaque, earning the clearest **fail** on Interpretability & Governance.

**DRL** encodes risk in the reward, making it as non-normality-aware as the reward design permits; the transformer-DRL-plus-Black-Litterman construction inherits Bayesian input stabilization while learning the policy.

**Decision-focused learning** inherits whatever risk measure the downstream optimizer uses, but trains the prediction to make that objective perform out-of-sample.

**The Hybrid BL+ML Framework** has the most explicit non-normality machinery: GARCH models volatility clustering, EVT models fat tails, and Copula models tail dependence—a far richer representation than variance, while confining the black-box component to view generation.

**Chapter verdict:** non-normality fidelity and structural interpretability are inversely related across the families, and **the hybrid construction is the only one that breaks the trade-off** by confining its black box to view generation while keeping the risk functional and optimizer auditable.

| Entity | Risk functional | Non-normality | Interpretability | Input-uncertainty |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| MVO | Variance | None | Highest | None |
| Black-Litterman | Variance (downstream) | Stabilizes inputs | High | Bayesian prior |
| CVaR/Robust | Conditional tail mean | Direct (scenario) | Moderate-high | Distribution-free |
| Deep Learning | Implicit/learned | Strong (data-shaped) | Low | Overfit-prone |
| DRL | Reward-embedded | Strong if designed | Lowest | Implicit |
| Decision-Focused | Optimizer's measure | Inherits downstream | Low-moderate | Decision-regret |
| Hybrid BL+ML | Variance + GARCH-EVT-Copula | Strongest | Moderate-high | Prior + learned views |

## 9. Cross-Model Comparison: Return Prediction

The families differ less in how they optimize than in what they believe about the expected return. **They occupy three belief regimes: the historical-mean regime (Markowitz), the equilibrium-anchored regime (Black-Litterman and hybrids), and the learned-signal regime (deep learning, DRL, decision-focused)**—each trading predictive content against estimation fragility on a different margin.

### 9.1 Three Belief Regimes

**Historical-mean (MVO).** The sample mean is unbiased but extremely noisy, with standard error declining only as the square root of sample length—why thousands of months are required. The named failure mode is the **Markowitz Optimization Enigma**: the optimizer over-weights assets whose sample mean was inflated by luck.

**Equilibrium-anchored (Black-Litterman + hybrids).** **The decisive advantage on Return Prediction Reliability: by discounting aggressive views in proportion to their stated uncertainty Ω, it mechanically tempers the error-maximization that cripples plug-in MVO.** The hybrid generates views mechanically via GARCH–EVT–Copula and LSTM rather than subjectively. **The paradox—a model celebrated for human judgment improved by removing the human—resolves because the genuine value was never the subjectivity but the shrinkage-toward-equilibrium structure, which survives when views are machine-generated.**

**Learned-signal.** Gu, Kelly, and Xiu demonstrate large economic gains from machine-learning return forecasts. The contrarian complement: high in-sample predictive content need not translate into tradeable realized return. On Interpretability & Governance these families **fail relative to both classical families.**

**The data-trust dial is the master variable:** MVO trusts the data completely and pays in estimation noise; Black-Litterman imposes a strong equilibrium prior and pays in potential misspecification; the learned families impose almost no prior and pay in overfitting and opacity.

### 9.2 Overfitting, Non-Stationarity, and the Complexity Trade-off

**The virtue-of-complexity finding.** Kelly et al. prove theoretically that **simple models severely understate return predictability compared to complex models in which the number of parameters exceeds the number of observations**—a direct repudiation of parsimony orthodoxy.

**The overfitting counterweight.** Deep learning on non-stationary temporal data produces overfitted models that underperform under regime changes; over-stationarization removes the ability to capture bursty events. **The tension resolves by conditioning on stationarity: complexity is a virtue when the data-generating process is stable enough that extra parameters learn signal, and a vice when it shifts fast enough that they learn the last regime's noise.**

The Nonstationarity-Complexity Trade-off is causal: a more complex model lowers misspecification error → demands a longer training window → increases exposure to non-stationarity by reaching into stale regimes. The joint model-and-window selection method **improves out-of-sample R² by 14–23% on average across 17 industry portfolios, and by at least 80 basis points during the 2001 recession**—the largest quantified gain, concentrated where non-stationarity bites hardest.

**Signal decay.** Published return predictors **lose 58% of their performance after publication**, split roughly evenly between statistical bias and arbitrage. Here the equilibrium anchor earns its keep: by discounting aggressive, recently-discovered views, it under-weights exactly the signal most vulnerable to post-publication compression.

### 9.3 Forecastability Limits

The intermarket-data study finds bonds, index futures, and commodities futures produce statistically significant positive impact on S&P 500 prediction, **directly challenging the efficient-market hypothesis**. The contrarian reading: if these signals proxy time-varying risk premia, the predictability is exactly what an efficient market with priced factors would produce.

**The practical forecastability ceiling is low.** With predictors losing 58% post-publication and the best adaptive methods recovering only 14–23% of R², the realistic forecastable component sits in the low-single-digit R² range for most liquid markets. **The implication is decisive: because the forecastable signal is small and perishable, the marginal value of a more powerful predictor is capped, shifting competitive advantage toward the families that convert a weak signal into robust weights efficiently.**

| Family | Return Estimator | Predictive Content | Reliability | Dominant Failure |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mean-Variance | Historical sample mean | Low | Very fragile (~3000+ months) | Error maximization |
| Black-Litterman | Equilibrium prior + views | Moderate | Robust via shrinkage | Subjective view bias |
| Hybrid BL+ML | ML views + prior | Moderate–High | Robust; data-driven | View-model misspecification |
| Deep Learning | Learned nonlinear map | High (in-sample) | Conditional; decays fast | Overfitting + 58% decay |
| DRL | Implicit in policy | High | Conditional; regime-sensitive | Non-stationarity collapse |
| Decision-Focused | Trained against allocation | Moderate | Robust by construction | Mismatch resolved |

**Chapter verdict:** return-prediction reliability, not predictive power, separates the families—and **the families that distrust their own signal most (Black-Litterman and decision-focused) are best positioned to convert a weak, perishable edge into durable out-of-sample performance.**

## 10. Cross-Model Comparison: Allocation Mechanics

How estimates become weights is where the families diverge most visibly. **The thesis: allocation mechanics—not input quality alone—govern realized cost and weight stability, and the families can be ordered by whether their objective can natively absorb a transaction-cost term.**

### 10.1 Objectives and Constraints

**The three classical families** establish a single structural fact: **the capacity to absorb a transaction-cost term natively is determined by the objective's mathematical form, not input quality.** MVO and Black-Litterman share a quadratic objective carrying no native cost term—turnover is whatever the solver produces. The CVaR convex program embeds cost and turnover penalties directly. The Bayesian posterior improves inputs to the quadratic program but does not change its structure.

### 10.2 Cost Internalization in Learned Families

The capacity to internalize transaction costs tracks how tightly the forecast is welded to the decision:

- **Deep forecasting (predict-then-optimize)** severs forecast from decision, must bolt cost on downstream; tends to high turnover unless penalized.
- **DRL** folds cost into the reward, so the agent learns to self-limit turnover without a hand-tuned penalty.
- **Decision-focused** welds forecast to decision via a differentiable optimizer, internalizing cost in the training loss.

| Family | Objective form | Native cost term | Input sensitivity | Turnover |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| MVO | Quadratic | No | Very high | High |
| Black-Litterman | Quadratic (posterior input) | No | Moderate | Moderate |
| CVaR/Robust | Convex/linear | Yes (embeddable) | Scenario-dependent | Controllable |
| Deep Forecasting | Two-stage | No (bolt-on) | High | High |
| DRL | Learned policy | Yes (in reward) | Regime-dependent | Endogenous |
| Decision-Focused | End-to-end | Yes (in loss) | Decision-aligned | Endogenous |
| Hybrid BL+ML | Posterior + learned | Partial | Reduced | Moderate |

**The central pattern: input sensitivity and turnover fall together as the objective moves from the cost-blind quadratic form toward the cost-internalizing convex and learned forms.**

## 11. The Assumptions–Limitations Trade-off Matrix

Three tensions organize the comparison: **statistical rigor against subjective judgment, predictive power against interpretability, and theoretical elegance against real-world implementability.** Each resolves differently per family, and the resolution—not raw capability—should drive selection.

### 11.1 Rigor vs. Judgment

Raw MVO's statistical rigor is self-defeating at realistic sample sizes; Black-Litterman's judgment injection repairs it but imports view-misspecification risk; the zero-input 1/N benchmark shows the winning move is to blend estimation with structure. The contested verdict resolves through Yuan and Zhou: **raw, unshrunk optimization loses to the zero-estimation benchmark, while shrunk or blended optimization can win—because blending imports 1/N's estimation-error immunity while retaining the optimizer's signal**. **Statistical rigor and subjective judgment are not rival philosophies but complementary regularizers of the same fragile quadratic program.**

### 11.2 Predictive Power vs. Interpretability

**Interpretability loss is locatable, not uniform.** Pure deep forecasters and DRL allocators place opacity in the load-bearing decision itself; **Decision-Focused Learning confines opacity to the predictor and keeps the allocation step transparent.** The deep-learning-limits literature establishes the residual opacity is irreducible without accuracy loss; post-hoc explanation is not genuine transparency—LLMs reproduce feature-importance rankings under prompts but fail to generate autonomous SHAP-aligned explanations. The practical implication: governance-constrained institutions can **locate the black box where it does least governance harm.**

### 11.3 Elegance vs. Implementability

CVaR is coherent but estimation-fragile; the Hybrid BL+ML Framework is powerful but architecturally complex. **The elegance-implementability gap closes through hybridization, not abandonment.** The Hybrid is the only family scoring a pass on every criterion at once—tail-aware risk fidelity, data-grounded return reliability, equilibrium-stabilized allocation, view-level interpretability, and regime adaptability.

## 12. Performance Across Market Regimes

**Regime-conditioned performance, not average performance, separates the families.** Regime-aware methods that explicitly infer state deliver materially higher risk-adjusted performance: the Wasserstein HMM portfolio reaches a **Sharpe ratio of 2.18 against 1.59 for equal-weight and 1.18 for SPX buy-and-hold**.

### 12.1 Bull, Bear, Crisis, and Low-Volatility

**MVO** assumes a stable joint distribution that holds only inside a single regime; across a bull-to-bear transition both the mean and covariance shift, so inputs computed over a straddling window are internally inconsistent. **In the crisis regime, variance is the least faithful of all risk measures**, because its symmetry assumption is violated exactly when fidelity matters most. On Regime Adaptability: clear **fail**.

**CVaR** is distribution-agnostic and passes Risk Measurement Fidelity where variance fails in crisis. The "coherent but fragile" paradox—a coherent measure that is nonetheless fragile—**resolves through sampling: coherence is a property of the true distribution, while fragility arises from estimating it on too few tail observations**; the fix is regime-stratified scenario sampling.

**DRL** places adaptivity in the policy: a well-trained policy can switch behavior within the same forward pass if training data spanned that regime. The danger is that regimes absent from training are handled by extrapolation. Verdict: **conditional pass**—adapts within its training distribution but fails the governance bar.

**The structural claim: adaptivity and auditability trade off along a single axis, and no single family occupies both ends.** MVO places no adaptivity and fails across breaks; CVaR places it in the risk measure and holds tails in crisis; DRL places it in the policy and adapts within but not beyond its training regimes.

### 12.2 Structural Breaks

**Explicit regime inference beats both static optimization and naïve diversification.** Regime-switching jump-model allocators label periods bullish/bearish via a statistical jump model, then forecast returns and risk per regime. The sparse jump model yields positive Sharpe across all factors with low cross-factor correlation; the jump-model-plus-MPC framework adapts weights to breaks while respecting costs, and outperforms both buy-and-hold and Markov-switching allocation. The Hybrid BL+ML Framework with regime-aware blending confirms the Sharpe 2.18 result. **Explicit regime inference is the decisive adaptability lever.**

## 13. Empirical Evaluation and Backtesting

**The central empirical finding: backtest protocol choices—not model sophistication—dominate the reported performance spread across most published comparisons.**

### 13.1 Out-of-Sample Validation

A credible backtest holds data, constraints, and cost model fixed across every competing family so the only variable is allocation logic. Key disciplines:

- **Strict temporal separation.** The information set at each rebalance contains only data realized strictly before that date; the input-estimation step must be inside the out-of-sample loop, never fit once on the full sample.
- **Cost-in-objective.** Cost cannot be a post-hoc subtraction; it must enter the optimization, because a cost-aware optimizer chooses different weights than a cost-blind one.
- **Walk-forward with purge gaps**, scoring forecasts against realized returns never visible during training.

The binding constraint is sample length: a few decades of monthly data contain very few independent regime transitions, so even a clean walk-forward rests on a small number of genuine stress events.

### 13.2 Metrics

A fixed, multi-metric panel chosen *before* the backtest is the only defense against metric-shopping, because a variance-based metric and a tail-based metric can rank the same two strategies in opposite order when returns are skewed.

| Metric | Penalizes | Normality required? | Best use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sharpe | Total volatility | Implicitly yes | Calm, near-Gaussian |
| Sortino | Downside volatility | Less so | Asymmetric returns |
| Max drawdown | Worst peak-to-trough | No | Path-dependent pain |
| Realized CVaR | Average tail loss | No (coherent) | Left-tail disclosure |
| Turnover | Trading cost drag | No | Net-of-cost realism |

### 13.3 The Naive Diversification Benchmark

**The single most important benchmark is the equally-weighted 1/N portfolio**, because it carries zero estimation error—and the evidence on whether sophisticated families beat it is genuinely unsettled. DeMiguel et al. quantified the estimation cost: ~3,000 months to beat 1/N with 25 assets, ~6,000 with 50. Bock found none of sixteen strategies consistently outperformed. **That is the empirical core of the "MVO is broken" position.**

The rebuttal indicts implementation, not optimization[^9]: a constrained, shrinkage-based allocator is a different animal from the unconstrained textbook optimizer. Yuan and Zhou reframe the debate toward conditional combination—combine estimated rules with 1/N in small universes, or with profitable ML portfolios in large ones.

**The contested verdict, stated plainly: raw unconstrained MVO is effectively broken out-of-sample for realistic sample lengths, but the optimization principle is not—it survives once constraints, shrinkage, and cost-awareness are added.** Empirical verdicts about model families are verdicts about protocols first and algorithms second.

## 15. Toward a Hybrid, General-Purpose Framework

**The recommended architecture is a three-layer stack: a Black-Litterman equilibrium prior, machine-learning signals injected as Black-Litterman views, and a robust CVaR-aware optimizer at the output.** The feasibility question—whether classic and learned strengths can be combined—receives a qualified yes: the coupling is implementable today, but its value over simpler baselines remains conditional on validation discipline.

### 15.1 Architecture

Four integration patterns were evaluated before committing to one:

1. **BL-prior-with-ML-views** (the committed backbone): an ML forecast supplies the view vector Q, and its predictive uncertainty supplies Ω.
2. **Bayesian deep learning**: the network produces a posterior over predictions, feeding calibrated variance into Ω.
3. **Deep learning inside robust optimization**: a network estimates returns and covariance jointly, populating the moments a CVaR optimizer consumes.
4. **End-to-end decision-focused**: a single network maps features to weights.

**The design logic: the equilibrium prior contributes stability, the learned view contributes forward-looking content, and the CVaR objective contributes tail discipline—each at the exact point in the pipeline where that property is scarcest.** Pattern four is declined as the default because a fully learned policy offers no documented intermediate view to audit.

### 15.2 Component-to-Strength Mapping

| Layer | Source Family | Strength Borrowed | Failure Mode Managed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Prior | Black-Litterman | Equilibrium anchor + shrinkage-at-source | Ω miscalibration |
| Middle | Deep Learning | Nonlinear signal as bounded view | 58% post-publication decay |
| Output | CVaR/Robust | Coherent tail pricing | Scenario fragility |

**The shrinkage-at-source property is the single most valuable contribution Black-Litterman makes**, because it bounds the tilt a noisy learned forecast can induce rather than letting that forecast drive an all-in corner solution. The structural defense against signal decay follows directly: because a decayed signal carries wider predictive uncertainty, a well-calibrated Ω widens, shrinking the stale view back toward equilibrium.

### 15.3 Governance by Design

Several 2026 developments reshape what a deployable hybrid must satisfy. Regulators now view ML models as systemic risk factors, ending the experimental era and requiring explainability for critical decisions. The **revised interagency model-risk guidance, effective April 17, 2026, replaced prior guidance with a principles-based framework that explicitly excludes generative and agentic AI** and introduces a $30 billion asset threshold.

**The exclusion means a view-channel hybrid—using discriminative forecasting, not generative models—sits squarely inside scope and must meet the effective-challenge standard in full.** The view channel is the governance pivot: because Black-Litterman requires a (P, Q, Ω) triple, the learned signal has nowhere to enter except through that documented interface, which is the SR 11-7 effective-challenge principle made operational. **A supervisor challenges the view, not the weights.** Pattern four, by contrast, exposes only a weight vector whose derivation is the network's opaque internal state.

The Total Portfolio Approach serves as a cautionary case—argued unsuitable for mid-market pension plans because it weakens governance and adds cost. **The lesson: integration for its own sake degrades governance unless each component remains separately documentable—exactly the property the view-channel hybrid preserves and pattern four sacrifices.**

### 15.4 Validation and Implementation Risk

The hybrid follows a three-stage chronological backtesting protocol—In-Sample tuning, Walk-Forward Analysis with **purge gaps**, and an Out-of-Sample holdout touched exactly once. Random cross-validation, standard in general ML, destroys the temporal structure that makes a financial backtest meaningful.

**The hybrid inherits a demanding acceptance bar: it must beat 1/N net of transaction costs out of sample, not merely in a tuned backtest**. Yuan and Zhou give the constructive path: combine estimated rules with a stable anchor—precisely the Black-Litterman shrinkage structure.

| Risk | Control | Grounding |
|---|---|---|
| Overfitting | Single-touch OOS holdout, variant logging | Spurious predictability |
| Data quality | Retrain on recent data, monitor signal decay | 58% decay |
| Regime shift | Stress-test and refresh scenario set | CVaR fragility |
| Governance | Documented view interface, effective challenge | 2026 model-risk guidance |
| Baseline failure | Fall back to 1/N if OOS edge absent | DeMiguel; Bock |

The contrarian caution: a backtest showing the CVaR layer reducing drawdown may capture a known factor premium rather than genuine integration benefit, so **factor-attribution analysis must accompany every performance claim.** The Bayesian parametric-policy result suggests the hybrid's advantage over 1/N will concentrate in drawdown periods rather than spread evenly.

**Chapter verdict: a general-purpose hybrid is feasible not because any one family is complete, but because the families' failure modes are non-overlapping—Black-Litterman shrinkage bounds the forecast noise overfitting generates, and the CVaR objective prices the tail the equilibrium covariance ignores. The view channel makes the composition governable rather than merely performant.** What remains unresolved is empirical: no single source measures the assembled three-layer stack's out-of-sample performance net of costs against 1/N over a full regime cycle.

## 16. Limitations and Future Research

**The strongest claims—the superiority of tail-aware risk measures, the fragility of deep learning under regime shift, and the top-tier placement of the Hybrid—are regime-conditional and sample-conditional, not universal laws.**

**Evidence quality.** A single out-of-sample Sharpe, net of identical frictions, for all seven families on one dataset does not exist. The only identical-constraint benchmark covers just the three classical methods; the learning families are evaluated in separate studies with their own conventions. The base supports confident mechanism-level claims and directionally confident rankings, but not a precise, single-metric head-to-head.

**Scope.** Bounded to liquid, price-return, allocation-horizon settings—silent on credit (which demands default-correlation modeling), high-frequency (where order-book dynamics govern), and execution problems. Governance evidence is predominantly U.S.-focused (SR 11-7, April 2026 revisions), not systematically covering the EU AI Act or Asian frameworks.

**Time validity.** Every superiority claim is conditioned on regime and calendar window. Regime dependence bites hardest on deep learning: overfitting under regime change and over-stationarization removing the ability to capture bursty events mean a model's in-sample risk estimate understates live tail exposure. The chain: regime shift → training-distribution mismatch → accuracy loss → backtested risk understates live tail → ranking advantage erodes.

**Sampling.** The base over-represents equity data and mainstream, reproducible RL agents (DQN/DDPG/PPO/SAC). The 1/N debate is well-balanced (including both attack and defense); the Return Prediction verdict carries the strongest equity tilt and should be discounted by multi-asset readers.

### Falsifiers

The top-tier placement of the Hybrid is a defeasible claim with five named exit conditions:

| Rubric criterion | Falsifying observation | Test setting |
|---|---|---|
| Allocation Robustness | 1/N beats Hybrid net-of-cost OOS | Small universe (Yuan & Zhou) |
| Risk Fidelity | CVaR portfolio realizes deeper drawdown than variance | Stressed regime, shared inputs |
| Return Reliability | DL forecaster error matches linear baseline post-shift | OOS, post-shift window |
| Allocation Robustness | Hybrid underperforms low-turnover classical after costs | Bid-ask + turnover charged |
| Interpretability | BL view step no more auditable than NN under SR 11-7 | Supervisory "effective challenge" |

**With 80% of managers expecting AI to fuel revenue growth and tech-as-a-service potentially adding 12% by 2028, the central research risk is that the field will scale deployment faster than it scales falsification.** The single most valuable next investment is a **shared, identical-input, net-of-cost testbed spanning all seven families across multiple regimes**—which would settle most of the five falsifiers within a single research cycle.

## 17. When to Use What: Conclusion

**The right model is the one whose failure mode is least likely to be triggered by the conditions you actually face.** The families partition cleanly into a transparent-but-weak-forecasting camp (MVO, Black-Litterman, CVaR) and a strong-forecasting-but-opaque camp (DL, DRL, Decision-Focused). **No standalone family occupies both**, so selection reduces to which liability your regime punishes least.

**Selection map:**

- **Mean-Variance** — best fit to stable, liquid, approximately-Gaussian regimes with small universes. By 2028, increasingly a prior-generating core inside Bayesian pipelines rather than a standalone allocator.
- **Black-Litterman** — when you hold defensible, structured views and want them blended conservatively with market consensus. Passes Allocation Robustness where raw MVO fails.
- **CVaR/Robust** — when normality is indefensible and tail losses govern: crises, fat-tailed credit and emerging markets, options-heavy books. Clearest pass on Risk Measurement Fidelity.
- **Learning families (DL, DRL, Decision-Focused)** — data-rich, high-dimensional regimes with stable feature-return relationships. Strong forecasting, but **all sit on the black-box opacity end**—their shared liability.

**Classical families pass Interpretability & Governance and struggle on Return Reliability; the learning families invert that ranking exactly—which is the structural reason to combine them.**

**Feasibility ruling on the hybrid.** Several components already have direct empirical support: Black-Litterman optimization on GARCH–EVT–Copula and LSTM models, multi-LLM sentiment through LSTM into Black-Litterman views, and transformer-DRL combined with Black-Litterman. The architecture chains cleanly—machine learning extracts nonlinear views → Black-Litterman shrinkage stabilizes them into auditable posteriors → a CVaR optimizer allocates on a coherent tail measure. **The framework is feasible in component form today. Through 2028, the binding constraint is governance, not capability: with 80% of managers expecting AI to fuel revenue, adoption outpaces auditability—and the view-channel hybrid is the design that keeps the two in step.**
