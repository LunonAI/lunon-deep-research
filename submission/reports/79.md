# Bodies in Transition: Transgender Themes in Middle Eastern and North African Cinema

Transgender themes in MENA cinema form a small but theoretically dense corpus that rewards reading through trans theory and film theory together rather than either alone. Across roughly two dozen feature, documentary, and short films—from the 1954 Egyptian comedy *Miss Hanafi* to the 2022 Moroccan drama *The Blue Caftan*—fewer than ten place a transgender life at narrative center, and only three sustain a full-length analysis worthy of close reading: *Facing Mirrors* (Iran, 2011), *Be Like Others* (Iran, 2008), and *The Blue Caftan*.

**The dominant framing—that Iran's state-funded sex-reassignment surgery (SRS) constitutes a "paradise" of Islamic gender accommodation—obscures a coercive medical-legal regime that channels gender nonconformity into surgical erasure.** That framing, advanced by festival rhetoric and echoed in the reception of Tanaz Eshaghian's documentary, is sharpened against Western media by Musa Shadeedi's 2025 *My Kali* critique. My verdict: *The Blue Caftan* leads as the most ethically and formally accomplished trans-adjacent MENA film; *Facing Mirrors* ranks second as the foundational narrative breakthrough; *Be Like Others* ranks third—indispensable as document, compromised as ethics.

## 1. The Corpus and Its Framework

A research-grade reading of MENA trans cinema requires an explicit contract before any close reading: what counts as a qualifying film, what "transgender themes" means in contexts where the category is not native, and which theoretical instruments apply. This study assembles a twelve-film corpus spanning seven national contexts and nearly seven decades.

### Scope and Selection

The geographical scope is the Middle East and North Africa, treated as a contested geopolitical framing rather than a cultural essence. The corpus clusters in Iran, Egypt, Morocco, and Tunisia, with single entries reaching toward Jordan, the Gulf, and the Maghrebi diaspora in France. Linguistically it spans Persian, Egyptian and Maghrebi Arabic, and French-language diaspora production—a spread the field has only begun to treat comparatively, as the 2025 University of Pennsylvania call for papers makes clear.

**The load-bearing selection criterion is a working definition of "transgender themes."** A film qualifies when it centers an identity, embodiment, or transition that crosses the assigned-gender line as a lived condition—not merely a costume gag. This separates genuine trans-identity narratives such as *Facing Mirrors*, whose protagonist Eddie is a transgender man seeking to live as his true self, from the comedy-of-disguise tradition exemplified by *A Girl Named Mahmoud*. The boundary is deliberately porous at one edge: *Miss Hanafi*'s "accidental sex change" plot is included precisely because its involuntary embodiment forces the question of where comedy ends and trans theme begins.

**The corpus deliberately over-samples the Maghreb and Egypt to counterbalance the scholarly fixation on Iranian SRS cinema.** Where the Iranian strand is medicalized and state-framed, the Egyptian comedies and Maghrebi art films open a register of desire and disguise that no SRS-centric account can capture.

### The Paired Analytic

Every film is read through a trans-theory lens and a film-theory lens together, so that no film is read through either field alone. *Be Like Others*, for instance, must be read simultaneously through medicalization theory and documentary ethics, because its coercive force lies precisely where the observational gaze meets state SRS policy.

A second axis tracks authorship: cis-direction versus trans self-representation, and insider versus outsider positioning. *Facing Mirrors* is cis-directed by Negar Azarbayjani yet insider-national; *Be Like Others* is made by a diaspora-Iranian who is both cultural insider and Western-festival-facing outsider. Azarbayjani works inside Iran's censorship economy under the Ministry of Culture, while Eshaghian works for an international documentary market via ITVS funding—producing opposite representational risks: domestic constraint versus Orientalizing exposure.

**The corpus's defining tension is that every gain in international visibility carries a corresponding risk of Orientalist framing.** The apparent paradox—that the most celebrated trans MENA film, *Be Like Others*, is also the most ethically contested—resolves once authorship and reception are read together: its Sundance acclaim and its coercion critique are co-produced, because the same observational distance that won festival praise renders the clinic's pressure legible as critique.

### Key Terms

- **Transgender themes**: lived gender-crossing as identity or embodiment, distinct from **cross-dressing/passing** as temporary plot disguise.
- **Gender performativity** (Butler): gender as repeated stylized acts rather than inner essence.
- **Trans embodiment**: the felt, material experience of a transitioning body, central to Eliza Steinbock's aesthetics of change.
- **Exit scapes** (Wibke Straube): cinematic spaces of escape and utopian sensibility through which trans figures evade gender's policing.
- **The trans gaze**: a proposed counter-concept to Mulvey's cinematic gaze—a mode of looking that witnesses rather than objectifies the trans body.
- **State-sanctioned SRS**: surgery institutionalized under Iranian fatwa-state policy.
- **Localized/mercurial queer**: gender-variant coding rooted in local categories (*khawal*, *khanith*) that resist Western "gay" framing, after critiques of Joseph Massad's "Gay International."
- **Orientalist representation**: framing MENA gender variance as exotic or backward for Western consumption.
- **Situated trans film criticism**: a reading practice that resists both Western-centric framing and indigenous romanticization.

## 2. Defining Transgender Themes for a MENA Context

Applying "transgender themes" to MENA cinema requires distinguishing at least four phenomena that English-language criticism routinely conflates. **A film carries a transgender theme when it represents a persistent cross-gender identification or embodied transition—as distinct from a temporary disguise, a stylized stage persona, or a coded suggestion of same-sex desire.** As Roshanak Kheshti argued in 2009, the "transgender move" on screen can function as a site of agential potential even where no medicalized transition occurs.

### Three Working Categories

**Trans identity and embodied transition** anchor the strongest pole. *Be Like Others* follows young Iranians toward state-sanctioned SRS, where gender is institutional and surgical rather than theatrical, because Iran's fatwa-state apparatus converts religious sanction into a medical pathway. *Facing Mirrors* occupies the same pole through fiction. The distinction from cross-dressing is sharp: where a disguise plot resolves when the costume is removed, both films present identifications that medical and legal transition aim to make permanent—which is why both attracted scholarship on hegemonic masculinity rather than on theatrical play.

**Cross-dressing and disguise** form the oldest and most populous MENA screen tradition. *Miss Hanafi* (1954) and *A Girl Named Mahmoud* (1975) belong here: the gender crossing is plot machinery, resolved within the narrative. The interpretive danger is reading these as proto-trans texts—a category error that flattens the historically specific *khawal* tradition of male cross-dressing performance into a modern identity label it never carried. Deborah Starr's study of Togo Mizrahi's Alexandria films shows 1930s–40s Egyptian cinema deploying gender-bending within a comic register rather than an identitarian one. Yet Kheshti's argument complicates this: even reversible cross-dressing can open agential potential. The contradiction resolves once we separate diegetic function (the plot restores order) from spectatorial effect (the audience has seen the binary made arbitrary).

**Gender-variant coding** sits between the poles: screen gestures that suggest non-normative gender or desire without naming a trans identity. This is the register of Maghrebi art cinema—*Le fil*, *Tlamess*, *Razzia*—and the diasporic *Tarik El Hob*. The crucial move is to keep transgender themes analytically central rather than dissolving these texts into a generic LGBTQ reading, because much gender-variant coding in MENA cinema concerns same-sex desire, not cross-gender identification.

### Why Western Categories Cannot Transplant Whole

These distinctions are useless if "transgender" is treated as a stable, exportable identity. Afsaneh Najmabadi's *Professing Selves* demonstrates that Iranian trans subjectivity is co-constituted with Shi'i jurisprudence and state medicine, producing a category that is neither Western "transgender" nor a timeless indigenous essence. The methodological commitment of this study is therefore **situated trans film criticism**: reading each film through the legal, religious, and production context that gives its gender-crossing meaning. This is why the study preserves indigenous terms—*khanith* for the Omani/Gulf category, *khawal* for the Egyptian performer—without glossing them as "transgender."

## 3. MENA as Structured Heterogeneity

"MENA cinema" names not a unity but a structured heterogeneity along three axes.

**Geography.** The single most consequential fault line is the division between Persianate Iran and the Arabophone world. Iran is not an Arab nation; its tradition, language, and—decisively—its legal treatment of gender transition diverge sharply from its neighbors'. **The state-sanctioned SRS regime structuring both Iranian films is a specifically Iranian phenomenon with no Arab equivalent**, rooted in post-revolutionary fatwa-state jurisprudence. This divide predicts which trans-theory lens each film invites: medicalization dominates the Iranian cluster, exit scapes the Maghrebi works. Iran produces the region's densest concentration of trans cinema precisely because Khomeini's 1980s fatwa created a juridical category of the state-recognized transsexual, producing a documented population and a cinema able to narrate transition without triggering censorship on grounds of homosexuality.

**Language.** The region's indigenous gender categories resist translation. The Egyptian *khawal* and the Omani/Gulf *khanith* denote socially embedded roles that predate and diverge from the medicalized transsexual of Iranian law. French functions as the second language of Maghrebi cinema; *Tarik El Hob* and *Le fil* route their queer/trans coding through a French-language address that presupposes European festival circulation. This linguistic routing is inseparable from funding: French co-production money shapes which MENA trans stories reach international screens.

**National traditions.** Iran's "New Iranian Cinema" provides a realist humanism legible abroad and survivable at home, its trans narratives presupposing a state that recognizes transition. Egypt's commercial studio cinema generated cross-dressing comedies in which disguise is a narrative engine, not an identity claim. Morocco and Tunisia sustain festival-oriented art cinemas reliant on European co-production; the Gulf represents a nascent, tightly controlled frontier; diaspora production operates outside any single national apparatus. **Across these modes, production scale and state control vary inversely with narrative explicitness.**

## 4. Trans Theory: Performativity, Embodiment, Passing

Three load-bearing concepts test unevenly against the corpus, and that uneven fit is itself the finding.

### Performativity

Butler's proposition—that the gendered subject is produced through iterated acts—best explains the Egyptian comedies. *Miss Hanafi* turns on a man who undergoes an accidental sex change and must learn to perform womanhood, a premise traced to a real 1947 news item reversed for the screen. The comedy's engine is the visible gap between assigned category and learned repertoire; the film **literalizes Butler's thesis that gender is a doing rather than a being—but treats transition as misfortune to be reversed, not identity to be inhabited.** *A Girl Named Mahmoud* stages gender as strategic disguise, foregrounding gender's constructedness yet resolving by restoring the protagonist to heterosexual closure. The genre cannot let the citation become permanent without ceasing to be comedy.

### Embodiment

Steinbock's *Shimmering Images* theorizes trans cinema as an aesthetics of change, conveying embodied transformation through dissolves, close-ups, and textural shifts. This frame best explains the Iranian cases. In *Facing Mirrors*, embodiment registers not in surgical spectacle but in the everyday somatic labor of holding a masculine body legible under hostile scrutiny—performed by the cisgender actress Shayesteh Irani. The casting generates a genuine tension: no openly trans male actor was castable within the Iranian industry of 2011, so embodiment is achieved by a cis performer's craft rather than a trans body's presence.

*Be Like Others* films embodiment at the medical threshold, where the body's transformation is routed through clinic, fatwa, and operating theater. **The documentary exposes a distinctive MENA configuration: embodiment is not self-authored but state-mediated, and the surgical threshold functions as the price of recognition.** A traceable coercion follows: Iranian law insists cross-gender identification be resolved through SRS, producing a clinical pipeline that pressures subjects—including those whose distress concerns same-sex desire rather than gender—toward operations they might not otherwise choose.

### Passing

Passing best explains the experimental and diasporic films, where recognition is provisional. *Khastegi* treats passing not as seamless assimilation but as a destabilized, fractured visual condition, exemplifying the localized/mercurial queer. *Tarik El Hob* relocates passing to a transnational frame, where it becomes less a matter of surgical certification than of navigating between incommensurate regimes of recognition.

**Together these three establish that no single trans-theoretical concept suffices for the MENA corpus, because the films distribute themselves across distinct regimes of gendered legibility—comic, medical, and mercurial.**

| Concept (scholar) | Best-fit films | Key tension |
|---|---|---|
| Performativity (Butler) | *Miss Hanafi*, *A Girl Named Mahmoud* | Constructedness recontained by comic closure |
| Embodiment (Steinbock) | *Facing Mirrors*, *Be Like Others* | Cis-performed vs. state-medicalized body |
| Passing (Kheshti) | *Khastegi*, *Tarik El Hob* | Localized/mercurial queer vs. disclosure binary |

## 5. Visibility, Medicalization, and Self-Representation

**In the MENA corpus, visibility arrives almost never as pure recognition but as a double bind—exposure that simultaneously confers legibility and manufactures vulnerability—and this bind is tightened by the region's distinctive linkage of recognition to medicalization.** Najmabadi's *Professing Selves* supplies the baseline: recognition in Iran is routed through a juridical machinery that converts gender variance into a medical condition to be resolved.

*Be Like Others* is the corpus's most direct study. Religious sanction transforms a stigmatized identity into a treatable diagnosis, so that surgery becomes the only sanctioned route to social existence. **Several subjects voice that surgery is pursued less as the fulfillment of embodied desire than as an escape from the criminalization of homosexuality**—a substitution the film captures without resolving. The documentary is structurally tempted toward medical chronology, yet its most powerful moments refuse closure.

*Facing Mirrors* approaches the same regime from the opposite pole—narrative fiction rather than observational documentary—and **relocates the locus of recognition from the medical institution to the interpersonal**, making self-representation a narrative achievement rather than a clinical outcome through the gradual recognition between Eddie and the conservative driver Rana. That Eddie is played by a cisgender actress complicates the claim, but the emphasis on his own voice marks a departure from the medicalized gaze.

The Egyptian comedies prove that **medicalization is a historically specific condition of visibility, not a universal one**: what *Be Like Others* presents as the inescapable price of recognition is absent from the Egyptian frame, where gender change is comic spectacle. Mohamed Soueid's *Cinema Fouad* (1993), a portrait of the Syrian trans woman Khaled El Kurdi in Beirut, proves the double bind survives even where no clinic structures the frame: by filming El Kurdi within her own domestic and performance worlds, Soueid anchors the gaze in her self-staging.

**Self-representation in this corpus is measured not by surgical outcome but by the degree to which the trans subject authors her own appearance against the medical and observational gaze.**

## 6. Exit Scapes and Transfeminist Reading

Wibke Straube's *Trans Cinema and its Exit Scapes* supplies the affirmative counter-concept the corpus needs: the **exit scape**, a momentary opening in a film's sensory texture through which a trans character escapes the regulatory grid of binary gender. Straube grounds it in three figures—the sonic rupture, the aqueous dissolve, and the liminal doorway—each an affective event at which the apparatus stops policing the body and shimmers. The release is utopian in the Muñozian sense: not a blueprint but a felt rehearsal. Crucially, it relocates trans agency from narrative resolution, where the medical-legal apparatus captures it, to the level of sensation.

**For the MENA corpus, the productive move is to ask not whether a film grants its trans character a legal resolution but whether it grants them an exit scape.**

In *Facing Mirrors*, the road-film structure literalizes the figure: the car is a mobile liminal chamber where neither Rana's domestic role nor Eddie's natal family can enforce their gender scripts. The film's politics reside less in its SRS plotline than in these ungoverned intervals of passing. Where *Be Like Others* confines its subjects to the clinic waiting room—an architecture of enclosure—*Facing Mirrors* grants Eddie the open road.

The Maghrebi art films reward Straube's sensory registers most fully. *Tlamess* stages the exit scape at its most mythic: a central body dissolves into forest, water, and uterine darkness, suspending gender legibility altogether through Slim's near-wordless mise-en-scène. *The Blue Caftan* works the threshold through tactile enclosure—the hush of the tailoring workshop, the slow passage of the needle, the caress of silk. **The exit scape is not a single aesthetic but a spectrum, running from dissolution (Slim) to enclosure (Touzani).** Both films are cis-authored, risking the aestheticization of an interiority their directors do not share; Straube's resolution is that the exit scape lives in the spectator's sensory experience, so a trans spectator may claim the opening regardless of authorship.

The frame has limits. The Egyptian comedies resist Straube's utopian reading: their generic contract is restoration, not release. The disguise comedy wants the reassuring snap-back to a stable sex—the opposite of an exit scape. Documentary poses a different limit: *Be Like Others* sits uneasily with the exit scape because its observational form is committed to the real, though we can locate the scape in the spectatorial act of reading *against* the coercive frame.

## 7. The Gaze, Spectatorship, and the Trans Gaze

Mulvey's cinematic gaze cannot simply be transplanted onto MENA trans films, because the trans gaze and the politics of Orientalist representation jointly reconfigure who looks, who is seen, and what recognition costs.

**Whereas Mulvey's female object is eroticized, the MENA trans body is more often pathologized or comically exposed.** In *Be Like Others*, the documentary camera frames pre- and post-operative bodies in clinical close-up, rendering the trans woman an object of medical and international curiosity. In the Egyptian comedies, the look solicits laughter rather than desire. Mulvey's scopic economy persists while its affective charge shifts from arousal to laughter or diagnosis, because censorship forecloses overt eroticism.

Spectatorship after Mulvey fractured the monolithic spectator into positioned, resistant viewers—indispensable for a corpus whose films mean one thing at a Tehran screening and another at a Berlin festival. Applied to *Be Like Others*, the Western festival viewer's "sympathetic" distance can itself be a mode of Orientalist representation, consuming Iranian trans suffering as spectacle. Ji Yeon Lee's study of "self-Orientalised" films shows how filmmakers internalize Western critical labelling, producing images that confirm festival audiences' prior frames. Films foregrounding state-sanctioned SRS become legible to juries precisely as confirmations of an expected narrative about Islamic repression—a trap *The Blue Caftan* resists by refusing the explanatory, ethnographic mode.

**The trans gaze** names an emergent mode that renders transition itself as aesthetic process rather than fixing the gender-variant body as object. Steinbock locates a distinctly trans aesthetic in the cinematic "shimmer"—the dissolve, the moment of perceptual flux—materializing transition as process. A caution is warranted: aestheticizing transition can itself appropriate trans experience for an art-world gaze. The trans gaze is genuinely oppositional only when anchored in trans self-representation, not merely formal experiment by cisgender auteurs.

**Across the corpus, gaze regime correlates less with the filmmaker's identity than with the circuit of circulation and the genre contract.** Commercial Egyptian comedy and international documentary both objectify, by different means; experimental and art-cinema registers open the oppositional and trans gaze. This shifts the ethics-of-representation debate away from authorial identity and toward the institutional conditions—funding, festival programming, censorship—that determine how looking is structured.

## 8. Realism, Documentary Ethics, and Melodrama

Three aesthetic regimes govern how MENA films render the trans body as credible, and **each buys its truth-claim with a specific ethical cost.**

**Documentary ethics.** *Be Like Others* is the clearest test. Its observational mode produces its authority—the spectator believes they watch unmediated reality—and its danger. **The observational camera cannot register coercion without appearing to endorse it.** When the film shows young women pushed toward surgery as the only legal route out of criminalized desire, its refusal to editorialize leaves the coercive structure legible only to the already-informed viewer. Neutrality itself becomes an argument, yet the film cannot control whether its truth-claim is read as documentation of Iranian trans agency or as Orientalist spectacle of Islamic coercion. The SRS documentary belongs to a broader genre of medicalized **transnormativity**, granting trans legitimacy only through the narrative of diagnosis, surgery, and before/after resolution—a convention *Be Like Others* both inhabits and, through its attention to coercion, strains against.

**Realism.** *Facing Mirrors* imports the realist grammar of New Iranian Cinema—location shooting, naturalistic performance, the road as spine—to render trans embodiment as ordinary fact rather than spectacle. **Realist restraint is its central strategy.** Yet realism simultaneously renders visible the legal machinery (documents, the SRS certificate) conditioning Eddie's existence, so the ordinariness is shadowed by the double bind. Realism's deniability—nothing is spectacularized—offers the safest passage through state film control, making it the most likely vehicle for future MENA trans features seeking domestic release.

**Melodrama.** *The Blue Caftan* channels gendered and same-sex desire through melodramatic mise-en-scène: the tactile labor of caftan-making becomes the medium of an intimacy the narrative cannot name. **Melodrama resolves a censorship problem by displacement: desire forbidden in dialogue migrates into texture, gesture, and craft.** Melodramatic excess makes coded desire *more* legible than declaration would. Yet the form grants legibility only at the price of mortality—the queer/trans intimacy arrives inseparable from the wife's death. The Egyptian comedies occupy melodrama's comic twin, where the disguise plot is recuperated into restored cisgender heterosexual order by the final reel.

**Observational documentary clusters around the Iranian SRS narratives because the fatwa-state supplies a ready-made arc, while melodrama clusters around the Maghrebi features because coding is the only route to screen illicit desire under tighter censorship.**

## 9. National Cinema, Authorship, and Queer Cinema

National cinema, authorship, and queer cinema each operate as a **claiming apparatus**, and the contest over who claims the MENA trans film is where representational politics are decided.

**National framing.** The "Iranian cinema" brand functions as a capture mechanism that converts a trans person's story into a statement about the Islamic Republic. The Maghrebi art film is constitutively transnational: French co-production money enables screen representations of gendered desire that domestic distribution could not sustain. **The most tender MENA screen representations of gender-variant desire are financed by the former colonial metropole**—colonial-capital dependency buys expressive latitude at the cost of audience address: the films speak most fluently to Paris, not Casablanca. The diaspora film escapes both the Iranian capture effect and the Maghrebi festival address, because the diaspora filmmaker answers to no Ministry of Culture—making it the most likely site for the first fully trans-authored MENA feature.

**Authorship.** Every major film in this corpus is directed by a cisgender filmmaker. **Cis-direction is the structuring condition of the entire corpus**, traceable to a causal pathway: state censorship suppresses the emergence of openly trans filmmakers, producing a directorial pool that is overwhelmingly cisgender, which produces a corpus in which trans subjects are filmed from outside. Kheshti complicates simple condemnation: agency can be located in performance and the spectatorial encounter, not solely in the director's chair. Still, the structural absence of trans self-representation means the corpus cannot yet furnish a MENA instance of Steinbock's trans aesthetic, which presumes the image-maker shares the embodied knowledge depicted.

**Queer cinema** functions primarily as a festival and distribution category. *Facing Mirrors* becomes "queer cinema" not through any property of the text but through placement in LGBTQ festival sidebars, which retroactively recodes a state-SRS narrative as queer resistance. Massad's "Gay International" critique poses the sharpest challenge: the same festival category that grants visibility imposes a Western identity grid that may misname the experience it claims to recognize. This resolves not by abandoning the queer frame but by holding it against the localized/mercurial queer.

## 10. The Combined Analytic

Trans theory and film theory become analytically productive only when each trans concept is bound to a filmic technique specific enough to be tested against a shot. Five concept pairs name scene-level operations.

| Concept Pair | Scene-Level Question | Anchor Film | Primary Friction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Trans gaze × spectatorship | Who controls recognition? | *Facing Mirrors* | Relayed look vs. fixed spectacle |
| Embodiment × realism | How does technique produce the body? | *The Blue Caftan* | Craft stillness vs. shimmer |
| Exit scapes × national cinema | What does "escape" mean? | *Facing Mirrors* | State-as-refuge vs. state-as-threat |
| Medicalization × doc. ethics | Is choice or coercion recorded? | *Be Like Others* | Recognition vs. exposure |
| Passing × disguise | Will the reveal come? | *Miss Hanafi* | Durable condition vs. reversible gag |

In *Facing Mirrors*, the "trans gaze" is a narrative achievement, not a camera property: spectatorial identification is routed through Rana's gradual un-knowing of her own assumptions. In *The Blue Caftan*, realism is an embodiment-producing technique—the camera's patience with material process converts physical labor into a register of suppressed desire. The exit scape in an Iranian national-cinema frame cannot be purely utopian, because the state that licenses SRS is the same Eddie seeks to flee; the road film's perpetual motion keeps the tension provisional. The medicalization × documentary-ethics pair reveals the central knot: by observing subjects at the moment medicalization is presented as the only route to livable life, the observational camera risks recording coercion as if it were choice. Passing and disguise structure spectatorship in opposite directions: disguise depends on the spectator's secure knowledge that the "real" gender will be restored, whereas passing names a durable condition with no guaranteed reveal.

**The combined analytic concentrates where state medicalization and cinematic form collide—precisely the Iranian post-revolutionary scene**, which is why *Facing Mirrors* and *Be Like Others* carry the heaviest analytic load. Cis-direction conditions every pair: the trans gaze is always simulated rather than authored, a structural limit the analytic must flag rather than conceal.

## 11. Islam, Gender, and Religious Discourse

Religious discourse on gender variance in MENA is a differentiated spectrum, not a monolith, and each doctrinal position generates a distinct cinematic grammar.

**The Iranian Shi'a fatwa settlement.** Shi'a jurisprudence permits and institutionalizes SRS, traced to Khomeini's rulings and the activism of Maryam Khatoon Molkara. **Within Iran's fatwa-state framework, transsexuality is religiously legible precisely because it is medically "curable," whereas homosexuality remains a capital offense.** The theology generates the films' mise-en-scène: the fatwa legalizes SRS, producing the state-subsidized clinical infrastructure and the concentrated waiting-room population Eshaghian films. Against the celebrated "tolerance" reading, Kate Hashemi argues the regime coerces gay and lesbian Iranians toward surgery as the only licit resolution of their desire.

**Sunni jurisprudence and criminalization.** Across much of the Sunni-majority Maghreb and Mashriq, religious discourse folds gender variance into the prohibited category of *liwat*, producing criminalization rather than medical accommodation. This is why classic Egyptian cinema could treat an "accidental sex change" only as comedy: *Miss Hanafi* defuses the religious scandal through farce. **The religious unspeakability of durable gender variance in Sunni contexts is the condition of possibility for the cross-dressing comedy genre.** A genuine paradox follows: the region most criminalizing of gender variance produced its longest-running cross-dressing screen tradition—prohibition displaced the theme into licensed comic forms.

**Indigenous categories.** The *khanith* (Omani/Gulf) and *khawal* (Egyptian) are not "transgender" in the Western sense and must not be glossed as such. Where the Iranian SRS narrative routes identity through the clinic, the *khawal* and *khanith* route it through performance and kinship. Gulf cinema, where the *khanith* category is most alive, remains the corpus's largest blank—the religious discourse most resistant to the SRS model is also the least cinematically documented.

## 12. Legal, Colonial, and Postcolonial Framings

**The central paradox of the region's legal treatment is that Iran, the state most often caricatured as maximally repressive, operates the most elaborated legal pathway to medical transition**—a pathway whose generosity is inseparable from its coercive logic.

### The Three Legal Regimes

| Regime | Status of transition | Narrative device | Exemplary films |
|---|---|---|---|
| Iran (fatwa-state SRS) | Legal, subsidized, compulsory medicalization | Clinic as destination | *Be Like Others*, *Facing Mirrors* |
| Egypt (medical/ambiguous) | Surgically available, legally suspect | Accident and disguise | *Miss Hanafi*, *A Girl Named Mahmoud* |
| Maghreb/diaspora (criminalized) | No recognition; conduct criminalized | Exit scape; coding | *The Blue Caftan*, *Le fil*, *Tarik El Hob* |

In Iran, **legal recognition is routed through compulsory medicalization: to be recognized is to be surgically corrected.** The diagnosis and surgery precede and authorize the identity, inverting the Western self-determination sequence. Egypt occupies the revealing middle: SRS is medically available but read by authorities as a cover for same-sex desire—"the suspicion of homosexuality." A productive tension surfaces: Iran's repressive theocracy produces franker trans narrative cinema than Egypt's secular industry. The resolution lies in legal intelligibility: Iranian law gives screenwriters a nameable destination (the clinic, the fatwa), whereas Egyptian law's treatment of transition as latent homosexuality leaves only comedy and disguise.

In the Maghreb, Morocco's Article 489 and Tunisia's Article 230 criminalize same-sex conduct, which is why *The Blue Caftan* encodes desire through craft rather than declaration. **The recurrent structural solution is the exit scape—the elsewhere (France, the festival circuit) where the trans or queer subject can be realized because domestic law forecloses it.** *Tarik El Hob* literalizes this logic.

**Across all regimes, the legal treatment of transition determines not whether trans themes appear but in what narrative register they may be spoken**—medicalized directness in Iran, comic displacement in Egypt, spatial exit in the Maghreb.

### Censorship as Sorting Logic

MENA state film control does not blanket-ban transgender themes but **sorts them**, tolerating the SRS-narrative that resolves gender variance into legible heterosexuality while excising the desire that cannot be so resolved. Iran's Ershad (Ministry of Culture) exercises script-stage control favoring narratives pre-reconciled with fatwa-state policy: *Facing Mirrors* cleared domestic clearance precisely because Eddie seeks the one resolution the state endorses, while *Khastegi*'s non-resolving experimental imagery tests—and exceeds—the ceiling of tolerance. Egyptian comedy cleared censorship because the comedic frame renders cross-dressing temporary and corrigible. The international festival circuit functions as **counter-censorship** for banned films, at the price of recoding them for Western reception.

### The Colonial Reclassification

The colonial archive bequeathed MENA not a repressed sexuality but a reclassified one. Colonial modernity manufactured the grid that made existing roles—*khawal*, *khanith*—legible as deviance. Reading *Miss Hanafi*'s surgically altered protagonist through a modern "transgender" lens imports precisely the taxonomic violence the colonial record performed. The postcolonial state then nationalized the reclassified body: Najmabadi shows the Islamic Republic's embrace of SRS as inseparable from its post-1979 project of producing legible, heteronormative citizens. **The colonial grid reclassifies indigenous categories, the postcolonial state nationalizes the reclassified body, and the Orientalist festival circuit re-exports the result as spectacle**—a single apparatus operating at three moments.

## 13. Corpus Geography, Period, and Genre

The corpus falls into **two widely separated waves**: a classical Egyptian wave (1954–1975) and a contemporary wave (2008–2022), separated by a silence of more than three decades coinciding with the post-1979 reconfiguration of gender discourse. The classical wave is commercial comedy treating gender as reversible accident; the contemporary wave is festival art cinema and documentary treating gender as embodied identity.

**Genre functions as a censorship-management technology: the further a film moves from comedy's disavowing laughter toward art cinema's ambiguity, the more it depends on European co-production money and festival circulation.**

- **Comedy** (the Egyptian tradition) monetizes gender ambiguity by foreclosing it—disguise is always temporary, resolved into normative coupling. It remains the lowest-censorship-risk vehicle.
- **Documentary** is most exposed to observational ethics; *Be Like Others*'s access to the clinic grants evidentiary authority but positions the trans subject as medicalized specimen for an international gaze.
- **Melodrama** is the dominant feature-length mode, converting gender variance into familial suffering legible to both domestic and festival audiences—but legibility is purchased through European co-production money (Franco-Maghrebi for the art films, Anglo-American grants for documentary).
- **Art cinema** (*Tlamess*, *Khastegi*) deploys strategic ambiguity as both censorship shield and commercial liability, confining films to a festival-only existence.

**Across all twelve titles, directorial authority rests with cisgender or non-trans-identified filmmakers, making the trans gaze a theoretical aspiration rather than a realized production fact.** The festival and international-funder circuit is what makes MENA trans cinema viable, which simultaneously exposes it to the double bind: capital flows inward from Europe and North America, and interpretive authority flows outward to metropolitan audiences.

## 14. Case Study I: *Facing Mirrors* (Iran, 2011)

*Facing Mirrors* is the first Iranian narrative feature with a transgender protagonist. Directed and co-written by Negar Azarbayjani and produced by Fereshteh Taerpoor, it is an Iran–Germany co-production. Its narrative core is the asymmetric bond between Rana, a cisgender traditionalist driving a taxi to repay her imprisoned husband's debt, and Eddie, a transgender man fleeing an arranged marriage and a violent father to pursue SRS abroad. **The film matters less as a "first" than as the first Iranian fiction film to route the state's SRS apparatus through a protagonist's own desire rather than through the clinic's gaze.**

**The film's single most important structural decision is to withhold Eddie's transgender status until a mid-film revelation**, so that the spectator's trajectory mirrors Rana's, from suspicion to solidarity. The road structure organizes everything: the taxi is the film's primary exit scape, a mobile interior where Eddie's self-narration can be spoken before it can be seen.

### Form and Embodiment

The film constructs embodiment through the physical register of Shayesteh Irani's performance—gait, vocal pitch, guarded posture—rather than anatomical revelation. **The film withholds the revelatory "reveal" shot, refusing the diagnostic mastery Mulvey's gaze would predict.** The key scene in which Rana discovers Eddie's natal sex is staged not through visual exposure but through his transition documents, relocating the "truth" of gender from flesh to bureaucracy—a literalization of the fact that in Iran gender is juridically rather than visually adjudicated.

The cisgender casting produces a genuine tradeoff. The expected outcome of cis casting is a "safe," state-legible production; the actual resolution is messier, because Irani's androgyny imported real gender trouble back into a vehicle built to contain it. Cis casting buys institutional permission at the cost of representational authenticity—a tension *Facing Mirrors* pays only partially.

### Authorship and Reception

The film's structuring consciousness belongs to Rana, not Eddie: **the narrative routes the viewer's access to transness through a cis surrogate's dawning comprehension.** The structure is didactic by design—it teaches cis viewers to tolerate, but at the price of centering their learning curve. International reception folded the film into the prestige narrative of Iranian art cinema, so that Eddie's transness became a marker of national cinematic maturity rather than a claim by trans Iranians on their own representation. Against the "trans-authored or nothing" standard, the MENA evidence complicates the position: in contexts where SRS is state-channeled and same-sex desire criminalized, cis-insider authorship may be the only vehicle that clears censorship at all, and a purist standard would yield silence.

## 15. Case Study II: *Be Like Others* (Iran, 2008)

Tanaz Eshaghian's documentary delivers the corpus's most institutionally literal encounter with state-sanctioned SRS, filming inside a Tehran clinic where surgery is fatwa-authorized and free of charge. It opens in a waiting room "full of mostly young people, drawn there by their desperation." **The clinic is both the literal set and the governing metaphor**: by refusing to follow its subjects into post-transition life, the film renders SRS as the terminal horizon of gender-variant existence in the Islamic Republic.

The documentary's most analytically consequential claim is that **surgery is experienced by several subjects not as the fulfillment of identity but as the only available route out of an untenable position rooted in same-sex desire that has no legal existence.** Anoosh's plaintive "why can't I do so without surgery?" is the film's thesis in a sentence.

### The Observational Bind and Coercion

Eshaghian works largely in the observational register: long takes that let clerics, doctors, and parents speak without editorializing. **The observational style's apparent neutrality is itself the ethical problem: by declining to intervene, the camera appears to consent to the medicalizing logic it films.** Fidelity to the subjects' words becomes fidelity to the clinic's script, because the subjects have internalized the only state-legible vocabulary offered them.

The coercion operates as a causal chain: the state criminalizes homosexuality, which routes gender-nonconforming and same-sex-desiring people toward the diagnostic category of transsexuality, which makes SRS the legally sanctioned exit—so surgery becomes the price of survival rather than autonomous choice. **The camera does not assert this chain; it films subjects living inside it, which is why the documentary's ethics turn on whether observation of coercion amounts to complicity with it.**

A contrarian reading deserves naming: despite the common Western festival framing that *Be Like Others* exposes Iranian SRS as uniquely coercive, the IranNamag analysis argues the film simultaneously resists heteronormative nationalism and grants its subjects a genuine, if constrained, self-representation. **The coercion and the agency are not mutually exclusive; the film's achievement is refusing to resolve them.**

### The Gaze and Consent

The observational gaze positions the spectator as witness to a medical process, aligned with institutional authority even when sympathetic—what Evan Armstrong calls "the assisted eye." *Be Like Others* looks AT the body as medical object; *Facing Mirrors* looks WITH the body as narrative subject; *The Blue Caftan* looks ALONG the body's labor. **None of the three is trans-authored, so the "trans gaze" each achieves is an approximation produced by cis directors working in different genres.**

The film names subjects and circulates internationally, meaning its subjects' most intimate decisions were made permanently legible to a global audience the Iranian state's own discretion would never have granted them. This is the double bind operating at the level of method.

### Orientalism and Reception

*Be Like Others* arrived at Western festivals carrying a startling fact: that SRS is state-subsidized in the Islamic Republic. **That structural surprise—the "paradise of Islamic gender transition" frame—is precisely where Orientalist representation enters.** The reception protocol converted Eshaghian's subjects into evidence for a thesis (Iran forces gay men to transition) that the footage only partially supports. The mechanism is traceable: the SRS-as-novelty hook leads to a reading that treats subjects as victims of theocracy, producing a spectatorial position of secular-Western superiority.

The film's meaning is completed by its circulation context: **screened in Tehran it reads as recognition; screened in New York it reads as indictment.** The available sources document the film's festival reception thoroughly but cannot resolve how Iranian domestic audiences actually received it, so the asymmetry remains theorized rather than measured. **International reception functions as a co-author that can override directorial intent, and the degree of override tracks inversely with how obliquely a film codes gender variance.**

## 16. Case Study III: *The Blue Caftan* (Morocco, 2022)

*The Blue Caftan* relocates the question of gendered desire to a Moroccan artisanal milieu where no SRS plot, no medical gatekeeper, and no state transition protocol organizes the narrative. Halim, a master tailor in a Salé medina workshop, his terminally ill wife Mina, and the apprentice Youssef whose arrival activates Halim's long-suppressed desire—**unlike the SRS-narrative spine of *Be Like Others*, the film builds its drama entirely from concealment, craft labor, and the slow dying of a marriage that was always also a protective arrangement.**

Strictly, the film is a study of gender-variant coding and queer male desire rather than an explicitly transgender narrative—and naming that distinction is itself an act of situated trans film criticism, the refusal to collapse every MENA gender dissidence into a single "trans" bin. Its inclusion is theoretically productive: Halim occupies the most traditionally masculine artisanal authority position available, and the film stages masculinity's quiet internal dissidence within him, fraying at its seams.

### Mise-en-Scène and the Haptic Gaze

**The caftan is the film's governing element, and Touzani organizes her visual grammar around the tactile act of its making**—fingers drawing silk thread, the petrol-blue cloth, the slow pressure of the needle. **Desire is rendered visible not through the body but through the handling of material.** This haptic aesthetic resonates with Steinbock's account of trans cinema generating meaning through textures of change rather than the revealed genital "truth" cis-authored film demands.

Touzani withholds nondiegetic music across long stretches, privileging ambient workshop sounds. **The spectator is positioned not as a voyeur granted access to a secret but as a witness to restraint.** Touzani's aesthetic of silence protects her characters from the exposure that the documentary subjects of *Be Like Others* cannot escape, yet risks reinscribing the closet as the only available grammar. The resolution comes through displacement: by relocating the erotic into the shroud-making that closes the film—Halim sewing Mina's burial caftan—she **converts the unspeakable into a ritual of love that requires no verbal confession.**

### Class, Religion, and Festival Politics

Halim is an artisan of a dying trade; **his queerness and his craft are narratively fused: both are forms of devotion the surrounding economy has no vocabulary to value.** Where the Iranian state renders certain trans subjects legible through medico-legal SRS, the Moroccan state renders queer desire illegible by criminalization (Article 489), and Touzani's aesthetic of silence is the formal correlate. Mina, dying of cancer, is not an obstacle but a third term who comes to bless her husband's desire—the corpus's most generative counter-model to the wife-as-obstacle trope.

The film was a French–Moroccan–Belgian–Danish co-production, premiered in Un Certain Regard at Cannes, won the FIPRESCI prize, and reached the Oscar shortlist. **This circulation pattern is the structural condition under which queer-coded MENA cinema reaches international audiences**, raising the self-Orientalization question. But Touzani's restraint is read as authentic aesthetic choice and market-legible product at once, impossible to fully disentangle. Notably, the film screened commercially in Morocco despite Article 489: **the passing of the film through censorship mirrors, at the institutional level, the passing its characters perform at the narrative level.**

The three case studies together prove that the MENA trans-film corpus cannot be read through a single register of visibility: the clinic, the road, and the workshop each produce distinct relations among body, state, and desire. **Despite the common reading that *The Blue Caftan* belongs only tangentially to a trans-film corpus, its haptic refusal of the diagnostic gaze makes it a more rigorous application of trans cinema's formal insights than several films that foreground transition explicitly.**

## 17. The Egyptian Comedies and Iranian Experimentalism

### *Miss Hanafi* (1954): The Corpus's Zero-Degree

*Miss Hanafi* is built around a man who undergoes an accidental sex change, and it is the earliest commercially distributed MENA feature to place medicalized sex transition at the center of a popular narrative. Its most revealing authorial decision is its genesis: the screenplay reverses an actual 1947 woman-to-man news story into a man-to-woman scenario. **This directional reversal discloses the gendered asymmetry of comic legibility in 1950s Egyptian cinema**—a real female-to-male case would have narrated upward mobility into patriarchal privilege, which the era's comic grammar could not render as laughter; the reversal stages a perceived loss of masculine standing.

**In *Miss Hanafi*, bodily sex changes but gender identity does not.** This is the cleanest illustration in the corpus of why performativity must be uncoupled from anatomical essence: the comedy presumes that once the body is female, femininity must be performed, and humor flows from Hanafi's failure to perform it. The film belongs to a pre-identitarian comic tradition of bodily misfortune, the 1954 absence of the vocabulary of gender identity that Iranian fatwa-state policy would later institutionalize.

Three findings establish its structural role: it proves a sex-change narrative is not necessarily a transgender narrative; its cis-authored, punitive comic gaze establishes the extractive baseline that self-representational films later depart from; and its uncontroversial censorship passage demonstrates that genre, not subject matter, governs what MENA state apparatuses tolerate. **Without a 1954 case that so cleanly separates somatic change from identity, the distinction between cross-dressing comedy and genuine trans self-representation would be far harder to fix across the rest of the corpus.**

### *A Girl Named Mahmoud* (1975)

Niazi Mostafa's comedy turns on a woman's voluntary masquerade as a man. Where *Miss Hanafi* stages an involuntary surgical change, *A Girl Named Mahmoud* stages voluntary, strategic cross-dressing—the two bracket the genre's poles, the accidental body versus the deliberate costume. The comedy depends on the audience knowing the truth onscreen characters do not, positioning the spectator as privileged holder of the secret. **Unlike the *khawal* tradition, the masquerade carries no permanence; it exists to be undone by the final reel.** The film's thin scholarly record is itself a finding: it marks the zero point of the corpus, the commercial-comedy baseline from which every later claim to trans visibility departs.

### *Khastegi*: Experimental Opacity

*Khastegi* is the anomaly within the Iranian cluster, operating through experimental, fragmentary imagery that resists the clinical legibility its siblings presume. **Its fragmentation is a direct formal rejection of the medicalization model that renders trans life intelligible only through surgical resolution.** Where Eshaghian's camera positions the viewer as ethnographic witness, Motamedian's montage denies any stable diagnostic vantage; its fragmentary structure functions as a sustained exit scape. It scores "non-medicalized" on the theme axis where every other Iranian entity scores SRS-narrative, making it the corpus's clearest test of whether experimental form can substitute for trans authorship.

A contrarian caution: aesthetic illegibility and representational authority are distinct axes. Motamedian's opacity may protect his subjects from the diagnostic gaze while foreclosing the insider articulation trans-authored cinema would provide. **Opacity without trans authorship remains an incomplete substitute for self-representation.**

## 18. The Maghrebi Art Films and Diaspora Geographies

### *Tlamess* (Tunisia, 2019)

Slim's *Tlamess* is the corpus's most formally radical entry, encoding gender politics through myth, landscape, and the desiring body rather than narrative declaration. Its second movement follows a deserter and a pregnant woman who shed clothing, language, and gender markers until their bodies blur into an animal, mythic state. **The film denaturalizes the gendered body as a removable cultural skin rather than a biological given.** Where the Iranian SRS regime insists gender be resolved into a recognized category, Slim's forest dissolves the categories themselves—routing gender transformation through the cosmological rather than the medical. The entire forest half functions as one extended exit scape, **making the scape not an interlude but the destination.** A real tension follows: a film with no self-identified trans character risks appropriating the register of transition for an art-cinema abstraction floating free of lived trans experience; its value lies in showing gender's contingency rather than representing a trans subject.

### *Le fil* (Tunisia, 2009)

Ben Attia's *Le fil* encodes gender and sexuality through a Maghrebi idiom of localized/mercurial queer desire, following a closeted gay architect who returns from Paris to Tunis. **Its gender-coding operates through the same repertoire of maternal surveillance, bodily concealment, and diasporic return that structures the Maghreb's trans-themed cinema.** The governing metaphor—the invisible string tethering Malik to his mother—figures internalized regulation as compulsory citation; the mother's panoptic look makes concealment the visual subject. The body that must pass is not crossing a gender line but a line of respectability, and this structural homology between queer concealment and trans passing licenses reading the film within a trans-analytic frame. **The film proves that visibility and legibility are not the same achievement**: its LGBT-circuit placement secured recognition while locking it into a gay-identity reading that partly occludes its gender-coding.

### *Our Males and Females* (Jordan)

Ahmad Alyaseer's short radicalizes the double bind by locating its trans subject after death: its central question is how to prepare a transgender child's body for burial. **The subject achieves screen visibility only at the moment agency has become structurally impossible.** The short form thrives in a production ecology distinct from the feature system policed by Ministries of Culture, circulating below the threshold of ministerial attention. The same obscurity that denies recognition also denies the exposure that renders gender-variant subjects vulnerable—**marginality functions as protective cover rather than mere limitation.**

### Diaspora as Exit Scape: *Razzia* and *Tarik El Hob*

Diaspora functions as the privileged geography where gender nonconformity that cannot be narrated at home becomes thinkable. *Razzia* (2017) reads its gender-nonconforming youth through Todd Reeser's "mercurial queer"—localized, shifting sexualities staged without fixed identity labels. *Tarik El Hob* follows a French-Algerian student researching a North African male same-sex marriage ritual, routing queer becoming through ritual performance and diasporic return. Butler's performativity illuminates the wedding ritual: it does not express a pre-existing identity but enacts one through citational performance. Massad's "Gay International" critique supplies the necessary caution: a diaspora film importing European queer grammar back onto Maghrebi ritual risks the epistemic colonization it seeks to honor.

**Razzia holds gender dissidence inside the national frame while routing its finance abroad; *Tarik El Hob* relocates the subject physically and narrates return. Together they prove the gender-variant-coding films achieve through spatial displacement what the Iranian SRS-narrative films achieve through medical institution**—two distinct routes to narratable gender dissidence.

## 19. Underexplored Frontiers

The analytical map thins at three frontiers: the Gulf, Turkey, and trans-authored production. **The corpus contains not a single confirmed trans-authored fiction feature from the Arab world.**

The **Gulf** presents the sharpest paradox: indigenous gender-variant categories (the Omani *khanith*) are ethnographically well documented yet cinematically almost invisible. The Saudi *Hijra* project marks the corpus's most provisional inclusion, conditioned by the post-2018 reconstruction of a domestic film industry under the General Entertainment Authority—an institutional frame making explicit gender-variant content unlikely to clear review. **Gulf trans cinema exists presently as a category of anticipation, not of texts.** A contrarian reading: where Iran's medicalized regime produced a documentary because transition was visible, Gulf societies that absorb gender variance into kinship and labor roles generate no equivalent archival pressure.

**Turkey** sits at the definitional edge, with a queer/trans tradition older and denser than the Gulf's. Övünç Ege traces a continuous line from Bülent Ersoy—a trans woman singer whose 1980s ban and rehabilitation made her a national fixture—to contemporary art cinema. Turkish directors frequently refuse the medicalized before/after structure, building around performance spaces where a trans gaze becomes possible—**positioning Turkish cinema as the corpus's richest site for theorizing the trans gaze, precisely because it least depends on the SRS narrative.**

**My committed projection: between 2025 and 2030, a diaspora-based, trans-identified Maghrebi or Turkish director is more likely than any Gulf or domestic Iranian filmmaker to produce the corpus's first trans-authored fiction feature**—conditional on the continuity of French and Qatari funding chains.

**Representation density tracks not the presence of gender-variant life but the configuration of medicalization, censorship, and festival funding**: Iran's SRS regime produces texts, the Gulf's kinship absorption produces silence, and diaspora funding produces a recognition-grammar risk.

## 20. Production, Circulation, and Reception

**Nearly every internationally visible MENA trans-themed feature since 2008 has depended on European co-production money and festival validation rather than domestic commercial finance.** The financing architecture shifted decisively in the 2010s with regional funds—the Doha Film Institute, Sanad, AFAC—reconfiguring how auteur projects reach completion. *The Blue Caftan* exemplifies the template; Worldcrunch identifies 2022–2024 as the most prolific period for queer-themed Arab cinema since the medium's origins, tied to these funds.

Festival selection functions as the de facto distribution system, substituting for domestic theatrical release that censorship forecloses. Three nodes structure circulation: Sundance/HBO carried *Be Like Others* into Anglophone visibility; Cannes legitimated *The Blue Caftan*; and BFI Flare has become the premier venue for MENA trans shorts. **Festival circulation resolves the distribution problem domestic censorship creates, but at the cost of routing the films' primary audience outside the region they depict.** Censorship operates as an "enabling" system governing distribution title-by-title, so that "censored" is a distribution state rather than an absolute ban.

Reception splits along a fault line. **International reception filters the films through an Orientalist frame**—Najmabadi and Leila Hudson argue that treating transsexuality as a "window" or "metaphor" for the Iranian state reproduces the Orientalism it claims to critique. Against this, a body of situated trans film criticism reads for the trans gaze and exit scapes. The strongest recent criticism holds both scales at once: the film as trans text and as nationally situated object.

**European co-production, festival gatekeeping, and Orientalist reception form a single circuit that privileges the festival-legible trans narrative—the SRS documentary, the art-cinema melodrama—while the trans-authored short and diaspora-streamed work represent the emerging counter-circuit.**

## 21. Comparative Synthesis: Recurring Motifs

Laid side by side, the corpus yields seven recurring motifs cutting diagonally across nation, period, and genre. **Transition, family, and the visibility double bind recur in every national tradition, but their narrative resolution splits cleanly along the fault line of state-sanctioned SRS.**

| Entity | SRS | Family/Concealment | Visibility bind | Desire/Embodiment | Migration/Exit |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| *Facing Mirrors* | strong | strong | mod | mod | strong |
| *Be Like Others* | strong | mod | strong | weak | weak |
| *Khastegi* | mod | weak | mod | mod | weak |
| *Miss Hanafi* | plot-device | strong | weak | comedic | weak |
| *A Girl Named Mahmoud* | plot-device | strong | weak | comedic | weak |
| *The Blue Caftan* | weak | strong | mod | strong | weak |
| *Tlamess* | myth | mod | weak | strong | mod |
| *Le fil* | weak | strong | mod | strong | mod |
| *Tarik El Hob* | weak | strong | mod | strong | strong |

**The SRS plot.** In Iran, **the apparent paradox—that the one MENA state most often cast as repressive is also the only one that funds transition—resolves once SRS is read as a technology of heteronormative order rather than liberation.** Outside Iran, no corpus film can stage this plot: where SRS is neither funded nor legally framed, transition becomes coded, deferred, or mythologized.

**Family and concealment** is the only motif load-bearing in every era and nation. The Egyptian comedies resolve concealment through public revelation and marital restoration; *The Blue Caftan* through private acceptance and death; *Facing Mirrors* through flight. **The family, not the state, is the corpus's most consistent antagonist and refuge at once.**

**Visibility's double bind.** Against the common claim that greater representation straightforwardly advances trans recognition, the corpus suggests a contrarian reading: visibility achieved through cis-authored, festival-bound films can consolidate Orientalist frames even as it names trans lives. The structural fix is authorship, not more visibility.

**Migration and exit scapes** encode privilege: migration as exit is available only to those with the class and diasporic capital to move.

**MENA trans-themed cinema is not one tradition but three overlapping economies—the Iranian medicalized, the Egyptian comedic-restorative, and the Maghrebi-diasporic desiring—each resolving the same familial and visibility pressures differently.**

## 22. Cross-Cutting Tensions and Conclusion

Four structural tensions—visibility, agency, Orientalism, and authenticity—form **a single constraint system in which maximizing any one axis tends to cost another.**

| Tension axis | Sharpest case | What is maximized | What is sacrificed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Visibility/agency | *Be Like Others* | State-legible exposure | Self-determination |
| Posthumous visibility | *Our Males and Females* | Recognition of erasure | Living agency |
| Orientalism/legibility | *Be Like Others* reception | Festival circulation | Local truth |
| Authenticity/authorship | *Facing Mirrors* | Sympathetic narrative | Trans self-representation |
| Craft vs. confession | *The Blue Caftan* | Embodied intimacy | Explicit category |

State-secured visibility buys exposure at the price of agency; diasporic authorship aimed at correcting Orientalism risks self-Orientalizing for the festival circuit; authenticity defined as trans self-representation nearly empties a corpus whose strongest works are cis-directed. **The resolution is methodological rather than filmic: situated trans film criticism refuses to score each film on a single axis, reading instead for the tradeoffs each negotiates under its specific legal, religious, and economic conditions.**

### What the Films Force Theory to Revise

The corpus is theory-generating in its own right. Steinbock's "shimmering" aesthetic presumes a spectator free to dwell in ambiguity; the Egyptian comedies instead weaponize that ambiguity for laughter, locating gender variance in disguise rather than interiority. Mulvey's gaze presumes a scopic economy organized around woman-as-image; the trans gaze, tested against *The Blue Caftan*, relocates desire into the tactile register of craft labor.

A situated trans film criticism refuses two opposed universalisms: the Western-diagnostic model that reads every gender-variant body through transition-as-identity, and the nativist romance that treats indigenous categories as pristine. **Neither survives contact with the films.** The Iranian case shows that legibility and coercion are not opposites but co-productions. Importantly, **Orientalist representation is not avoided by choosing insider authorship alone**: *Be Like Others* and *Facing Mirrors*, both directed by Iranian women, raise the cis-direction question without resolving it through biography.

### Verdict

MENA trans-themed cinema reshapes the theory of gender and embodiment by demonstrating that the gender-variant body on screen is never a neutral datum but always a state-mediated, genre-mediated, gaze-mediated construction—and that the only adequate criticism names those mediations film by film, refusing the twin comforts of Western diagnosis and nativist romance. The field's open problem—how representation shifts when trans self-representation replaces cis-direction—cannot be settled by the current corpus, because trans-authored MENA feature filmmaking barely exists yet. If emergent Lebanese and Gulf trans documentary work shifts the corpus toward self-authorship through 2030, situated criticism will need a third axis: a production-studies account of who controls the means of cinematic self-representation.

**A situated trans MENA film criticism is therefore not a method already complete but a program now begun.**
