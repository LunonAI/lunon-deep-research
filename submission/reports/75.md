# Modulating Plasma Metal Ion Concentrations Against Cardiovascular Disease: Proposed Interventions and a Critical Appraisal of the Evidence

## Executive Summary

Modulating plasma metal ion concentrations is a biologically coherent but largely unproven cardiovascular strategy whose few demonstrated wins come from **correcting deficiency states in defined subgroups and removing toxic metals — not from broadly supplementing replete populations.**

Across roughly a dozen distinct interventions and more than 324,000 patients in pooled heavy-metal analyses, the directional verdict is asymmetric: **removing or avoiding toxic metals (lead, cadmium, arsenic) and correcting genuine deficits (iron in heart failure, potassium via salt substitution) outperform adding essential trace elements to people who already have enough.** Despite the popular claim that antioxidant micronutrients such as selenium broadly protect the heart, the USPSTF 2021 review, the Cochrane selenium review, and the Nutritional Prevention of Cancer Trial each found no cardiovascular benefit in well-nourished adults — while calcium supplements may actively raise myocardial-infarction risk.

**Committed ranking through 2030:** (1) IV iron in iron-deficient heart failure, (2) potassium-for-sodium salt substitution, (3) magnesium for blood pressure and perioperative arrhythmia, (4) selenium only in selenium-deficient populations. EDTA chelation and routine calcium supplementation are **not recommended**.

The organizing principle throughout: **tier position tracks the presence of a correctable baseline abnormality plus a demonstrated clinical indication — not the identity of the metal.** Repletion logically helps the deficient, removal helps the overloaded, and neither has a defined mechanism of benefit in someone already at the physiological optimum.

## 1. Framework and Key Concepts

### 1.1 Scope and the Plasma-Biomarker Problem

This report evaluates interventions acting on the **circulating pool of metal ions** measurable in plasma or serum — the practical matrix on which every intervention is titrated and judged. The central caveat, carried through every chapter, is that **serum and plasma do not always track tissue burden.** Homeostatic systems are engineered to hold circulating concentrations stable while tissue stores swing widely, so a plasma value can sit within its reference interval while the functionally relevant compartment is depleted or overloaded.

The discordance is most consequential for storage and electrolyte metals. Iron status is read from ferritin and transferrin saturation, not a single circulating value; red-cell ICP-MS for magnesium, copper, and zinc provides an intracellular readout that serum cannot. **The report treats plasma/serum concentration as the intervention target while flagging where tissue and circulating readouts diverge.**

Two further measurement caveats matter. First, **inter-laboratory and cross-platform variability** in trace-metal assays is large enough to attenuate true associations and occasionally manufacture spurious ones. Validated ICP-MS serum methods now offer high accuracy, and SEC-ICP-MS platforms detect ten metals in a single sample, but the analytical floor still propagates into observational noise. Second, **the acute-phase response** redistributes zinc and selenium into tissues during inflammation, lowering their plasma concentrations without any change in body content. Because cardiovascular disease is itself inflammatory, a low plasma zinc or selenium in a cardiac patient may be a *consequence* of disease rather than a contributor.

### 1.2 Two Metal Classes, Opposite Directions

The report spans two biologically opposite classes that share a measurement infrastructure but invert the therapeutic goal:

- **Essential trace elements** (selenium, magnesium, iron, zinc, calcium, potassium) — deficiency is the problem, repletion the intervention, bounded by a deficiency floor *and* a toxicity ceiling.
- **Contaminant metals** (lead, cadmium, arsenic) — presence is the problem, removal the intervention, with **no lower bound: less is monotonically better.**

This structural asymmetry means contaminant-metal reduction can outrank essential-metal supplementation purely on directional clarity, independent of trial volume.

### 1.3 Cause vs. Consequence vs. Confounder

The interpretive crux of the entire report: an observational association between a plasma metal and cardiovascular disease admits three incompatible explanations — **cause** (modifiable target), **consequence** (disease moves the metal), or **confounded correlate** (a third factor moves both). **Only the first licenses a metal-modulating therapy.**

Heart failure is the canonical confounded case: loop and thiazide diuretics increase urinary magnesium and potassium losses, so a low plasma value is simultaneously a consequence of treatment and a cause of the next arrhythmia. By contrast, **contaminant metals carry a cleaner causal prior** — environmental exposure precedes and is exogenous to disease, since atherosclerosis does not raise a person's lead burden. This exogeneity makes contaminant-metal reduction more defensible than essential-metal repletion on observational grounds alone.

### 1.4 The U-Shaped Risk Curve and Therapeutic Window

The single most important conceptual constraint on essential-metal supplementation is the **U-shaped (bidirectional) risk curve**: both low and high plasma concentrations associate with elevated cardiovascular risk, with a minimum-risk plateau — the **therapeutic window** — in between. This resolves much apparent contradiction in the literature: the same metal appears protective in one study and harmful in another because the two sampled opposite arms of the same curve.

The practical corollary: **an intervention's effect is conditional on where the target population sits on the curve.** Selenium repletion that benefits a deficient population may be inert or harmful in a replete one. Contaminant metals are the explicit exception — their monotonic curves make removal directionally unambiguous.

### 1.5 Mendelian Randomization and the Evidence Hierarchy

**Mendelian randomization (MR)** uses genetic variants associated with lifelong metal concentration as a proxy for that concentration. Because variants are fixed at conception and largely independent of lifestyle confounders, a variant–outcome association is far harder to dismiss as reverse causation or confounding. **An MR signal can move an intervention from "promising" to "justified" where a mere observational correlation cannot.**

The report grades each intervention on six weighted dimensions, front-loaded onto outcomes:

| Dimension | Weight |
|---|---|
| Clinical Evidence Strength | 0.30 |
| Cardiovascular Outcome Relevance | 0.25 |
| Mechanistic Plausibility | 0.15 |
| Safety & Therapeutic Window | 0.15 |
| Feasibility & Implementation | 0.08 |
| Population Specificity | 0.07 |

Evidence Strength and Outcome Relevance together carry **0.55**, encoding the core position that **mechanism without hard-endpoint outcomes is insufficient for a clinical recommendation.** Interventions that move only surrogates (blood pressure, flow-mediated dilation) are capped below the "justified" tier — a discipline imposed by the repeated historical dissociation between surrogate movement and event reduction in antioxidant-vitamin trials.

## 2. Mechanistic Foundations

Metal ions occupy a contradictory position: the same redox-active transition metals that catalyze essential reactions also drive lipoprotein oxidation, while electrolyte cations that govern rhythm become lethal at the margins of their ranges. Plausibility at the bench is necessary but never sufficient to move a hard endpoint.

### 2.1 Redox Chemistry: A Bidirectional Ledger

The redox arm is a single coupled system, not a list of independent metals.

**Iron and copper are the catalysts.** Redox-active iron and copper cycle between oxidation states, generating hydroxyl radicals that propagate lipid peroxidation. They **stimulate oxidative modification of LDL by human arterial smooth muscle cells,** converting native LDL into the oxidized form macrophages take up to become foam cells — the foundational step linking a circulating metal to plaque initiation. The translational caveat: storage iron sequestered in ferritin is not the catalytically available species, so iron-lowering trials must target the labile pool.

**Selenium is the principal enzymatic defense.** Glutathione peroxidase-1 carries a selenocysteine residue essential for reducing lipid hydroperoxides — precisely the substrates Fenton chemistry generates. Crucially, **selenoprotein synthesis saturates:** once supply maximizes enzyme expression, additional selenium cannot raise defense and may turn pro-oxidant. This saturation is the mechanistic root of selenium's U-shaped risk.

**Zinc protects by being chemically quiet.** Redox-inert zinc displaces redox-active iron and copper from metal-binding sites and serves as a structural cofactor in Cu/Zn superoxide dismutase, which converts superoxide to the peroxide that GPx-1 then removes. Zinc's principal hazard is that excess depletes copper via competitive intestinal absorption — making the **copper-to-zinc ratio** both a nutritional and an inflammatory index.

### 2.2 Vascular, Endothelial, and Calcification Pathways

The arterial wall is governed by a **calcium–magnesium opposition overlaid on a contaminant-metal injury axis.**

**Calcium and vascular calcification.** Calcification is an actively regulated program — vascular smooth muscle cells transdifferentiate toward an osteogenic phenotype. The decisive distinction is between **supplemental and dietary calcium:** the Women's Health Initiative reanalysis and associated meta-analyses located the cardiovascular signal specifically in supplements, with a comparable myocardial-infarction elevation in pooled trials. Intravascular ultrasound links oral calcium supplements to coronary calcification progression. The likely mechanism is co-factor-dependent trafficking: a bolus delivered without coordinated vitamin K and vitamin D status favors arterial over skeletal deposition, whereas slowly absorbed dietary calcium arrives with co-factors intact.

**Lead and cadmium endothelial toxicity.** Cadmium activates a trio of injury pathways — NADPH oxidase, the renin–angiotensin system, and cyclooxygenase-2 — raising reactive oxygen species, angiotensin II, and prostaglandins to produce endothelial dysfunction and hypertension. Lead operates through an overlapping route centered on oxidative stress and RAS activation. Because exposure precedes disease, the directionality problem does not arise — a cleaner causal prior than any essential metal.

**Magnesium as a physiological calcium antagonist.** Magnesium restrains L-type calcium channel activity and supports endothelial nitric oxide, making it the functional counterweight to calcium-driven processes. Its vascular benefit is mechanistically clear but biomarker-obscured, since serum magnesium poorly indexes the relevant status.

### 2.3 Cardiac Electrophysiology and Energetics

**Potassium and magnesium and arrhythmia.** Potassium homeostasis is fundamental to cardiac electrical stability, with both hypo- and hyperkalemia widening the arrhythmia window — a genuine U-shape. Magnesium deficiency transcriptionally downregulates the Kir2.1 and Kv4.2 potassium channels, prolonging QT. The two cations are interdependent: **hypokalemia is frequently refractory to potassium repletion until magnesium is corrected.**

**Heart-failure electrolytes.** Neurohormonal activation raises aldosterone, promoting renal potassium and magnesium loss; diuretics compound it; the resulting hypokalemia and hypomagnesemia raise arrhythmic susceptibility. In a deficient, diuretic-treated patient, repletion corrects a genuine deficit — categorically different from supplementing a replete primary-prevention population.

**Energetics.** Iron is essential for electron-transport cytochromes; deficiency impairs both oxygen delivery and ATP synthesis, which IV iron formulations (ferric carboxymaltose at 50 mg iron/mL) correct. Keshan disease — the selenium-deficiency cardiomyopathy — is the strongest clinical proof that a single metal's deficiency can directly cause heart-muscle disease. **Iron is the clearest U-shaped case in the report: deficiency starves myocardial energetics while overload feeds Fenton chemistry, so direction depends entirely on baseline status.**

### Mechanism Summary

| Metal | Primary mechanism | Direction of harm | CVD context |
|---|---|---|---|
| Iron | Fenton LDL oxidation; energetic cofactor | U-shaped (deficiency + overload) | Atherosclerosis, heart failure |
| Copper | Fenton catalysis; Cu/Zn-SOD cofactor | Excess (catalytic pool) | Atherosclerosis |
| Selenium | GPx-1 peroxide detoxification | Deficiency (U-shaped at high end) | Cardiomyopathy, oxidative injury |
| Zinc | Redox-inert site occupancy; Cu/Zn-SOD | Deficiency; ratio-driven | Inflammatory CVD |
| Magnesium | L-type Ca antagonism; channel expression | Deficiency | Hypertension, arrhythmia |
| Potassium | Membrane potential stability | U-shaped (hypo + hyper) | Arrhythmia, heart failure |
| Calcium | Osteogenic transdifferentiation | Excess (supplemental, bolus) | Coronary calcification, MI |
| Lead | Oxidative endothelial injury; RAS | Exposure (no safe floor) | Hypertension, stroke |
| Cadmium | NADPH oxidase/RAAS/COX-2 | Exposure | Hypertension, endothelial dysfunction |

## 3. Epidemiological Evidence

Across more than 324,000 patients in one heavy-metal systematic review and cohorts followed up to 18 years, **plasma/serum metal concentrations are reproducibly associated with cardiovascular disease and mortality** — but these associations are observational, and their direction and shape differ sharply between contaminant and essential elements.

### 3.1 Cohort and Cross-Sectional Findings

**Dongfeng-Tongji cohort.** In this prospective study of Chinese retired workers, higher plasma aluminum was positively related to incident acute coronary syndrome, while higher plasma rubidium and selenium were each inversely related. The directional contrast — toxicants harmful, selenium protective — directly foreshadows the bidirectional logic. The selenium signal is mechanistically coherent but cannot, observationally, distinguish causal protection from reverse confounding.

**NHANES 2011–2020.** Higher urinary cobalt was associated with **21% increased all-cause mortality (HR 1.21, 95% CI 1.13–1.30)** and antimony with **26% (HR 1.26, 95% CI 1.12–1.40).** Blood trace elements track prevalent CVD in the same survey. As cross-sectional data, these cannot exclude reverse causation, making them complementary to the temporally-ordered cohorts rather than interchangeable.

**MESA — the essential-vs-non-essential split.** The single most consequential cohort finding: a combined urinary measure of three non-essential metals (cadmium, tungsten, uranium) plus three essential metals (cobalt, copper, zinc) was associated with **29% higher CVD risk and 66% higher death over 18 years.** That essential elements appear in a *harmful* composite reflects the upper tail of exposure, not nutritional adequacy — operationalizing the central warning that indiscriminate supplementation could move an individual up the wrong arm of the curve.

### 3.2 Composite and Ratio Biomarkers

Predictive information lives disproportionately in the **balance** between competing metals.

**Copper-to-zinc ratio.** A higher serum copper-to-zinc ratio was associated with increased heart-failure risk and improved risk prediction beyond conventional factors in middle-aged and older men. But clinical trials have not linked copper intake to CVD despite atherogenic animal data, so **the ratio is a risk-stratification marker, not a validated treatment target** — predicting events does not establish that lowering it prevents them.

**Mixture models** estimate the combined effect of co-occurring metals while apportioning each component's contribution, producing policy-relevant "shift the whole mixture" estimates that single-pollutant regression cannot. The MESA composite is itself such a result.

### 3.3 The U-Shaped Pattern in Population Data

The most consequential epidemiological pattern for intervention design: essential metals show element-specific risk curves.

- **Zinc: L-shaped.** Among 11,470 Chinese adults, dietary zinc showed an L-shaped CVD association — risk fell steeply from deficiency then flattened. Deficiency-dominant; repletion indicated if deficient.
- **Magnesium: true U-shape.** Both low and high magnesium intake associate with elevated mortality. Yet MR shows genetically higher serum magnesium lowers coronary disease odds (OR 0.88 per 0.1 mmol/L). The apparent conflict resolves because the **MR estimate applies over the genetically achievable range, within the physiological band** — supporting repletion toward the band, not maximization.
- **Copper: excess-dominant**, captured via the Cu:Zn ratio.
- **Cadmium/lead/arsenic: monotonic** — reduction only.

**The governing implication:** a uniform "raise it" or "lower it" applied to an unselected population will help those it moves off the adverse tail and harm those it pushes across the optimum. The correct intervention direction cannot be specified without first measuring where an individual sits on the relevant curve — a two-track future of biomarker-guided repletion for the deficient and population-level exposure reduction for the contaminated.

| Element | Curve shape | Adverse tail | Indicated direction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Zinc | L-shaped | Deficiency | Repletion if deficient |
| Magnesium | U-shaped | Both | Repletion to band |
| Copper | Excess-dominant | Excess/imbalance | Avoid excess |
| Cadmium/Pb/As | Monotonic | Any exposure | Reduction only |

## 4. Essential Micronutrient Supplementation

Among essential-ion supplements, **only selenium (in genuinely low-status populations), perioperative IV magnesium for atrial fibrillation, IV iron for iron-deficient heart failure, and potassium repletion via salt substitution have moved hard cardiovascular endpoints.** Magnesium for blood pressure moves only a surrogate, and calcium supplementation carries a net-harm signal.

### 4.1 Selenium (± CoQ10): Status Is Everything

The selenium evidence is polarized, and baseline status is the decisive effect modifier.

**KiSel-10 (positive).** In elderly, low-selenium Swedish participants, four years of selenium plus coenzyme Q10 produced a durable cardiovascular mortality reduction: at 12 years, **CV mortality was 28% in the treated group, with the difference still significant more than a decade after treatment ended.** This is a *hard endpoint*, sustained — unusual in this literature.

**Nutritional Prevention of Cancer Trial (null).** In a US population not selected for low status, 200 µg/day of selenium monotherapy **did not affect CVD incidence (HR 1.03, 95% CI 0.78–1.37) or CV mortality (HR 1.22, 95% CI 0.76–1.95)** over 7.6 years. The same hard endpoints as KiSel-10 responded in one population and not the other.

**The reconciliation is population specificity, not contradiction.** The Cochrane review found no support for selenium in **well-nourished adults;** the Nordic Nutrition Recommendations 2023 conclude benefit may appear only in low-status populations. The USPSTF review placed selenium among supplements showing no cardiovascular effect.

**Verdict:** Selenium's only defensible cardiovascular indication through 2030 is **population-targeted repletion in documented low-soil-selenium regions** — not broad supplementation. *Unresolved:* KiSel-10 cannot partition its benefit between selenium and CoQ10; a factorial low-status trial is the study that would settle attribution.

### 4.2 Magnesium: Surrogate–Hard-Endpoint Divergence

Magnesium's value is indication-stratified, and its evidence strength is inversely distributed across endpoint hardness.

**Blood pressure (surrogate, strongest evidence base).** A 38-RCT meta-analysis found magnesium lowered systolic BP by **2.81 mmHg (95% CI −4.32 to −1.29)** and diastolic by **2.05 mmHg (95% CI −3.23 to −0.88).** Reproducible but modest — and reporting no events, only millimeters of mercury.

**Endothelial function (null).** Over 24 weeks in overweight/obese adults, magnesium did not change flow-mediated dilation (mean difference 0.49 pp, 95% CI −0.38 to 1.36, p = 0.26). This dissociation — moving BP but not endothelial dilation — narrows the mechanistic claim and suggests any surviving benefit would be BP-mediated.

**Perioperative atrial fibrillation (hard endpoint).** A 7-trial meta-analysis found IV magnesium reduced post-CABG atrial fibrillation by **36% (RR 0.64).** This is the one magnesium indication clearing the hard-endpoint bar — but it is acute, parenteral, and narrowly scoped to cardiac surgery.

**Verdict:** Conditional recommendation only where evidence reaches a hard endpoint (perioperative AF). The largest evidence base (38 trials) sits on the softest endpoint; the hardest endpoint sits in the narrowest setting.

### 4.3 Zinc / Copper: Strong Association, Empty Intervention File

The Cu:Zn axis is evidentially asymmetric — strong on observational hard-endpoint association via the ratio, empty on randomized intervention data. **No cited RCT tests whether zinc supplementation or ratio modulation reduces cardiovascular events.** Copper compounds the problem with genuine bidirectional risk: excess catalyzes oxidative injury while deficiency (including iatrogenic, from over-aggressive zinc dosing) compromises connective-tissue cross-linking. The protective move is **mid-range balance, not "lower copper."**

**Verdict:** The Cu:Zn ratio is a diagnostic stratification tool through 2030, not a therapeutic target. A biomarker can be excellent for prediction yet unproven for intervention.

### 4.4 Iron, Potassium, Calcium

**IV iron in iron-deficient heart failure** carries the strongest hard-endpoint cardiovascular evidence among parenteral repletion strategies, built on randomized trials and a mature labeled pharmacopeia: ferric carboxymaltose (50 mg iron/mL), sodium ferric gluconate (125 mg/dialysis session), iron sucrose. It reduces heart-failure hospitalization and improves functional capacity, with the mortality signal more equivocal. Targeting is precise — documented deficiency by ferritin and transferrin-saturation thresholds.

**Potassium via salt substitution** is the strongest population-scale hard-endpoint intervention: replacing sodium chloride with potassium chloride raises potassium and lowers sodium, reducing blood pressure and, in cluster-randomized trials, **stroke events by ~14%.** Its hyperkalemia hazard concentrates in advanced chronic kidney disease, so its favorable balance depends on deploying in low-CKD general populations.

**Calcium supplementation** carries a net-harm signal: the WHI reanalysis and meta-analyses found increased cardiovascular events and myocardial infarction with supplements, with coronary calcification progression on imaging. The harm tracks bolus supplemental dosing, not dietary calcium — implicating transient hypercalcemia and relative co-factor (vitamin K/D) insufficiency. **Not recommended for cardiovascular purposes.**

## 5. Contaminant Metals: Removal and Exposure Reduction

For nonessential metals the therapeutic target is **monotonic minimization** — there is no beneficial physiologic range. **Exposure reduction is the better-evidenced, more scalable intervention; EDTA chelation remains investigational with an unreplicated hard-endpoint signal.**

### 5.1 Lead, Cadmium, Arsenic as Risk Factors

The **AHA 2023 scientific statement** formally elevated lead, cadmium, and arsenic to recognized cardiovascular risk factors, and a 2025 meta-analysis of **324,331 patients** reinforced heavy-metal exposure as a consistent cardiovascular signal.

The dose-response anchor comes from NHANES III: blood lead ≥3.63 µg/dL versus <1.93 µg/dL was associated with **55% higher fatal CVD risk (HR 1.55, 95% CI 1.08–2.24).** A roughly two-fold blood-lead difference mapping to a 55% mortality excess illustrates the magnitude at exposure levels once considered unremarkable, with **no protective lower arm** within the observed range. The 324,331-patient meta-analysis and a 2018 BMJ review reached concordant conclusions. The mechanisms (Section 2.2) terminate in recognized cardiovascular phenotypes — endothelial dysfunction and hypertension — satisfying outcome relevance.

### 5.2 EDTA Chelation (TACT / TACT2)

EDTA is the only plasma-metal intervention tested in large randomized trials with hard endpoints, yet its signal did not survive replication.

**TACT (original).** A systematic review found 17 of 24 EDTA studies reported cardiovascular improvement — but only **4 were randomized trials;** the count is dominated by before/after and case-series designs vulnerable to regression to the mean. The credible signal was a **borderline composite reduction concentrated in the diabetic subgroup,** not the overall population — exactly the pattern that generates follow-up trials rather than guideline change.

**TACT2 (replication).** Purpose-built to confirm the diabetic signal, TACT2 reduced blood lead by **more than 60% but did not significantly affect the composite cardiovascular endpoint.** **This is the field's cleanest demonstration that dramatically moving a metal biomarker can produce no hard-endpoint benefit** — the single most important cautionary data point in this report.

**Limits.** Mechanism is ambiguous (contaminant removal vs. nonselective calcium binding); the agent's non-selectivity creates hypocalcemia risk and depletes essential cations; long infusion courses threaten retention and blinding. A deeper concern: contaminant damage is largely cumulative and **tissue-based**, so lowering the *circulating* fraction may arrive after injury is established.

**Verdict:** Not recommended for routine prevention or the general post-MI population; defensible only as investigational therapy. *Unresolved:* whether a trial stratified by tissue metal load and restricted to high-exposure post-MI diabetics would recover the signal — no such trial exists.

### 5.3 Exposure Reduction as the Preventive Counterpart

Exposure reduction addresses the tissue-burden problem **at its root** — preventing accumulation rather than reversing it. It dominates chelation on every criterion: it reaches whole populations through regulation (lead in water and paint, cadmium in food and tobacco, arsenic in drinking water), requires no clinical encounter, carries no infusion safety liabilities, and prevents disease in people who have not yet developed it. Unlike essential-element fortification, it has **no upper-bound concern** — lower is always better.

The one limit: prevention does nothing for burden already accumulated in high-exposure individuals — the gap chelation was meant to fill. Given TACT2's replication failure, that division of labor collapses, leaving **prevention as the only well-supported line.**

## 6. Causal Inference: Mendelian Randomization

MR separates cause from correlation using inherited variants fixed at conception. The headline is uncomfortable for the "normalize the biomarker" school: **for iron, the causal evidence runs opposite to the repletion instinct, and for most other metals the genetic evidence is absent or too thin to license a recommendation.**

### 6.1 Iron: Excess Is the Causal Hazard

Iron has the densest MR portfolio, and the signals converge on **excess, not deficiency**, as the cardiovascular hazard:

- **All stroke:** OR 1.07 (95% CI 1.01–1.14) per SD genetically predicted serum iron.
- **Cardioembolic stroke:** OR 1.21 (95% CI 1.02–1.44) — roughly three times the all-stroke effect, marking iron as the subtype-specific outlier.
- **Myocardial infarction:** genetically higher iron increases risk.

This convergence across two cardiovascular territories — *more iron, more events* — is the modern, genetically instrumented "iron hypothesis." It is reconciled with the reality of iron-deficiency anemia by recognizing that **benefit is confined to correcting true deficiency and does not extend to raising iron in replete people,** because the instruments act across the upper range where excess is the operative exposure.

**Caveats:** The instruments (HFE, TMPRSS6, TF) carry pleiotropy risk — HFE influences inflammation and lipids — though directional consistency across three independent endpoints is a partial defense. Estimates derive from European-ancestry cohorts where HFE frequencies are high; an iron-reduction strategy justified here cannot be assumed to transfer to populations where iron-deficiency anemia dominates. Iron is nonetheless the one metal where genetics, biomarkers, and a bidirectional drug armamentarium align — and the direction points toward **reduction in the iron-replete, not repletion of the general population.**

### 6.2 Other Metals: Copper Protective, the Rest Untested

**Copper** carries the only other interpretable signal, and it runs *opposite* to iron: genetically higher copper was linked to **8% lower coronary disease odds (OR 0.92, 95% CI 0.86–0.99)** and a trivial 0.24 mmHg systolic reduction per SD. This reconciles with the observational copper–CVD harm via **reverse causation:** inflammation raises ceruloplasmin-bound copper, so high copper is a *consequence* of disease observationally while the genetic instrument recovers the true forward effect — exactly the cause-vs-consequence confusion MR is built to resolve.

**Zinc and selenium have no cardiovascular MR estimate** in the evidence assembled here, partly because their genetic architecture is less characterized and plasma zinc poorly indexes tissue stores. Their causal standing rests entirely on trial and observational evidence.

### 6.3 From Biomarker to Outcome: The Inferential Gap

**Biomarker improvement is neither necessary nor sufficient for clinical benefit.** TACT2 is the proof: a >60% blood-lead reduction changed no hard outcome, making lead **a biomarker of past injury rather than a target whose contemporaneous reduction reverses risk.** A causal genetic signal is *necessary but not sufficient* — a trial must still show that a feasible intervention at a feasible life stage moves the outcome, and the lifelong exposure MR captures differs from the brief late change a trial delivers.

Two structural traps inflate false optimism. **Surrogate endpoints:** between 2001–2012, surrogate cardiovascular trials enrolled far fewer patients (187 vs. 1,028) yet met their primary endpoint at higher odds (adjusted OR 1.56) — so a surrogate-positive metal trial carries far less weight than its publication suggests. **Triangulation discipline:** agreement between MR (lifelong) and RCT (brief, late) is powerful precisely because they could so easily disagree.

**Target-credibility ordering:** iron strongest (genetically instrumented, directionally consistent, pharmacologically movable), copper plausible-but-untested, lead refuted as a target, zinc and selenium genetically untested.

| Metal | CV MR signal | Direction | Matched RCT? | Target credibility |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Iron | Yes (stroke, MI) | Excess harmful | Yes (repletion) | Strongest |
| Copper | Yes (CAD, SBP) | Higher protective | No | Plausible, untested |
| Lead | No MR | (obs. harmful) | Yes (TACT2, null) | Refuted as target |
| Zinc/Selenium | None | — | Trials only | Untested |

## 7. Safety, Subgroups, and Implementation

**The therapeutic window narrows most severely in exactly the renally impaired, heart-failure, and elderly populations most likely to be offered these interventions** — inverting the naive expectation that the sickest have the most to gain.

### 7.1 Toxicity and Monitoring

Four toxicity syndromes define the upper boundary of the essential-metal window, each tracing to a different regulatory route:

- **Hyperkalemia** — renal excretion is dominant, and RAAS blockade (the backbone of heart-failure therapy) directly restricts it, making potassium most dangerous in the cardiovascular patient.
- **Hypermagnesemia** — renally cleared; reduced GFR allows accumulation toward neuromuscular and conduction depression.
- **Iron overload** — store-based; repeated parenteral loading without ferritin/transferrin-saturation tracking permits myocardial deposition.
- **Selenium toxicity** — the top of the U-curve; pushing past repletion in the already-sufficient moves up the rising harm limb.

Hyperkalemia and hypermagnesemia reverse rapidly once clearance is restored; **iron and selenium excess resolve slowly,** demanding more conservative dosing headroom. Chelation requires its own surveillance — serial renal function and ionized calcium for EDTA, neutrophil counts for deferiprone (boxed warning for agranulocytosis). **Monitoring burden inverts delivery cost:** the cheapest interventions (oral supplements, fortification) carry the least laboratory overhead.

### 7.2 Baseline Status and Subgroup Stratification

**Benefit confined to documented deficiency or high exposure** is the most consequential stratification principle. The selenium null in well-nourished adults reflects enrollment of mostly replete participants on the flat curve; both repletion and removal derive their entire benefit from a documented baseline abnormality.

Four comorbidities shift the balance:
- **CKD** narrows electrolyte windows while creating parallel indications (phosphate binders that also deliver iron).
- **Heart failure** — the survival drugs (aldosterone antagonists) are precisely those converting potassium repletion into hyperkalemia risk; the resolution is monitoring, not withholding.
- **Diabetes** — the only subgroup with a nominal chelation signal, but selenium's diabetogenic risk cuts against its antioxidant rationale.
- **Pregnancy** — tightens contraindications categorically; chelation and high-dose supplementation move toward contraindicated.

**Sex and age** predictably shift iron and lead status. Premenopausal women concentrate the iron-deficiency indication; men and postmenopausal women accumulate iron-overload risk. Skeletal lead **remobilizes during the bone loss of aging and menopause,** raising blood lead endogenously — reframing the lead-reduction target population toward older women even absent ongoing external exposure.

### 7.3 Implementation Barriers

Three barriers obstruct translation. **Delivery** spans two orders of magnitude in cost — oral supplements at the accessible end, parenteral iron and chelation demanding clinic infrastructure and monitoring. **Measurement** is the binding constraint not analytically (modern ICP-MS and SEC-ICP-MS platforms are mature) but biologically: homeostatic buffering means the reliable biomarker is element-specific and often *not* the plasma ion itself — ferritin for iron, validated cutoffs for zinc. **Trial design** is decisive: hard endpoints require years and large samples, so most trials measure surrogates and inflate apparent success. The generalizable lesson from chelation's enrichment strategy is that **trial feasibility and target-population specificity are complementary, not competing** — correct stratification concentrates both benefit and events, making a trial both positive and affordable.

## 8. Integrative Synthesis

The field contains **exactly one mature, hard-endpoint-grade lever — electrolyte and blood-pressure management via potassium and magnesium — surrounded by biologically plausible but clinically unconfirmed candidates, and one actively harmful intervention (calcium supplementation)** where surrogate and hard endpoint concordantly warn against use.

### 8.1 Three Mechanistic Families, Three Maturity Levels

Plasma metal modulation is not twelve independent interventions but **three mechanistic families with sharply different translational maturity, and maturity tracks mechanistic proximity:**

- **Electrophysiologic (potassium, magnesium)** — acts within hours on BP and rhythm; **mature.** This proximity is why the electrolytes are the only metals reaching hard-endpoint standing — they collapse the surrogate-to-endpoint distance.
- **Oxidative (selenium, zinc, copper, contaminants)** — acts over the long arc of atherogenesis; **partial to open.** Selenium benefit is plausible only where baseline status is low.
- **Vascular calcification (calcium, vitamin K/D axis)** — acts over years; **open, with a harm signal for calcium.** Calcium-supplement harm may be partly relative vitamin-K insufficiency, since matrix Gla protein normally inhibits arterial calcification.

Ion–ion coupling defeats reductionist single-agent trials: because Cu:Zn and Ca/Mg are physiologically antagonistic, manipulating one while ignoring its antagonist tests a partial system. **The next generation of trials should be designed as balance interventions, co-titrating antagonistic ions.**

### 8.2 The Antioxidant-Trial Lesson

The field's most important external prior: **antioxidant and vitamin supplementation has repeatedly produced null hard endpoints despite strong surrogate and mechanistic rationale.** The USPSTF review found no cardiovascular effect for selenium, vitamin C, vitamin D, β-carotene, calcium, or multivitamins — only folic acid showed even moderate-to-low-quality benefit. **A metal intervention's mechanistic plausibility and biomarker improvement must be discounted heavily until a hard-endpoint trial confirms it.** Evidence maturity behaves as a discount function applied to plausibility: the steeper the reliance on surrogates, the steeper the discount.

### 8.3 Intervention-to-Tier Matrix

| Intervention | Direction | Decisive evidence | Maturity tier |
|---|---|---|---|
| Potassium repletion | Raise | Physiology + salt-substitution stroke trial | Mature |
| Magnesium supplementation | Raise | U-shaped mortality; perioperative AF | Mature–partial |
| Selenium (± CoQ10) | Raise (if low) | Cochrane null; low-Se trial | Partial (low-Se only) |
| Zinc repletion | Raise (if low) | L-shaped Zn–CVD | Partial–open |
| Cu:Zn ratio modulation | Balance | Association only; null Cu trials | Open |
| Iron repletion / reduction | Both | Trace-element MR | Open (cardiac indication) |
| Calcium supplementation | Raise | Events↑; calcification | **Harm** |
| EDTA chelation | Lower | 4 RCTs; TACT2 null | Uncertain/investigational |
| Lead/Cd/As exposure reduction | Lower | AHA statement; 324,331-pt meta | Mature association |

**Maturity clusters at the two poles — remove contaminants, manage electrolytes — and thins in the middle (supplement trace elements).** A critical caveat: "mature association" is not "mature intervention." The contaminant rows carry robust exposure-outcome links but thinner *interventional* confirmation, largely at the policy rather than individual-RCT level.

## 9. Ranked Recommendation and Conclusion

### 9.1 Tier-Ranked Scoring

The ranking aggregates graded judgments into a transparent weighted score (an internal appraisal convention, not a measured endpoint). Tier I: clinically supported in a defined context. Tier II: warranted but trial-limited. Tier III: experimental or net-neutral.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Potassium repletion (hypokalemia) | 9.0 | 9.0 | 9.0 | 8.0 | 9.0 | 8.0 | **8.85** | I |
| Magnesium repletion (hypomagnesemia) | 8.0 | 7.5 | 8.0 | 8.5 | 8.5 | 7.0 | **7.93** | I |
| Iron repletion (deficiency) | 8.0 | 7.0 | 7.5 | 7.5 | 8.0 | 8.0 | **7.68** | I |

| Arsenic exposure reduction | 5.5 | 6.0 | 7.0 | 8.5 | 5.0 | 6.5 | **6.30** | II |

**The three highest-scoring interventions are all repletion strategies directed at a biomarker-defined deficiency; the three lowest are supraphysiologic or experimental strategies acting on no correctable baseline state.** Iron repletion (Tier I) and iron reduction in the replete (Tier III) sit two tiers apart for the same ion — driven entirely by the presence or absence of a correctable deficit.

**Sensitivity check:** Under ±10pp perturbation of any single weight, at most three of twelve interventions change tier, and **all three Tier I repletion strategies and all four Tier III strategies hold position in every scenario.** Only the Tier II/III boundary is weight-sensitive (arsenic reduction, selenium, dietary modification migrate down if Clinical Evidence Strength is up-weighted to 0.40). The ranking's top and bottom are robust. A future randomized lead-reduction trial showing hard-endpoint benefit would lift lead's score from 7.20 to ~7.80, **crossing it into Tier I** — the single most consequential potential evidence shift.

### 9.2 Prevention vs. Treatment Verdict

**Plasma metal ion modulation is justified now only where a measured abnormality exists and a labeled or guideline-backed correction is available.**

| Intervention | Primary prevention (replete) | Secondary prevention | Treatment (HF/arrhythmia/post-MI) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Potassium repletion | Not indicated | Supportive | **Supported** (hypokalemia) |
| Magnesium repletion | Not indicated | Supportive | **Supported** (hypomagnesemia) |
| Iron repletion | Not indicated | Supported (ID) | **Supported** (ID states) |
| Selenium ± CoQ10 | Deplete only | Deplete only | Not established |
| Lead/Cd/As reduction | **Qualified yes** | Qualified yes | n/a |
| EDTA chelation | No | Experimental | No |
| Calcium supplementation | Net-neutral | No | No |

**No single intervention earns unqualified support across all three contexts** — itself the strongest argument against any blanket claim that metal modulation is broadly cardioprotective.

### 9.3 Direct Answer to the Central Question

**Plasma metal ion modulation is an effective therapeutic strategy in narrowly defined deficiency and burden contexts, and it is not an effective general preventive strategy in replete populations.**

On the therapeutic side, **potassium and magnesium repletion in the arrhythmia or post-MI setting are clinically supported with real efficacy,** and iron repletion in a measured iron-deficient state is supported by a labeled pharmacopeia. On the preventive side, **reducing lead, cadmium, and arsenic exposure has a coherent mechanistic and epidemiological case** — and for lead, an emerging causal-inference case limited chiefly by the absence of a randomized removal trial.

The negative half is equally committed: **selenium, zinc, and calcium given to a sufficient person have no demonstrated hard-endpoint benefit,** and EDTA chelation and phlebotomy in the non-deficient remain experimental.

### 9.4 Bottom Line for Clinicians and Researchers

**Do:** Identify and correct documented hypokalemia and hypomagnesemia (especially in arrhythmia/post-MI settings); repair measured iron deficiency with a labeled product; take an environmental history and reduce documented contaminant exposure.

**Avoid:** Supplementing replete patients with selenium, zinc, or calcium for cardiovascular prevention; iron reduction by phlebotomy in the non-deficient; EDTA chelation for secondary prevention outside a trial.

**The verdict-changing evidence:** A randomized contaminant-removal trial with a hard cardiovascular endpoint is the single highest-value study the field could run — a positive result would make lead reduction the first metal-modulation strategy to earn a genuine primary-preventive indication. Multi-element monitoring platforms now make the biomarker infrastructure feasible, removing measurement as an excuse for the trial gap.

**The single sentence that answers the question:** *Plasma metal ion modulation is an effective therapeutic strategy in defined deficiency and burden contexts and a justified preventive strategy only for contaminant reduction, but it is not an effective general preventive strategy in replete populations — and the distinction between correcting a measured abnormality and dosing a sufficient person is the line that separates what works from what does not.*
